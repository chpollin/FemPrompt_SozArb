## ReflectAI:  Design  and  Evaluation  of  an  AI Coach  to  Support  Public  Servants' Self-Reflection

<!-- image -->

Hans-Henning Näscher ( B ) , Jan Schaffner , and Michael Koddebusch

University of Münster, Münster, Germany {hans.naescher,jan.schaffner}@uni-muenster.de, michael.koddebusch@ercis.uni-muenster.de

Abstract. Digital transformation in public administration requires the development of digital government competences (DGCs) among public servants. Self-reflection, a critical personality-related DGC, fosters adaptability and continuous learning but remains challenging to cultivate through traditional training. Employing a design science research approach, we developed ReflectAI, an LLM-based AI coach designed to support public servants in developing self-reflection competence. The system was evaluated through a two-week user study with seven public servants. Our findings revealed three key benefits: increased awareness of self-reflection opportunities, improved thought structure, and valuable conversation documentation. Implementation challenges included time constraints, technical infrastructure limitations, and organizational support. Our study contributes to research by demonstrating the applicability of AI coaching to personality-related DGC development, thus extending the body of knowledge of both competence development and AI coaching. With regard to practice, we highlight the importance of structured self-reflection processes and the required organizational conditions necessary for successful adoption. The resource-efficient approach to developing self-reflection competence facilitates this adoption through an innovative application of AI in public organizations.

Keywords: AI Coaching ≤← Digital Government ≤← Self-Reflection ≤← Design Science Research

## 1 Introduction

The digitalization of public organizations has long not been a means in itself but an essential necessity in living up to its value propositions, that is, the efficient provision of (digital) public services [ 33] and the generation of public value [ 38]. However, public organizations are lagging behind in digitally transforming, especially compared to their private sector counterparts [ 8]. For successful digital transformation, there is broad academic agreement on the significance of the public servants working inside these organizations [ 15, 28] and their digitalization competences [ 12] - henceforth referred to as digital government competences

(DGCs). Public servants lacking DGCs, in turn, are a barrier to digital government (DG) success [ 40]. Consequently, a substantial research stream has emerged that considers DGC education and development through, i.e., formalized education [ 19], MOOC-based learning [ 9, 22], or using simulation games [ 18].

These publications, however, focus on a rather restricted competence understanding, especially promoting skills and knowledge, for instance, in artificial intelligence (AI), business process management, or general DG knowledge. This narrow understanding neglects modern conceptions of competence , which not only involve skills and knowledge but also personality-related components [ 6]. In fact, these personality-related competence components are fundamental for DGC acquisition, given their importance in yielding the intent to develop knowledge and skills in the first place [ 6]. Chen and Chang (2010) thereby speak of a 'bottom up causal relationship' [ 6, p. 685] between a hidden layer (personality) and visible layer (abilities, i.e., knowledge and skills) of competence. This relevance of personality has also been confirmed for DG by Distel et al. (2019) [ 12].

One such personality-related DGC is self-reflection, which is defined as 'intraindividual processes [...] to capture inter-individual differences in behavior' [ 2, p. 514]. In other words, self-reflection allows public servants to assess their reasoning and behavior with respect to their interactions with others. Based on this systematic assessment, self-reflection has proven essential in adapting to challenges and continuously developing new competences to navigate the complexity of change processes [ 5] like the digital transformation; therefore, we consider self-reflection as a prerequisite for other DGCs [ 5], such as critical thinking, communication, continuous learning, or leadership.

Developing personality-related DGCs is tricky, because they 'cannot be acquired through formal education' [ 12, p. 291]. However, in line with other researchers, we argue that more informal and exchange-oriented approaches like coaching bear significant potential [ 7] for the development of personality-related DGCs. Such formats, however, are scarce in public organizations, which is likely attributable to a lack of awareness, time and financial resources, and a limited number of available coaches. To overcome these bottlenecks, recent developments in generative AI suggest AI coaching , which promises to enhance cost efficiency and flexibility of coaching practices [ 10].

Thus, our article aims to explore the potential of AI coaching for developing personality-related DGCs with a particular focus on self-reflection. We employ a design science research (DSR) approach to achieve the following goal:

Design and evaluation of ReflectAI, an AI coach to support public servants in developing self-reflection competence.

Our results expand the body of knowledge on overcoming digital transformation challenges in public organizations. More specifically, we contribute to the literature on DGCs, with a particular focus on personality-related DGCs and self-reflection. Additionally, we contribute to research on AI applications in the public sector, highlighting their potential for competence development.

The remainder of this article first presents the relevant research background and elaborates on the DSR process, before presenting our artifact and the evaluation results. Afterward, the results are discussed with respect to their contributions before concluding with final remarks.

## 2 Research  Background

## 2.1 Digital  Government  Competence  and  Self-Reflection

Despite its relevance, a unified definition for DGC is still absent. For instance, Distel et al. (2019) [ 12] define competences 'as knowledge, skills, and abilities', Halsbenning et al. (2021) [ 17] as 'abilities to cope with digitalization', and Koddebusch et al. (2024) [ 21] as a 'combination of [...] skills, knowledge, self-concept, and motives', whereby self-concept and motives refer to personality as per Chen and Chang (2010) [ 6], who have also described the competence concept as 'fuzzy'.

For the context of this article, we draw on a combination of the above. Our primary DGC source is Distel et al. (2019) [ 12], as their framework comprehensively incorporates personality-related competences, aligning with our theoretical understanding that personality is an essential dimension of competence. We amend their understanding of competence, however, with the one of Chen and Chang (2010) [ 6], who conceptualize personality as an inseparable component of competence. Our DGC understanding, therefore, comprises both the abilities (skills and knowledge) and the personality of public servants to act in a digitally transforming public sector.

Self-reflection is one of 18 personality-related DGCs Distel et al. (2019) [ 12] identified in their review. As outlined in the introduction, we understand selfreflection as a critical prerequisite to other DGCs, such as critical thinking, communication, continuous learning, or leadership. In digitalized environments, selfreflection fosters learning, adaptability, and a continuous evaluation of the own role and responsibilities [ 32, 34]. Therefore, individuals with high self-reflection proficiency are known to be key drivers of organizational renewal, as their ability to critically assess their own practices and adapt accordingly fosters innovation and continuous improvement within the organization [ 35].

Given this significance, scholars have proposed several models to guide selfreflection competence acquisition, typically in multi-step conceptualizations of competence development [e.g., 13, 23]. Among these, Korthagen's ALACT model [ 24] stands out as the most suitable option for our purposes. The model provides a five-step framework: Action, Looking Back, Awareness of Essential Aspects, Creating Alternative Methods, and Trial. Unlike more general cyclic models such as Kolb's learning cycle, the ALACT model emphasizes systematic learning from action. This focus on continuous self-reflection and improvement makes it particularly well-suited for fostering continuous professional development in practice-oriented settings such as public administration.

## 2.2 AI  Coaching

Coaching is defined as a 'dyadic helping relationship between a professional coach and client who work jointly along a systematic process of collaborative goal setting' [ 14, p. 107]. Such collaborative means of professional development have proven to be effective in developing rather personality-related competence components like the ones discussed above [ 7]. Coaching has long been a widespread tool for HR development [ 10], but as it occurs in face-to-face settings [ 10], it comes with resource constraints resulting in scalability issues.

Addressing these issues, technology-induced digital and AI coaches have emerged [ 10, 11], which employ digital technologies and AI components to substitute a human coach. Consequently, the coaching profession is expected to transform [ 14], although fully matured products and reliable insights are still scarce. Compared to human coaches, AI coaching promotes flexibility, cost-effectiveness, and availability [ 10, 14, 27]. Acknowledging challenges in AI coaching, such as limited trust-building and emotional management [ 10], scholars and practitioners are rapidly progressing in overcoming these. For instance, human coaching practices are increasingly integrated with chatbot design methodologies [ 37], accounting for theoretical foundations, relationship-building, and ethical compliance. A pertinently well-received framework was put forth by Terblanche (2020) [ 37] and will also guide the development of this article's artifact.

## 3 Method

Aiming to develop an innovative artifact, we employ the DSR methodology as per Peffers et al. (2007) [ 30]. The inherent Communication is absent here, given that the project's communication is depicted by this very article. Employing DSR supported our research objectives in two ways. First, it allowed us to gain both literature-based and practice-related insights on how self-reflection is perceived as a practice, enabling us to design our artifact accordingly. Second, it provided us with immediate feedback on the designed artifact, enabling us to collect participants' candid opinions. Figure 1 shows the research process.

Problem Identification and Motivation: Exchange-oriented professional development means, like coaching, bear the potential to enhance DGC development, especially with regard to personality-related competences and self-reflection. However, such approaches are resource-intensive and challenging to scale.

Define Objectives of a Solution: Design and evaluation an AI coach to support public servants in developing self-reflection competence. Concrete solution objectives are (1) positive perceived or expected helpfulness in self-reflection competence development and (2) positive perceived well-being while interacting with the AI coach in terms of usability and security aspects. The artifact development shall be guided by established self-reflection and AI coaching frameworks.

Design and Development: Guided by Terblanche's AI coaching framework [ 37], we reviewed the literature by means of a Scopus database search 1 . We identified design requirements (DRs) across multiple sources, eliminated duplicates, and adapted them to our specific context. This synthesis resulted in four DRs (see Sect. 4). Building on these DRs, we developed ReflectAI, integrating various AI models (e.g., OpenAI's 4o, 4o-mini, and Whisper) with system prompting to direct the system's responses. For iterative testing, we created LLM-generated personas representing diverse roles, challenges, and reflection needs. This humancentered approach allowed us to test ReflectAI across realistic self-reflection scenarios before proceeding to user evaluation.

Demonstration and Evaluation: The demonstration and evaluation of ReflectAI was conducted with seven public servants (federal and state level) from different administrative areas, such as digital transformation, urban development, and financial administration. The participants were 35 years old on average and volunteered to partake in a 30-minute introductory interview, a two-week test phase, and a 45-minute evaluation interview. We used a convenience sampling strategy for participant selection, approaching familiar public servants from previous projects. Although they were not involved in the current research, we believed they would be open to participating in this innovative endeavor. While we acknowledge the limitations of this approach, such as a pro-digitalization bias, we believe that the insights provided are valuable for future endeavors. During the test phase, the participants could autonomously interact with ReflectAI via a web application available both on desktop computers and mobile devices. The evaluation interviews provided valuable insights with regard to our solution objectives.

We employed an inductive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's [ 4] guidelines. This involved transcription and an initial categorization of results. Similar categories were grouped into themes to highlight the relationships between clusters. To summarize the themes, we identified the emerging key findings, each supported by illustrative quotes.

## 4 Artifact  Description

Through a comprehensive synthesis and analysis of existing literature, we developed four key Design Requirements (DRs) for ReflectAI. Our systematic review of current research in AI coaching, human-computer interaction, and ethical AI implementation allowed us to identify critical requirements, which we consolidated into these foundational DRs. These requirements form the theoretical and practical foundation for creating an effective AI coach that meets both technical and user-centric needs.

DR 1: The AI coach should be based on established theoretical models and recognized coaching techniques to provide users with sound and competent support [ 1, 37].

- DR 2: The AI coach should convey a sense of human interaction through the use of social cues, thereby enabling users to determine the degree of humanity themselves by selecting the preferred form of interaction and appearance according to their needs [ 16, 31, 37].

DR 3: The AI coach should proactively direct users through the self-reflection process utilizing multimodal communication channels, respond to their needs, and facilitate a clear, understandable, and flexible conversation to ensure a beneficial and adaptable user experience [ 16, 37, 39].

DR 4: The AI coach and the provider should adhere to data protection regulations, uphold ethical standards, and provide transparent information about the handling of sensitive data to ensure trust, security, and the responsible management of personal information [ 1, 3, 16, 37].

The following sections detail how we systematically implemented these design requirements in ReflectAI, creating a comprehensive system that combines theoretical foundations, user-centered design, and ethical considerations.

ReflectAI employs a modular architecture to ensure flexibility, scalability, and seamless integration of AI-driven coaching functionalities. The developed system is based on a React frontend for user interactions and a Flask backend architecture for AI-supported conversation control. The application is hosted via Amazon Web Service (AWS) EC2 and data storage via Relational Databases for user profiles and conversation histories and AWS S3 buckets for audio files. The core technologies are OpenAI's GPT-4o for response generation as well as AWS Polly for text-to-speech synthesis and OpenAI's Whisper for speechto-text transcription. OpenAI's GPT-4o-mini is used to guide conversations by identifying and correcting deviating conversation content [ 20].

Prior to the introductory interview, participants received comprehensive data protection information, which was further explained during the meeting to ensure complete understanding and informed consent about data handling practices.

The user interface (Fig. 2) follows a clean, minimalist design focused on coaching interactions and familiar chat patterns. Users can customize their experience by selecting the coach's name, voice, and appearance, influencing the perceived humanity level in interactions and potentially enhancing trust [ 31]. While personalization can strengthen user engagement, Krauter [ 25] highlights that excessive realism in AI design can lead to the Uncanny Valley effect, where near-human

features cause discomfort due to subtle inconsistencies, such as mismatched emotional expressions or unnatural behaviors [ 25]. To maintain an appropriate balance of human-like interaction, we restricted customization options in both scope and realism, balancing personalization with user comfort.

The system provides visual feedback through an animated typing indicator during message processing, enhancing credibility and reinforcing perceived intelligence [ 16]. The interface supports both text and voice messaging, with automatic transcription of voice inputs and synthesis of voice outputs, ensuring accessibility and accommodating diverse user preferences.

The conversation engine implements established coaching principles through a carefully engineered system prompt that incorporates the self-reflection model while maintaining natural dialogue. Through iterative development, the prompt defines ReflectAI's role as a self-reflection coach, establishing its core goals and communication guidelines. The objectives align with the principles of the ALACT model, which comprises five phases: Action, Looking back, Awareness, Creating alternative methods, and Trial. Each phase is seamlessly integrated into the conversation flow, with respective goals and sample questions. For example, phase 2, 'Looking back,' includes questions like, 'How did you perceive the situation?' and phase 4, 'Creating alternative methods,' includes questions such as, 'What could you do differently next time?' The communication framework incorporates warm, clear, professional language, empathetic formulations (e.g., 'I understand that...') as well as active listening techniques like rephrasing and regular comprehension checks.

Additional guidelines for conversation management include the use of open and closed questions, along with clarifying questions when needed. To ensure the effectiveness of these features, the prompt development process involved iterative testing and refinement. Evaluations with both human-crafted and LLMgenerated personas optimized the balance between professional coaching competence and natural interaction.

## 5 Evaluation

The evaluation of ReflectAI followed a three-phase approach: First, we conducted introductory interviews (n = 7) to assess participants' self-reflection practices. This was followed by a two-week test phase of the prototype, after which we conducted evaluation interviews.

Introductory  Interviews. Self-reflection practices varied widely in terms of frequency and structure. While most participants reported engaging in selfreflection daily or several times a week, these moments were rarely planned or systematic. Rather, self-reflection was primarily triggered by specific events such as meetings with superior officers, conflict situations, receiving feedback, or during commutes after work. The current practice of self-reflection is predominantly informal and unstructured. The most common form of self-reflection was mental self-talk, in which situations and decisions are processed mentally. In

It is noteworthy that none of the participants stated that they used special tools or structured methods for selfreflection. Several participants specifically mentioned the lack of a systematic approach in their self-reflective practice as a reason.

Participants' expectations of AI coaching focused on two main aspects. Several participants expressed their interest in receiving support in systematizing their self-reflection processes, with one participant specifically mentioning: 'What would help me the most is to better structure this whole self-reflection mechanism and process [...].' Similar to other studies such as [ 26], the participants had

certain expectations of ReflectAI's ability to process language due to chatbots such as ChatGPT. As one participant noted: 'My expectations of the chatbot are basically that the question-and-answer style is appropriate and that proper language is used. This is standard nowadays and should be no worse here than in other use cases.'

Evaluation Interviews. ReflectAI's evaluation through interviews revealed several important findings. It received consistently positive feedback for its intuitive interface and ease of use. These usability aspects underpin our second solution objective, which targets positive perceived well-being regarding usability and security. Participants emphasized how straightforward it was to initiate and maintain interactions, with one participant noting: 'My initial impression was that it was easy to use.' ReflectAI's linguistic capabilities exceeded expectations, particularly in its ability to engage in meaningful dialogue while providing structured instructions and neutral feedback. One participant commented: ' I'm really positively surprised at how quickly the tool responds. You get a response to everything you type in and I usually find it helpful.'

Participants particularly valued the specific and tailored suggestions provided by ReflectAI, which aligned well with their preliminary expectations. These tailored suggestions further reflect the first solution objective of helping participants develop self-reflection competence, as highlighted by one participant: 'I found it particularly helpful when specific suggestions were provided.' The practical relevance of recommendations was frequently highlighted as a strength, with one participant expressing surprise at the high degree of relevance of the customized advice: 'It was a positive surprise [...] that some concrete solutions were found in the end. All in all, it worked really well.' ReflectAI's ability to maintain neutral language throughout interactions was also appreciated: 'ReflectAI doesn't give its own assessment, but it stays really neutral on most topics, which I really appreciate. It never says whether you're right or wrong or whether it thinks the idea itself is a good one.'

In the context of human-AI interaction, participants reported a strong sense of trust in ReflectAI. This trust originated primarily from its non-judgmental nature, which made them feel more comfortable discussing personal or sensitive topics than they would with human counterparts: 'I sometimes discussed things with the tool in a way that I would perhaps not talk to people so directly now. That was because it's 'just' a tool that I could actually tell anything to. There is no person behind it who could take offense or be overloaded.' This sense of comfort supports our second solution objective by fostering positive perceived well-being in user interactions. Interestingly, participants also described instances in which the AI aspect of ReflectAI became less apparent, suggesting a seamless integration into the conversation flow: 'There was a moment when I wasn't actively thinking about the fact that I was writing with an AI.' One participant further emphasized this potential for enhanced realism through integration into existing communication systems, noting that it would 'almost immediately feel like you're talking to a real person' if incorporated into internal chat programs.

The participants reported three main qualitative benefits of using ReflectAI. These reflections clearly tie back to our first solution objective, which highlights ReflectAI's effectiveness in fostering a perceived self-reflection competence development. Participants revealed an increased awareness regarding opportunities for self-reflection. One participant shared the growing intentionality in his self-reflective practice: '[...] I would say that, in a broader sense, I am simply thinking more actively about the topic [ed. self-reflection]. [...] I don't know yet whether I'm doing it better now [...]. But I'm definitely doing it more consciously [...].' The structured prompts provided by ReflectAI were considered useful in organizing participants' thoughts. As one participant recounted, the process of composing messages fostered a more objective and structured perspective: 'When I wrote my own message, I initially thought: 'Well, the colleague is a bit to blame'. But as I was formulating it, I thought,'Okay, if you say it like that, it's not really like that'.' Finally, participants valued the ability to revisit prior conversations, allowing them to track their thought processes and reactions over time. One participant noted: 'Unlike human conversations, I can always go back to the conversation and think: What was the situation like again? What was the reaction?'

Despite all these positive perceptions, concerns about data protection emerged as a critical factor that would influence adoption rates. Several participants expressed worries about server locations, even despite the repeated security assurances: 'I did have reservations about its lack of local server [...], despite assurances regarding data protection.' Additionally, different shortcomings were noted in the interaction patterns. ReflectAI's empathetic responses were sometimes perceived as exaggerated, with some participants preferring more direct communication: 'What I noticed is that unlike other chatbots, the AI coach always adds extra sentences that somehow simulate humanity.' The practice of paraphrasing and asking for confirmation received mixed reactions. While some saw it as a way to encourage deeper engagement, others found it unnecessarily time-consuming, noting: ' When I gave a short answer, I still got this huge text.' Finally, the critical feedback also centered on the responses feeling rigid and predictable, with one participant quickly recognizing the repetitive nature of the ALACT model: 'You understand the structure and the response behavior relatively quickly. And that is of course very schematic.' Some participants noted the limitation in providing truly personalized responses, with one stating: "A system like that can't assess my character at all and can't give me an answer that is really tailored to me as a person."

During the two-week test phase, participants used ReflectAI to reflect on past situations, as one participant describes: 'It was always about situations that I had experienced myself, and I looked at how I reacted in the situation and what the coach suggested to me afterwards.' Some also relied on it to prepare for upcoming events: 'I mainly focused on follow-up meetings, but also on preparing for meetings, for example during discussions with head teachers or in the department.' The times of use varied greatly, as some integrated ReflectAI into their daily work routine, while others used it on their way to work or at home in

the evening. Many participants mentioned a lack of time during working hours as a frequent restriction that limited their use of ReflectAI, as this participant highlights: '[...] It's also often a challenge to find the right time slots for application.' Another participant explained: 'I used it once at work when I needed a mental break between tasks... Otherwise, I've used it more privately because I can't find time for it during working hours.'

The integration of ReflectAI within the daily work environment faced several challenges, primarily regarding time management, as previously indicated. Additionally, challenges arose concerning the technical infrastructure and a lack of organizational support. Participants struggled to fit self-reflection into their structured workdays, noting a lack of allotted time and suitable spaces. One explained: 'There is simply no provision [...] for explicit time for self-reflection or personal development in the daily routine.' Strict public administration IT guidelines further limited AI tool usage on official devices, leading many to rely on private devices or outside work hours. To address these barriers, participants proposed organizational support that explicitly permits and encourages reflective activities during work hours, alongside technical solutions such as reminder features and calendar integration. For example, one participant noted: 'If the chatbot sent me a message twice a week saying, 'Hey, we haven't reflected this week yet, would you like to reflect with me?', I would definitely do so.'

Participants expressed varying interest in regularly using ReflectAI. Many could envision consistent use if it offered long-term benefits, as one participant noted: "I would be really curious to see what it's like when you use the coach over a longer period... whether you eventually feel like you've 'learned everything' or if it continues to provide new impulses." The willingness to use it depended on three main conditions: seamless technical integration into work devices ( "If it were integrated on my work PC, absolutely. As it is now, I would use it regularly, but definitely less frequently. I don't know if I would manage to remember to do it at home in the long term.' ), explicit organizational support for dedicated reflection time ( 'It needs to be wanted that employees develop themselves. To really say: It's okay if you spend an hour a day or week on this âĂŞ be it for self-reflection or other topics." ), and an active reminder system to encourage regular engagement. Some participants felt ReflectAI could not replace face-toface conversations but saw potential in soft-skills development, such as receiving feedback on argument structure: 'That you say: 'Okay, I have an argument here, I'll write it down or speak it, and then the AI will give me feedback on it'.' Trust also emerged as an obstacle, given uncertainty about the coach's 'black box' nature: '[...] you don't know exactly where he [ed. ReflectAI] gets the knowledge from or which language model is being used. For me, there's still a bit of a lack of trust.'

Finally, participants suggested several improvements for ReflectAI, focusing on customization options like adjustable communication styles, response lengths, and self-reflection models. They recommended adding a reminder system to encourage regular usage. Many participants wanted ReflectAI to maintain context across sessions, though this raised privacy concerns that would need to be addressed through transparent data handling practices.

## 6 Discussion

This study investigated the potential of AI coaching to foster self-reflection competences in public administration.

The evaluation revealed two key mechanisms through which the AI coaching facilitated self-reflection. First, the predefined structure of ReflectAI actively encouraged the implementation of self-reflective processes. Our analysis showed that structured prompts helped participants move from reactive to proactive selfreflection Our findings also align with Park et al.'s [ 29] findings on expressive writing in mental health, suggesting that the chat-based format may inherently support more natural engagement in reflective practice compared to traditional journaling approaches. This is further supported by Terblanche et al. [ 36], who found that while voice interaction boosts initial engagement, text is preferred for reflective tasks requiring careful input. ReflectAI's usage patterns confirm this tendency, as participants favored text-based input over voice input, reinforcing the idea that structured writing fosters deeper and more deliberate self-reflection. Second, regular use of ReflectAI led to an increased perceived awareness of selfreflection and a willingness to consciously reflect, which is an essential precursor to behavior change. Thus, we suggest that long-term engagement with ReflectAI could lead to a tangible increase self-reflection competence that promotes deeper and more sustainable self-reflective practice.

While one participant would not use ReflectAI for self-reflection, she identified its potential for other competences. She emphasized the promotion of soft skills such as communication, argumentation, or negotiation, which aligns with existing insights [ 41]. The integration of AI coaching into HR practices offers the opportunity to promote individual competences more targeted.

The evaluation findings indicate that the solution objectives have been successfully addressed. ReflectAI effectively supported public servants in developing their perceived self-reflection competence (SO1), and was considered intuitively usable, non-judgmental, and thus offered a solution contributing to individual well-being (SO2). However, concerns about data security emerged as a moderating factor influencing long-term adoption.

## 6.1 Implications  for  Research

Our study advances theoretical understanding in three ways. First, we contribute to the theory of digital coaching by demonstrating how organizational context influences the effectiveness of AI coaching implementations, especially in highly regulated environments such as public administration. Our findings demonstrate that successful AI coaching integration requires addressing both technical and organizational dimensions, particularly in public administration. Second, it extends the application of AI coaching beyond the educational context to public administration and shows how the DAIC framework [ 37] can be used for self-reflection competence development in public administration. Third, the study shows how evidence-based theories like Korthagen's ALACT model [ 24] can be integrated into AI coaching to effectively guide users' self-reflection.

## 6.2 Implications  for  Practice

The results highlight the practical implications of integrating AI coaching into public administration, emphasizing its scalability and cost-effectiveness as an alternative to traditional coaching while identifying key conditions necessary for successful implementation. Key recommendations for successful adoption include organizational support, such as the creation of clear policies for allocating dedicated time for self-reflective practices during work hours. Additionally, seamless integration of AI coaching into existing internal systems is crucial. This may involve the use of locally hosted AI models and features such as active reminders and calendar integration to minimize barriers and ensure consistent engagement. Finally, addressing concerns around data protection and the 'black-box' nature of AI tools highlights the need for transparent communication about underlying language models, self-reflection frameworks, and data handling practices.

## 6.3 Limitations,  Concluding  Remarks,  and  Outlook

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The evaluation period of two weeks offers only initial insights. A more exhaustive study is recommended to better understand usage and impact. Additionally, our reliance on self-reported competence development could be complemented by more objective measures in future research. The sample size of seven participants limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader public administration context.

Our study highlights both the potential and limitations of AI coaching in fostering self-reflection competences in public administration. The qualitative findings indicate promising benefits by enhancing awareness of self-reflection opportunities and providing guidance for structuring thoughts.

One of the key strengths of AI coaching lies in its ability to provide structured, non-judgmental guidance, which supports users in engaging with self-reflective processes in a consistent and accessible manner. The integration of evidencebased frameworks ensures that ReflectAI follows a structured approach to facilitate meaningful self-reflection. Our findings demonstrate that AI coaching can effectively enhance self-reflective thinking by encouraging users to articulate their thoughts in a structured format, reinforcing prior research on expressive writing and self-reflection in digital environments. At the same time, ReflectAI's limitations highlight the need for continued refinement of AI coaching to improve adaptability and user trust.

Future research should implement long-term studies to evaluate the organizational impact of AI coaching on competence development. Particular attention should be paid to addressing the organizational barriers identified in our study, such as time constraints and technical infrastructure limitations, and to determine how to integrate AI coaching into existing HR development programs. Future research should also explore the application of AI coaching to other competences beyond self-reflection.
