(IFSW, 2014, para 2), social work is 'a practice-based profession and an academic discipline that promotes social change and development, social cohesion, and the empowerment and liberation of people'. Professionally, social work is underpinned by theories from social sciences, humanities, and Indigenous knowledge systems, which are leveraged to engage individuals and structures to address life challenges and enhance wellbeing  (Chowa,  2024;  Saleebey,  2000).  Social  workers  operate  across  many  levels  of hospital and healthcare practice (Eddison et al., 2024; Orr et al., 2021; Ruth &amp; Marshall, 2017). At the micro level they provide direct support to individuals and families (Moon &amp; McDermott,  2020;  Orr  et  al.,  2021;  Pockett  et  al.,  2022;  Vo,  2020);  at  the  meso  level they  work  with  groups  and  communities  (Nayda  et  al.,  2025);  and  at  the  macro  level they influence policy, advocate for systemic change, and contribute to social development (Booth &amp; Venville, 2021; IFSW, 2014). Their work spans diverse settings including hospitals, schools, correctional facilities, and government agencies (among other institutions and human service organisations).

In  Australia,  the  Australian  Association  of  Social  Workers  (AASW)  defines  social work as a profession committed to the pursuit of social justice, human rights, and professional integrity (AASW, 2025). The AASW emphasises the importance of self-determination, empowerment,  and  advocacy  in  addressing  structural  inequalities  and promoting  wellbeing.  As  such,  social  workers  are  uniquely  positioned  to  bridge  the gap  between  individuals  and  the  systems  that  affect  their  lives.  They  are  trained  to assess  complex  biopsychosocial  factors,  facilitate  access  to  resources,  and  support people through crises and transitions. Whether working in healthcare, child protection, mental  health,  or  aged  care,  social  workers  play  a  critical  role  in  promoting  dignity, inclusion, and equity for all members of society (AASW, 2025).

Hospitals and health services are complex places for social workers to develop and represent  their  professional  status,  role,  and  identity  in  the  midst  of  multidisciplinary and  interdisciplinary  teams  and  misunderstandings  about  social  work  practice  (Long et  al.,  2023).  Social  work,  as  a  profession,  is  embedded  deeply  in  the  psychosocial model of care, emphasising the social, cultural, psychological, and political dimensions of health and wellbeing. Unlike medical professionals whose roles often are associated with tangible evidence-based procedures, treatments, and clinical environments (World Health Organization, 2013), social workers engage in complex, relational, and often  invisible  forms  of  care  (Cederbaum  et  al.,  2022;  Fantus  et  al.,  2022;  Morley, 2022).  Social  work  roles  include  advocacy,  emotional  support,  crisis  intervention,  and connecting  individuals  with  community  resources;  tasks  that  are  not  captured  easily through  conventional  visual  or  discursive  tropes  (Cootes  et  al.,  2022;  Dimla  et  al., 2023; Payne, 2020).

## AI in Healthcare

The  integration  of  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  into  healthcare  systems  has  accelerated rapidly,  reshaping  how  care  is  delivered,  documented,  and  visualised.  Among  the many applications of AI, generative image technologies increasingly are used to create visual representations of healthcare professionals. Often these images are used in promotional, educational, or illustrative contexts, carry implicit messages about professional identity,  roles,  inequities,  and  hierarchies  within  healthcare  (Agrawal  et  al.,  2025).  In

Gisselbaek  et  al.  (2024),  AI-generated  images  of  'heads  of  department'  and  general anaesthesiologists as predominantly male and paediatric and obstetric anaesthesiologists as predominantly female, suggested that this could diminish a woman's contributions in the field and influence medical students' career perceptions and aspirations and demonstrating the bias hidden within AI-generated images. Similarly, in Currie et al. (2024), generative  AI  images  of  pharmacists  were  males  with  light  skin  tone.  However,  the visual  portrayal  of  certain  professions,  particularly  those  grounded  in  social  and  relational  care,  remains  underexplored.  As  such,  an  exploration  of  the  representation  of social workers is timely.

AI-generated images are trained on vast datasets that often reflect existing societal biases (Celi et al., 2022). In healthcare, this is particularly problematic. For example, often  AI-generated  depictions  of  healthcare  professionals  default  to  white,  ablebodied,  and  conventionally  attractive  individuals  in  clinical  settings,  reinforcing narrow  and  exclusionary  norms  (Centres  for  Disease  Control,  2024).  When  applied to  professions  like  social  work,  where  diversity,  cultural  competence,  and  relational care are central, these images can distort public understanding and devalue the profession's  core  principles  (Byrne  et  al.,  2024).  Moreover,  AI  systems  are  not  yet capable  of  nuanced  interpretation,  and  this  results  in  profound  ethical  implications. As the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) notes, the misuse of AI in public  health  can  exacerbate  existing  disparities  and  reinforce  systemic  inequities (Centres for Disease Control, 2024). Indeed, without careful oversight, AI-generated imagery risks becoming a tool of erasure, rendering the very people and practices it aims to represent, invisible. Thus, while AI offers powerful tools for visualisation, its application  in  healthcare  and  social  care  must  be  approached  with  critical  scrutiny, ethical safeguards, and a commitment to inclusive representation (Bohr &amp; Memarzadeh, 2020; Uddagiri &amp; Isunuri, 2024).

Social worker researchers including Boetto (2025), Hodgson et al. (2023) and Singer et al. (2023) expressed concern about the development of generative AI tools such as Large Language Models (LLM) that can generate text, images, video, and audio, and the implications for integrating AI tools into social work practice. Similarly,Miskolczi's  (2025)  research  about  how  AI-generated  images  have  fooled  social  media  users and  Reamer's  (2023)  discussion  about  ethical  issues  about  AI  in  social  work  have raised  awareness  and  concern  amongst  social  work  researchers,  educators,  and practitioners.

The aims of this study were to critically explore the representation of social workers in the  hospital  setting  through  AI-generated  images  and  to  consider  how  these  images reflect  the  social,  cultural,  historical  and  political  positioning  of  social  workers  and those they care for. This study adopted a critical discourse analysis (CDA) framework (Machin &amp; Mayr, 2023) to interrogate how AI-generated images depict social workers and the hospital or healthcare settings in which they operate. In the study, authors analysed the semiotic and ideological dimensions of these images, exploring how they reflect, or fail to reflect, the social realities of the profession. In doing so, this article contributes to a growing body of literature that critiques the role of AI in shaping professional identities  and  social  narratives.  It  raises  important  questions  about  whose  work  is  made visible, whose is erased, and how technological tools may inadvertently reinforce existing power structures within healthcare.

## Positionality

Reflexivity (Fook &amp; Gardner, 2007) is important for researchers to explicitly recognise their professional background and experiences as this directly influences the ways they make sense of the world and how they construct meaning. In this study, Author one is an experienced social work academic who has not worked as a clinician in a hospital but has worked extensively to provide professional development training and supervision to social workers and social work students during field education in hospital and healthcare settings. Authors two and three are experienced nurses and academics with extensive experience in clinical nursing and nurse leadership roles in hospitals in Australia. The professional  education,  experiences,  and  world  view  influence  the  lenses  that  the authors  use  when  analysing  images  of  social  workers  in  a  hospital  environment  and the assumptions that they make about what seems to fit in and what looks to be out of place.

## Methodology

## Research Design

This  research  took  a  critical  approach,  using  a  critical  discourse  analysis  (CDA) framework.

## Procedure and Data Collection

This article leveraged the published work of Byrne et al. (2024), based on the work of Machin  and  Mayr  (2023)  and  Fairclough  (1985),  who  used  AI  images  to  explore  the positioning and representation of aged care nurses. As such, a systematic methodological approach was adopted. Firstly, two generative AI products were selected, based on university  subscription  and  availability  to  the  researchers.  The  programs  included  Adobe Illustrator and Adobe Express Text to Image Generator. It is recognised that, depending on the prompt used to generate these images, different depictions of any topic will be created. Thus, prompts were tested and applied to align with the aim of the study. For each  product,  the  team  provided  the  following  image-generating  prompts,  as  can  be seen in Table 1.

Prompts were entered into the two image generators on 29 January 2025. The collected  data  sample  included  the  first  four  images  from  each  prompt  in  each  program (16 images in total).

## Data Analysis

Analysis followed the work described by Byrne et al. (2024), adapted from Machin and Mayr  (2023)  and  Fairclough  (2015),  demonstrated  in  Figure  1.  Images  were  grouped together  for  analysis.  Semiotic  choices,  lexical  interpretations,  values,  visual  elements including what is denoted and their connotations were analysed as per the framework in Figure 1.

Once  generation  of  the  images  occurred,  each  author  independently  reviewed  and examined  the  images  using  the  analysis  framework  (Figure  1).  Images  were  assessed (inspired  by  the  work  of  Machin  &amp;  Mayr,  2023)  for  denotation,  connotation,  visual elements,  representations,  recontextualisation  and  legitimisation  via  a  purpose-built extraction  table.  For  example,  framing,  gender,  presentation,  colours,  background, environment,  and  features  such  as  facial  expression,  positioning  of  a  clipboard,  and wearing of a stethoscope were noted and tabulated by each author.

A subsequent meeting with all authors was held to review the findings. Where consistency across authors was noted, these were categorised and then merged into initial themes. These themes were positioned back into the social, cultural, and political landscape of social work practice, providing more depth to the analysis, offering critical perspectives.  For  example,  the  social  identity  of  the  social  worker  was  considered, and  their  relation  to  the  environment,  background  colours,  and  content.  A  final meeting was held to generate the agreed overall themes, which were achieved via consensus. This allowed for a rich analysis of what was depicted in the image, as well as through the image.

## Findings

A total of 16 images across four groups were generated, as per Table 2. Bronfenbrenner's  (1979)  Ecological  Systems  Theory  was  a  useful  framework  for  deconstructing and  understanding  the  various  interactions  between  the  generated  images  and  their meaning.  There  were  many  terms  that  are  used  to  describe  the  service  user  (client, consumer,  patient)  and  as  the  setting  is  a  hospital,  the  term  'patient'  is  used  to mean  the  person  receiving  the  service.  The  findings  were  summarised  and  presented under social orders framework, and can be categorised under the following ideologies: The  Invisible  Person  (Micro),  'Social'  work?  (Meso),  and  The  Medical  System:  Controlling  and  Controlled  (Macro).  The  discussion  then  considered  the  wider  implications  over  time  (the  Chronosystem).

## The Invisible Person (Micro)

An unexpected finding from the analysis was the lack of people (as patients or clients) depicted  in  the  images.  Indeed,  of  all  16  images,  only  two  images  (2a  and  2b)  depict others in the background, and these appear to be healthcare professionals. One image (1a)  shows  a  muted,  blurred  image  of  a  person  who  cannot  be  identified  as  either patient  or  staff.  The  lack  of  patient  representation  was  curious,  given  that  the  work that  social  workers  do  is  deeply  connected  to  engagement,  developing  relationships, and identifying and supporting the social needs of people.

Although patients were not represented as people in the images, representations of who  patients  are  and  how  they  are  positioned  are  evident.  The  images  detailed  artefacts  that  acted  as  patient  representations;  for  example,  hospital  beds  (2b  and  4a),  a desk  or  kiosk  (4b),  and  clipboards  (3d  and  4c).  Hospital  beds  in  these  images  act to  order  patients;  indeed,  image  2d  shows  an  orderly  row  of  beds  in  a  ward.  The staff  member  (who  is  not  identifiable  as  a  social  worker)  is  framed  as  central  to  the image, with the patient beds (and thus the patients themselves) relegated to the background. Similarly, image 4a positions the hospital bed in the background of the image. The staff  member stands in front of the bed. The bed physically positions the patient in  a  lower  position  to  the  staff  member,  who  stands  over  them  in  delivering  care. Symbolically,  it  signals  a  power  structure  whereby  healthcare  professionals  enact care and patients receive it. The positioning of this (both physically and ideologically) denotes  subordination  of  patients.  Along  these  lines,  image  4b  shows  a  healthcare worker behind a desk or kiosk-type structure. The desk itself acts as a symbolic structure  that  separates  the  patient  from  the  worker  and  denotes  a  sense  of  order  and structure  in  the  environment  and  in  the  behaviour  of  patients.  In  these  images, patients  are  rendered  invisible,  both  by  their  physical  absence,  and  through  the  subordinate  positioning  the  environment  enforces.  The  use  of  a  clipboard  (3d  and  4c) reinforces  the  relationship  to  the  environment.  An  interesting  gesture  is  noted  in image  4c:  the  finger  pointing  in  the  air  denotes  a  sense  of  control,  knowledge,  and authority.  The  gesture,  while  done  so  with  a  smile  and  open  body  language,  gives the  sense  that  the  control  of  communication  and  information  comes  from  the worker  and  is  provided  to  the  patient.  This  subordinate  positioning  of  patients  acts

to remove autonomy and reaffirm expectations of what a patient 'should' be and how they  should  behave.  This  is  relevant  in  the  hospital  setting,  as  the  model  espoused  is usually  one  of  self-determination,  autonomy,  and  shared  goal  setting  and  decision making (Törrönen et al., 2013). As such, the micro space renders the person as invisible,  while  at  the  same  time  rendering  the  'social'  invisible.

## 'Social' Work? (Meso)

Images of social workers in this dataset provided unusual depictions of the profession. At a surface level, the images show clean, healthy individuals as 'social workers'. Indeed, the images of social workers themselves show a lack of diversity, suggesting a medical and biomedical dominance, and, thus, an authoritarian positioning of the worker.

The images reflect  normative gender conforms, for example, women in the images have  long  hair  (1c,  1d,  2a,  2b,  2c,  2d,  3a,  3c,  4b,  and  4c),  while  men  have  short  hair and  facial  hair  (1a,  1b,  3b,  3d,  and  4a).  One  male  does  not  have  facial  hair  (4d)  yet sports  the  clean-cut  image.  The  cartoon  images  further  drive  gender  norms  through longer eyelashes and eyeliner makeup for the women (3a, 3c, 4b, and 4c). While race is  represented in images (1a, 1d, 2c, 2d, and 4d), the majority appear to be Caucasian. The  images  do  little  to  express  individuality:  there  are  no  earrings,  hair  colours  are natural,  and there are no tattoos or cultural expressions. Overall, the expressed vision of social workers is that of a clean professional image of healthy men and women of a consistent age.  In many ways, the images are tokenistic, denoting a sense of class, for example,  the  women  have  highlighted  hair  (1c,  2a,  2b,  2c,  and  2b),  and  the  people have straight white teeth (1a, 1c, 2a, 2b, 2c, and 2d). The images denote a sense of intelligence, for example, the knowing smile in images 1b and 1d, and the finger in the air of image 4c.

Uniforms  and  medical  artefacts  act  to  identify  the  workers  and,  at  the  same  time, remove  them  from  'social'  work.  All  images  depict  social  workers  as  wearing  scrubs or medical coats (1a-4d). Further highlighting these workers as belonging to the hospital system, name badges can be seen (1a, 1b, 1c, 2d, 3a, 3b, 3c, and 3d). One artefact that featured in all images is the stethoscope (1a-4d). Byrne et al. (2024) highlighted stethoscopes in images as a nod to the biomedical model of care and the medicalised forms of care. For example, stethoscopes are used to assess heart and lung sounds (among other uses), a task which is not within the scope or aim of the social worker.

Indeed, much of the work that social workers do (though not limited to) is in listening, supporting,  engaging  with  individuals  and  families,  and  linking  people  with  services; social workers work with people, not at them. These images, as such, provided a false image of a social worker, rendering the work that they do invisible and markedly subservient to the biomedical model of care. The lack of diversity in the images speaks to a normalising of what it is and, more importantly, what it is not to be a healthcare worker. The use of stethoscopes and the neutral clean image simultaneously positions social workers as authoritative over patients and positions social work as invisible; the biomedical model dominates. In this way, social workers in these images have been rendered indistinguishable,  as  has  social  'work'.  In  fact,  these  images  are  not  of  social  workers;  they  are  of doctors, medical officers, or nurses.

## The Medical System: Controlling and Controlled (Macro)

As discussed earlier, the images denote a medical dominance and a clear framing of the biomedical model of healthcare. The images of the people have acted to position social work as subservient to medical care and, in doing so, have also provided an image of what medical or hospital care is .

The images show the hospital environment as clean, calm, and orderly. For example, natural light is seen coming through large windows (1a, 1d, 2a, 2b, and 2d). Images where people are in the background denote a calm pace; image 2a shows professionals liaising, image 2b shows professionals moving down a hallway. The beds in images 2d and 4a and the kiosk in 4b are neat and orderly. The people and the environment evoke a sense of control and structure.

The  stethoscope,  as  mentioned  earlier,  is  a  specific  metaphor  for  the  biomedical model. Being around the neck (1a-4d) of the professionals (though the normal way it is  worn  for  convenience)  is  indicative  of  the  dominance  and  stranglehold  of  both  the medical  profession  and  the  medical  models,  which  are  often  ill-equipped  to  support the  social,  cultural,  and  psychological  needs  of  patients.  In  fact,  the  important  aspects of  social  care  do  not  feature  in  the  images,  for  example,  there  are  no  patients,  care  is occurring only in the hospital, 1 vulnerable and diverse populations are not represented (as patients or as workers), nor are socioeconomic factors. By badging the professionals with such biomedical markers, it acts to exclude those who do not fit this mould, including social workers themselves.

Along these lines, the analysis demonstrated that power and control are clearly evident in the images. All images (1a-4d) frame the individuals centrally, and although this was the focus of the prompts, the framing acts to exclude those they care for. For example, there is no image of a social worker providing support to a patient or having a conversation with a patient either in person or over the phone. Control and order are also demonstrated in images such as 4b, where a worker sits behind a desk, symbolising power and authority. Similarly, image 4c shows the working with a finger in the air, giving an air of authority. By doing so, these images reinforce ideologies of who owns knowledge, who is in  charge  of  care,  and  how  care  is  delivered  to  those  in  need.  The  environments  (for example,  the  ward  in  image  2d,  the  hallway  in  2a,  the  desk  in  4b,  and  the  clipboards in 4c and 3d) are exclusionary; those who seek care must do so under the enforced conditions; to do otherwise is to be non-compliant (or other titles that exclude those outside of the healthcare 'norm').

What is clear from the analysis is that across the social orders, social work and social workers are not represented. In fact, by framing the patient, social worker, and system in such  stereotyped  and  medicalised  ways,  social  work  has  been  excluded  from  the  care landscape.

## Discussion Over Time: The Chronosystem

This study set out to critically explore the representation of social workers in the hospital setting through AI-generated images and to consider how these images reflect the social, cultural,  historical,  and  political  positioning  of  social  workers  and  those  they  care  for. Across the micro, meso, and macro structures, it raised questions about the invisibility

of patients, the 'social' work that social workers do, and the wider system in which they are positioned. The findings of this study revealed not only the visual erasure of social work but also the deeper temporal and epistemological forces at play. These images do not  merely  depict  the  present;  they  are  trained  on  datasets  steeped  in  the  past.  Their bias  can  increase  minority  marginalisation  and  increase  inequality  of  healthcare  (Celi et al., 2022) reproducing visual tropes that privilege biomedical authority and marginalise relational, social forms of care. Indeed, when viewed under the lens of the chronosystem order (the structuring of visibility through time), researchers may better understand how AI images reflect and reinforce historical biases in healthcare representation.

Social work is a comparatively young profession, emerging formally in the early twentieth century in response to growing awareness of inequality, poverty, and the need for structured welfare systems (Ruth &amp; Marshall, 2017). Unlike medicine or nursing, which have long-standing institutional histories and clearly delineated roles within healthcare, social work evolved from grassroots activism and community-based support (Mendes, 2005).  The  foundations  lie  in  social  justice,  advocacy,  and  relational  care,  principles that  often  resist  the  standardisation  and  procedural  logic  of  biomedical  systems (IFSW,  2014).  As  such,  social  work  does  not  always  align  with  the  hierarchical,  taskoriented frameworks that dominate healthcare institutions (Fantus et al., 2022). The profession's emphasis on cultural responsiveness, empowerment, and systemic change positions it at the intersection of health, social policy, and human rights, domains that are frequently  siloed  in  practice  (Mapp  et  al.,  2019;  Orr  et  al.,  2021).  This  misalignment can lead to  the  marginalisation  of  social  work  within  clinical  environments,  where  its contributions  may  be  undervalued  or  misunderstood  (Cootes  et  al.,  2022).  The  result is that the relational and often invisible nature of social work practice challenges dominant visual and discursive representations of healthcare, which tend to privilege tangible interventions and medical authority, as demonstrated in this analysis. The translation of this representation is that social work is rendered visually 'stuck' in a biomedical past, a context where care is equated with clinical procedures, uniforms, and hierarchical structures. The absence of patients, the dominance of stethoscopes, and the central positioning of  healthcare  workers  all  point  to  a  visual  regime  that  privileges  control,  order,  and medical  decision  making  and  intervention.  This  regime  is  not  neutral;  it  is  a  product of  historical  narratives  that  have  excluded  the  social  dimensions  of  health.  This framing  intersects  with  questions  of  epistemic  authority,  described  by  Jager  (2025)  as the  authority  assigned  to  people  in  relation  to  beliefs,  knowledge,  and  understanding. Deeper  than  this,  it  refers  to  the  power  of  legitimacy  in  knowledge,  and  how  people understand  who  can  hold  authority  and  truth  (Popowics,  2021).  Socially,  power  and knowledge  are  enacted  in  careful  interplays  within  certain  contexts,  where  no  one person may hold authority, but rather the world is fashioned through history, power, influence, and dominance that work to subtly legitimise what it is to be an 'authority' (Foucault, 2006).

In this case, AI has provided a window into the social 'truth' of healthcare, and the way  society  has  structured  such  institutions.  Artificial  intelligence  systems,  trained with  dominant  cultural  narratives,  appear  to  act  as  knowledge-producing  tools.  They do  not  simply  reflect  reality;  they  appear  to  (re)construct  it.  In  doing  so,  they  define what care looks like, who provides it, and under what conditions. gender, health standards (fit and healthy), branded as recognisable healthcare workers, performing  their  roles  within  the  biomedical  structure  (after  all,  a  social  worker  has little need for a stethoscope). For professions like social work, which resist reductionist definitions and operate within complex, relational, and culturally responsive frameworks, this is deeply problematic; the images generated for this study do not just misrepresent social work, they act to redefine it, stripping away its core principles and reinscribing it within a biomedical logic. If AI-generated imagery becomes the default (or at least an easily accessible and readily available form) representation in educational, promotional, and policy contexts, then the epistemic authority of social work is at risk, an issue raised in  the  nursing  profession  by  Byrne  et  al.  (2024)  and  in  social  work  by  Boetto  (2025), Hodgson et al. (2023), Reamer (2023), and Singer et al. (2023).

There are risks for the social work profession in the ubiquitous nature of generative AI tools (Reamer, 2023; Singer et al., 2023). These tools are now embedded in many day-today software programs, and it can be difficult to identify AI-generated content compared with human-generated content (Miskolczi, 2025). Time poor academics, students, and social  work  practitioners  will  be  tempted  to  use  generative  AI  tools  (Boetto,  2025; Hodgson et al., 2023) and most people will not be able to tell the difference. The profession may find itself not only visually repositioned, but also epistemologically subordinate in its knowledge systems, values, and practices. At the very least, such images mean that social workers must continue to fight to define their professional identity, and the importance of their role in the medically-dominated sphere. Excluding social workers from  visual  and  epistemic  representations  of  healthcare  serves  to  reinforce  existing power  structures  that  benefit  from  a  narrow,  biomedical  framing  of  care.  Professions with long-standing institutional authority, such as medicine and nursing, often are privileged in both policy and practice, and their visibility in AI-generated imagery further consolidates  their  epistemic  dominance.  Furthermore,  healthcare  leadership  positions are often dominated by nurses and medical officers (Eddison et al., 2023), further facilitating  avenues  to  reinforce  traditional  roles  and  structures.  This  is  not  incidental;  it maintains a system where care is defined by clinical procedures, measurable outcomes, and  hierarchical  control.  This  may  not  be  the  intent  of  leaders  or  other  workforces, but rather a historic positioning that is often unyielding in its structure. By rendering social  work  invisible,  systems  run  the  risk  of  neglecting  the  more  complex,  relational, and systemic dimensions of health, dimensions that challenge efficiency-driven models and require deeper structural change.

Social work historically has faced significant challenges, particularly in the early years when there  were  debates  over  its  purpose,  definition,  and  methods.  These  differences gave rise to what Ruth and Marshall (2017) described as a 'dual heartbeat', a division between the work of clinical  social  workers  and  case  management  and  those  engaged in the broader community and structural reform. Despite these tensions, the profession responded with empowering and liberatory approaches, drawing inspiration from social activism  and  the  liberation  theology  of  the  1960s  and  1970s  (Saleebey,  2000).  Today, social  workers  practising  in  public  health  commonly  integrate  both  micro  and  macro approaches  (Ruth  &amp;  Marshall,  2017).  Although  AI-generated  imagery  may  suggest  a diminishing visibility  of  social  work,  the  profession  remains  resilient  and  maintains  a strong presence in clinical care, advocacy, community development, and policy reform.

## Limitations

This  study  offers  a  critical  exploration  of  AI-generated  imagery;  however,  limitations must  be  acknowledged.  First,  the  dataset  comprised  only  16  images  generated  from two AI platforms, Adobe Illustrator and Adobe Express Text to Image Generator, limiting the breadth and diversity of visual outputs. The findings may not be generalisable across other generative AI tools or platforms with different training datasets and algorithms. Second, the prompts used to generate images were refined through internal discussion,  but  prompt  engineering  remains  a  subjective  process.  The  interpretation  of images is  also  inherently  influenced  by  the  researchers'  professional  backgrounds  and positionality,  which,  while  acknowledged,  may  shape  the  analysis  in  specific  ways. Finally,  the  research  focused  solely  on  visual  representations  and  did  not  explore  the impact  of  these  images  on  viewers'  perceptions  or  professional  identity  formation. Future  research  could  incorporate  participatory  methods,  including  interviews  or focus  groups,  to  examine  how  AI-generated  imagery  influences  understanding  of social  work  roles  in  healthcare  and  contributes  to  broader  epistemic  and  professional dynamics.

## Recommendations

This article acts as a starting point and conversation starter for future work in this space. As such, this research has recommendations for practice, organisations, social work education, the AASW, and future research. Accountability is needed for technology companies  that  develop  such  tools.  Future  work  could  place  a  focus  on  auditing  training datasets  for  bias  and  inclusion,  engagement  with  healthcare  professionals in  codesign, and representative outputs and agreed standards or terms being required for AI image generation.  This might be achieved through collaborative development of visual databases  that  accurately  reflect  the  work  that  social  workers  do.  Social  workers  must engage in defining and defending the visual narrative and better advocate for professional identity  in  materials.  In  particular,  social  workers  in  hospitals  and  healthcare  settings need to be aware of and challenge these misconceptions, particularly for students and new  graduates.  This  might  be  achieved  by  social  workers  and  educators  thinking about  professional  identity  and  the  visibility  of  social  work  to  ensure  that  social workers are visually portrayed as skilled professionals rather than as generic helpers or carers.

The Australian Association of Social Workers (AASW) is uniquely positioned to lead the conversation about AI-generated images of social workers, particularly in health and hospital settings and human service organisations. They need to explicitly name AI-generated images as a professional practice issue and clarify expectations around transparency and  misleading  representation of practice roles. This should  include clear guidelines  for  universities  and  human  service  organisations  about  how  social  workers should be represented visually and the ethical use of AI imagery in social work practice. The AASW role must extend beyond the profession to engage with government, other peak bodies, and human service organisations to advocate on behalf of the profession, support  members  to  challenge  misrepresentation  safely,  and  invest  in  research  and knowledge leadership to build the evidence base.

## Conclusion

This study critically examined the representation of social workers in healthcare through AI-generated imagery, revealing a pervasive erasure of the profession's core values and practices.  Across  micro,  meso,  and  macro  levels,  the  findings  demonstrate  that  AI tools, trained on dominant cultural narratives, misrepresent social work by privileging biomedical  aesthetics,  hierarchical  structures,  and  normative  depictions  of  care.  The absence  of  patients,  the  dominance  of  medical  symbols  such  as  stethoscopes,  and  the central positioning of healthcare workers all contribute to a visual regime that marginalises relational, culturally responsive, and advocacy-based forms of care (social work).

Researchers do not know the impact of generating these images and the connection to professional identity development of social workers in a hospital. This, along with asking social  workers  to  agree  on  what  a  social  worker  in  a  hospital  looks  like,  is  a  topic  for future  research.  What  is  clear  is  that  hospitals  and  health  services  are  environments where it is common for social work students to explore case studies as part of their education and many students will experience field education in a hospital or health service. As graduate social workers are thinking about and developing their professional identity, they need to be able to see themselves in the role of a hospital social worker or social worker in a community health service. Social workers need to challenge the generative AI tools that misrepresent the contribution of social workers in hospitals and healthcare settings and render social workers invisible. This visual and epistemic erasure has consequences not only for social work practice and the development of professional identity, but  also  public  understanding,  workforce  recognition,  interdisciplinary  collaboration, and greater system change. It underscores the urgent need for inclusive design practices, ethical  standards  in  AI  development,  and  active  engagement  from  social  work  professionals  in  shaping  their  own  representation.  Ultimately  the  authors  argue  that  the social work profession and those in healthcare need to lead and take ownership of the professional response to ensure that generative AI tools represent the values and ethics of the profession.
