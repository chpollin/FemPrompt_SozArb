## A Critique of Human-Centred AI: A Plea for a Feminist AI Framework (FAIF)

Tanja Kubes 1

Received: 17 March 2025 / Accepted: 29 July 2025 / Published online: 19 August 2025

## Abstract

As artificial intelligence (AI) reshapes science, industries, public discourse and our personal lives, it is becoming increasingly clear that there is a downside to unregulated advancements in the field. Human-centred AI (HCAI) has been hailed as an important step towards fairness, equity, and inclusion. However, a critical feminist analysis shows that HCAI falls short of achieving these goals. HCAI, as it is currently practised, remains to a large extend Eurocentric, androcentric, and anthropocentric, and is driven by a capitalist market logic without commitment to sustainability. It disregards and exacerbates global injustices and ecological impacts. The article criticizes HCAI from a feminist, post humanist, and neo-materialist perspective and proposes a Feminist AI Framework (FAIF) that also incorporates findings from more-than-human anthropology and body-phenomenology. FAIF aims to reassess the relationship between humans, other life-forms, and technology and explores the potential of collaborative, non-hierarchical design, usage, and controlling of AI. Rather than being primarily about damage control, FAIF serves as a proactive attempt to ensure the co-constitutive wellbeing of humans and non-humans by breaking with traditional narratives about AI and progress.

Keywords Feminist AI · Human-centred AI · AI ethics · AI governance · Posthumanism · Responsible AI

## 1    The disruptive effects of AI

Artificial intelligence has fundamentally changed the ways we live, work, do business, communicate, organize, learn, write, create artworks, make politics, wage wars, think and form relationships. No technology in history has impacted so many aspects in the lives of so many people in such a short time. AI has become part of our everyday lives as individuals, and it changes social life as a whole. While many still hope these changes will be for the better, the risks of a naive trust in technology are becoming increasingly apparent. As of today, artificial intelligences remain confined to specific tasks. In the areas of data processing, pattern recognition and automation, they are already superior to humans. In other fields, such as understanding context and moral decisions, humans are (still) ahead. However, if the predictions of many technology experts come true, generative AIs could significantly expand their range of action and

To help steer things in the right direction, we need global cooperation and transnational regulations (Batool/Zowghi/Bano 2025). In this paper, I argue that such a "foundational commitment to human rights" is not enough. It is not enough to stop developing AIs that primarily serve the interests of a technologically advanced, predominantly Northern elite and direct it toward the well-being of humanity as a whole instead. It is not enough to stop the accumulation of power and money in the hands of a select few and aim for a fair distribution of AI's benefits and the preservation of resources. It is not enough to democratise research and design in the spirit of citizen science and to engage in participatory technology design. A sustainable approach to AI needs to go further and requires a fundamental revision of its ontological and epistemological prerequisites.

This reassessment integrates a wide range of perspectives and synthesizes them into, what I call, a Feminist AI Framework (FAIF). With 'classical' feminism, it looks at the role of women and other marginalized groups in the tech sector. They are underrepresented in development teams and their particular needs for the use of new technologies are given little consideration. With postcolonialism, it addresses global inequalities and power relations. From science and technology studies, it adopts the idea of the situatedness of knowledge, and from environmental studies the attentiveness to the excessive consumption of resources by present-day AI. FAIF goes beyond these particular criticisms and asks about the fundamental shortcomings of today's narratives of human-centred AI.

Looking at such alternative forms of "worlding' (Descola 2024) can provide decisive impetus for a responsible use of AI. ## 2    Human-centred AI and socio-technical perspective

For some years now, efforts have been underway at national and EU levels to thwart the risks of AI through regulations and standardisations and ensure that technological advancements align with the expectations and requirements of their human users. The idea has a history that spans more than four decades within the domain of human-machine interaction (HMI). Applying the concept to AI is relatively new but has proven productive. A Google Scholar search, conducted at the beginning of March 2025, yielded approximately 27,700 scholarly publications on the subject of humancentred AI. 3 The EU AI Act 2024 (EUR-Lex 2024), the United Nations (UN 2024), the International Organization for Standardization (ISO 2023), and the German Institute for Standardization (DIN 2022) all address the subject. A recurrent theme in all these publications is the imperative for trustworthy AI systems to be designed with humans and their well-being as a paramount concern across all stages of development, implementation, and controlling.

Central to the success of any such measures is the adoption of a holistic socio-technical perspective. This points beyond mere technological feasibility and instead focuses on ethical and cultural questions and the complex interactions between technology and the individual, or technology and society (Suchman 2007; Zweig et al. 2021, DIN DKE 2022). Which cultural and social norms, for example, shape our interaction with chatbots? Should we say please and thank you in chats? Is it okay to get to the point immediately when we ask a question? How direct can an answer be without being perceived as rude and impolite by users? Or with regard to facial recognition software: What methods of automated personal identification can be used in a society in which people are veiled in public spaces or transport loads on their heads? And how does the technology interfere with local ideas of privacy and personal space? A socio-technical perspective on AI integrates technical elements (hardware, data, algorithms, etc.) with psychological and social elements (individuals, society, economy, labour market, politics, etc.) and aims to enable as many people as possible to profit from technological progress. So much for the theory.

In practise, however, the history of knowledge and science is deeply steeped in sexism, racism, and Eurocentrism (Barad 2007, Braidotti/Lykke 1996, Fox-Keller 1986, Haraway 1991, Haraway 1988, Harding 1998, Harding 1986, Harding 1991, Harding 2006, Vallor 2024, Wajcman 2004). Feminist authors like Ruha Benjamin, Sascha ConstanzaChock, Safiya Noble, or Joy Buolamwini demonstrated time and again how facial recognition software does not reliably recognize people of colour, how credit systems disadvantage people with certain ethnic and demographic characteristics, how body scanners misclassify queer and non-binary people, how medical diagnostic tools do not work properly for women or POC, how job hiring software systematically disadvantages women and POC, and how search engines may amplify sexism, racism and misogyny (Benjamin 2019, Buolamwini 2018, Costanza-Chock 2020).

Consequently, advocates of a feminist perspective on AI call for more justice, responsibility and transparency in AI and rightly emphasize the necessity of diverse and intersectional perspectives in order to do justice to the complex interactions between technology, society and the individual. In doing so, they open up the concept of human-centredness to more diverse intersectional perspectives. In fact, the feminist critique - and by extension, my Feminist AI Framework (FAIF) - goes even further. Not only does it identify concrete practices of discrimination and call for more diversity, equity, and inclusion (on company boards, in STEM, in design, etc.), it also labels the structural factors that are at the bottom of discriminatory practises. Central here, is the concept of "situated knowledges" developed by Donna Haraway (1988). It discards the notions of universal objectivity and ultimate knowledge and instead puts knowledge and truth in the plural. Following Haraway, 'objectivity' can only be thought of in the context of reflexive, responsible and context-bound acts of knowledge production. It is not something that exists outside of people, but rather, it becomes a performatively embodied - or: situated - understanding of what is true within the bounds of concrete power structures and valid only from a specific point of view.

For technical innovations, this means that the widespread illusion of 'neutral technology' turns out to be just that: an illusion. Knowledge and power cannot be separated. All knowledge, all technology, all innovation are rooted in specific cultural, social and historical contexts, and the inequalities established therein merge into intersectional structures. Knowledge, thus, necessarily remains perspectival, pluralistic, incomplete - or with Haraway (1992) and Braidotti and Lykke (1996): "monstrous". It can no longer be reduced to simple chains of cause and effect or reflection and replication but is constituted via intersectional interferences. It becomes "diffractive" (Haraway 1992).

There is a broad consensus that this is true for cultural and social knowledges, but within FAIF it also applies to the supposedly axiomatic knowledge of mathematics, the natural sciences, and computer science. The perspectivism and performance oriented approach of classical feminism and feminist STS form one pillar of FAIF. Their demands are important and correct, and in their broad interpretation they may also include key issues of postcolonial criticism. However, in order to substantially counteract the technodeterministic, capitalist narrative with its emphasis on profit maximation and market expansion (Engster/Moore 2020; Moore 2020; Fox 2023), it is necessary to tackle not only its patent manifestations, but to confront its roots. This is where FAIF brings critical posthumanism, new materialism, and more-than-human anthropology into play.

## 3    The easy and the hard problems of AI

The dominant narrative of human-centred AI builds upon a specific form of substance dualism, which places humans in a unique position within the order of the world. Drawing on Descartes' distinction between the 'immaterial' consciousness, capable of rational reflection ( res cogitans) , and its material environment incapable of thinking ( res extensa), the human mind is given primacy over its physical substrate. This ignores the "hard problem" (Chalmers 1995), namely the relationship between physical phenomena (e.g. neuronal activities) and mental experiences .  It  also dismisses the "paradox of modernity" with its "insurmountable tension between subject and object" (Latour 1993). Instead, large parts of AI research are based on an unspoken materialism that sees consciousness as a function of physical processes and assumes that sooner or later it will be possible to

## 4    Diffracting ontologies and more-than-human anthropologies

Modernity is characterised by dualisms: nature/culture, subject/object, male/female, original/copy, Western/non-Western. This ontological phrasing goes hand in hand with the epistemological belief that the world can be known and represented from a neutral, disembodied perspective - Donna Haraway's "god trick," a "leap out of the marked body and into a conquering gaze from nowhere" (Haraway 1988). Modernist epistemology claims representations objectively reflect reality. Like curved mirrors, they may present us a distorted image, but we can always trace back the optical transformations to see how things really are.

This simplistic view of the relationship between phenomena and their representations faces criticism from multiple directions. Physicists have long rejected naïve-materialist notions of reality. Already Heisenberg stated that quantum 'reality' - the collapse of wavefunctions from superposition to a classical probabilistic state - emerges only through observation. Haraway's concept of "situated knowledges" follows a similar trajectory. Haraway rejects both the god trick of "seeing everything from nowhere" and reducing physical objects to "ephemera of discursive production and social construction" (Haraway 1988). Like Heisenberg, she seeks alternatives to both realism and radical constructivism. Rather than embracing observer-independent reality or poststructuralist relativism, Haraway advocates "partial, locatable, critical knowledges sustaining the possibility of webs of connections called solidarity in politics and shared conversations in epistemology" (ibid).

This notwithstanding, Haraway does not endorse  an uncritical pluralism. She insists on separating "the good scientific sheep from the bad goats of bias and misuse" (ibid). In other words: Some interpretations of reality simply are 'better' than others. Haraway's idea of objectivity avoids both the god trick's disembodied observation and standpoint theory's essentialist claims about an epistemic privilege of marginalized groups (Hartsock 1983). Instead, she proposes radical openness to the point of even questioning the researcher's very identity. In Haraway's view both the knowing subject and its knowledge about the world only emerge in and through the research process.

In Meeting the Universe Halfway, Karen Barad (2007) goes even further. Barad, who holds a doctorate in particle physics and quantum field theory but has been professor for feminist theory in the History of Consciousness Department at UCSC since 2006 - the same institute that hosted Donna Haraway and Anna Tsing, who will be mentioned later - builds more strongly on the Copenhagen interpretation. Following Bohr (and thus ultimately Kant and Hegel), she takes " phenomena, rather  than independent objects with inherent boundaries and properties" as 'the primary ontological unit' (Barad 2007). Her theory of "intra-acting agencies" replaces pre-discursive identities with ontological entanglements - relations without preexisting relata. Barad

focuses on relationship patterns rather than ontological differences (Haraway 1997).

From the perspective of FAIF, there are two major consequences to this. First, human and non-human actors no longer need to be sorted into separate ontological spheres but form common actor networks or collectives in which people and things come together in specific articulations as instituted entities (Latour 2001). This results in a fundamental openness and incompleteness of ontological classes. And secondly, the fact that the selves crystallizing from such articulations are mutually constitutive does not mean that the relationship is necessarily non-hierarchical or organized on an equal footing. Acknowledging the agency of non-human actors does not mean granting them the same degree of intentionality, purposefulness and volition as the human part of the collective. We are used to privileging human agency in cross-species constellations and ultimately thinking of the agency of animals or things as simply some form of 'resistance' (Därmann 2014).

Current developments in AI, however, threaten to upend this relationship and, for the first time, put humans in the position of the more passive part of the articulation. This harbours considerable risks for the persistence of human existence. If, at some point, AI could make decisions and pursue its own goals, it could decide to use nuclear power to meet its energy needs without considering the effects of radioactive radiation on life. For an AI, the possibly fatal contamination of the environment for humans and many other living beings would simply not be a "difference that makes a difference" (Bateson 1985). The question of how collectives of humans and AI negotiate a common world is therefore an existential one that must not be left to the invisible hand of the market.

Even though AI is not there yet, we should take a moment to rethink our relationship with the world. In recent decades, it has become clear that modernity suffers from a structural antinomy that cannot be solved relying on conventional repertoires of critique. Our continued attempts to separate nature and society have led to a rapid proliferation of hybrid "quasi-objects" (Latour 1993). Humanity may have reached a remarkable degree of 'mastery of nature'. Geoengineering, space travel, assisted reproduction, vaccines, etc. testify to this. However, each new solution has also created new problems: climate change, tipping points, space debris, 'hereditary' infertility, allergies, etc. It seems like the quasi-objects are increasing in complexity. Even worse, none of them can be unambiguously assigned to either nature or culture any longer. The continued fabrication of quasi-objects thus takes any endeavour for separation ad absurdum. Also, the insistence on human exceptionalism the ontological and epistemological foundation of modernity - proves ineffective. Yet, how could we reconceptualise the relationship between (wo) man and the world?

Building on the epistemological reorientation through situating knowledges (Haraway) and the ontological reformulation of emergent subjectivity (Barad), it is worth looking beyond the ontological horizon of the global North. Anthropologists have time and again argued that the societies they study do not simply impose other categories on the world. Rather, they create other worlds . And what's more, worlding is by no means an exclusively human occupation. Anna Tsing emphatically points out that every organism, from the smallest bacterium to the giant tree, actively shapes its environment. They all dynamically contribute to the open, situational coming together of the most diverse actors we call the 'world'. Not only through the collaborative relationships they enter, but also through the disturbances they cause (Tsing 2015). 6 Tsing describes a world that refuses to be unified and hierarchically accessed. Rather, it only reveals itself to the viewer in locally limited "patches". If we want to understand the Anthropocene, Tsing concludes, we need to look at local actors. "[N]ot because planetwide conditions have local effects and instantiations, as so many studies have it; instead, (…) patches make the Anthropocene, including its planetary form" (Tsing et al. 2024).

Building on this perspective, the Relational Ontology of Multi-species Assemblages (ROMA) (Kubes/Reinhardt 2022) challenges modernist ontology by deconstructing its underlying dualism and anthropocentrism. While there is already a large body of literature on actor networks and multi-species structures (Kneer 2008, Ogden/Hall/Tanita 2013), ROMA goes one step further, highlighting the coconstitutive and co-creative nature of actors - biological, technical, and symbolic alike - integrating them terminologically and conceptually into interfering, diffractive forms of worlding . ROMA suggests a perspective on the liaison of human and non-human actors radically focussing on relationality, rather than identity. It explicitly emphasizes that all actors (human, non-human, biological, technical and symbolic entities) are woven into co-constitutive, co-creative fabrics of relationships.

Relationality, thus, not only serves as one epistemic or social factor among others, but as the fundamental base of existence itself. In ROMA, there are no discrete entities (as in other ontological models), but the world is understood entirely as a relational, constantly changing, emerging relational structure. This applies to existence in general, but especially to what we understand as life . In ROMA, life cannot be reduced to a specific form of materiality or a binary state but is defined as a gradual relational process.

6 A similar approach is pursued by the work of other representatives of multi-species anthropology, which draws attention to the multiple ways in  which  human  and  non-human  actors  (fungi,  trees,  animals, microbes,  etc.)  are  interconnected  (see  Hastrup  [ed]  2015,  Kohn 2013, Morizot 2022, Viveiros de Castro 2017).

Building on ROMA, FAIF specifically applies this concept to AI. Already today, artificial intelligences share some properties of 'life' in a broader sense: they participate in intra-active processes of co-creation and co-evolution, they shape the existence of actors they intra-act with, and they are agentially active in multifarious ways. Unlike Barad, who describes life as a material dynamic, ROMA defines it as an emergent property of relational structures. It is not one of two binary states (living vs. non-living), but a gradual, dynamic process of co-creations of realities, from which not only knowledge, but also existence itself emerges - without a clear separation between (wo)man, nature, matter or technology.

This calls for an ethical framework that allows for a collaborative, non-hierarchical and equitable design and control of AI. Given the blurring of boundaries and the recognition of non-human actors - animals, plants, ecosystems, things, technologies and spiritual beings - as participants on an equal footing with humans in social, cultural and ecological processes, FAIF advances findings from more-than-human anthropology and critical posthumanism - in particular their criticism of anthropocentrism (Braidotti 2013, 2021; Ferrando 2013, 2020). There are many reasons, why the modernist idea of the human as a rational, autonomous subject should be overcome. Rosi Braidotti argued that the rational, autonomous human being at the centre of classical humanism was not only rational and autonomous, it was also male and European . Women were excluded from this definition, as were members of non-Western societies and non-human beings. Thus, whenever "us humans" was evoked, everything non-masculine, non-European, non-human was systematically marginalized. Or, as Braidotti put it: "'we' are not one and the same" (Braidotti ).

Instead of such an exclusionary humanism, Braidotti argues for a relational understanding of subjectivity in which humans, animals, technology and the environment are intertwined in manifold ways. She borrows a key term for her post humanist theory of the subject nomadic - from Deleuze and Guattari: "The nomadic subject is a materially embedded and embodied, affective and relational collective assemblage, a relay-point for a web of complex relations that displace the centrality of ego-indexed notions of identity" (Braidotti 2011). FAIF and its concept of subjectivity as a process rejects human exceptionalism and calls for an affirmative ethics of entanglements in which non-human selves and their needs are taken into account.

What a non-anthropocentric ethics of entanglements can look like can be observed in numerous non-European societies. French anthropologist Philippe Descola, for example, vividly describes the collective patterns used by the Achuar, a subgroup of the Jivaro inhabiting the border zone between Peru and Ecuador, to fit the relationships between humans and non-humans in a symbolic order (Descola 2013). The modes of identification Descola distinguishes, provide answers to two seemingly simple questions: Who am I? (respectively: what is a human?), and what is the other? (respectively:  what  are  non-humans?)  As  "a  means  of specifying the properties of existing beings" (ibid), answering these questions allows us to distinguish two modes of existence: an inner level (Descola calls it: interiority) and a physical one ( exteriority, physicality). Interiority refers to "the universal belief that a being possesses characteristics that are internal to it or that take it as their source" - in other words, a kind of (self-)consciousness, self or soul - while physicality refers to "external form, substance, the physiological, perceptive and sensorimotor processes, even a being's constitution and way of acting in the world, insofar as these reflect the influence brought to bear on behavior patterns and a habitus by corporeal humors, diets, anatomical characteristics, and particular modes of reproduction' (ibid).

Contrary to what the humanist view suggests, the threshold between self (or the human) and other (the non-human) is by no means universal. Rather, it depends on who or what is attributed or denied which quality of interiority and physicality. The 'other' thus becomes gradual and relative. Descola calls the ruptures, or rather fracture patterns, that arise in this process of differentiation modes or patterns of worlding.

There are only four of them. When defining what it means to be human, every known human society distinguishes in some way between inner and outer qualities (interiority and physicality). These can be different from or identical to those of other living beings. The possible combinations can be represented in a simple crosstab. Descola has called the resulting "ontologies" or modes of identification animism, totemism, analogism, and naturalism (ibid). In the context of this article, it suffices to take a closer look at two of them: naturalism and animism Table 3.

Things get interesting when we ask what similarities and dissimilarities (and which possibilities of communication) arise in relation to non-human living beings. Modernists, in the tradition of Descartes, would obviously opt for the naturalist scheme. They could argue that although all living beings have a common physical basis, only humans have the ability to think in complex ways, to make moral judgments, and to create culture. Animals allegedly lack this complex interiority. Since they are thus seen as not endowed with consciousness and intentionality, they can be downgraded to

During his two years of fieldwork among the Achuar, Descola noticed that his hosts treated some (but not all!) elements of their environment like persons, 'endowed with cognitive, moral and social qualities analogous to those of humans, thereby making it possible for communication and interaction between classes of beings that at first sight seem very different' (ibid). As for interiority, the Achuar do not assume a fundamental dissimilarity between themselves and members of other Jivaro groups or Europeans and, say, a jaguar or a peccary. If they differ at all, it is in their physicality. The fact that only shamans in trance succeed in bridging the communicative gap between humans and e.g. a jaguar is not due to the fact that jaguars do not have selves, but to the dissimilar 'physiological, perceptive and sensorimotor processes', that shape a being's 'constitution and way of acting in the world' (ibid).

Now, it may be tempting to dismiss the Achuar worldview as just another exotic way of misrepresenting the 'real' relationships between humans and non-humans, between subject and object, between culture and nature. But can we really be sure that our naturalist ideas about the world are 'true' in a non-arbitrary sense of the word? What if our worldview is determined by cognitive schemes that cause a blind spot in our thinking? I have already pointed out the paradox of modernity, the unrealised separation between nature and society, and the dramatic consequences that the constant production of hybrid quasi-objects has for life on our planet. Could it be that the insistence on a naturalist ontology marks precisely such a blind spot?

At first glance, this may sound like some naïve ethnoromanticism. In view of the problems that the Anthropocene poses for life on this planet, however, it would be downright crazy to ignore the opportunity offered by an openness to alternative ontological framings of the world we inhabit. FAIF therefore calls for an open ontological reassessment of worlding options. If the advancements in AI continue at their current pace, we could soon be forced to do so anyway. As of today, most people would probably still be hesitant to say that AI has 'consciousness'. But what if the coming years actually produce an AGI with interiority that is no longer much different from the human? In the cross-tabulation of ontologies, we could suddenly find ourselves in the animistic field, sharing a world with a new form of consciousness from which only our specific corporeality distinguishes us. That is not nothing - but it would no longer align with the belief that humans are the pinnacle of creation.

## 5    FAIF - A Feminist AI Framework

The general idea of a 'human-centered AI' is well-intentioned, but it is based on false premises. HCAI, as currently practised, is Eurocentric and androcentric. It fails to consider the cultural diversity of a globally interconnected world or the multifarious ways in which humans and non-humans have co-evolved over time. It is driven by cold market logic and disregards the detrimental ecological and social effects of an unrestrained accumulation of power and capital. Calls for 'fairer AI' are usually limited to fighting the symptoms of unregulated advancements - what I have called (admittedly somewhat provocatively) the easy problems of AI . The hard problems - thinking in dichotomies, our failed attempts to separate (wo)man and environment, subject and object, nature and culture - remain unaddressed.

Contrary to that, the Feminist AI Framework (FAIF) proposed here expands on the tradition of feminist criticism and focuses particularly on the hard problems. Rejecting human fantasies of superiority and human exceptionalism, it assembles a multitude of perspectives, worldviews, and ontologies around one large table to face the challenges of AI with a sense of 'strong objectivity' (Harding 2024).

AI holds enormous potential for shaping the future in positive ways. Not only the future of humans, but of other living beings as well. Already today, we have a plethora of technological tools at our disposal, which can be used to address both scientific and social issues. From diagnosing diseases to predicting the weather, from protecting the climate to enhanced accessibility, from identifying fake information to studying DNA and creating new types of proteins - in all these cases, AI is already extremely useful and helps us to get to grips with our most pressing problems.

If we do it right, that is. FAIF provides us with an epistemological framework for identifying concrete problems and determining which actors must be considered in addressing them. Its grounding in gender studies helps us detect power relations, racist and sexist structures, and their intersectional interwovenness, facilitating intersectional bias auditing across race, gender, and class dimensions, e.g. in hiring algorithms and healthcare AI. Through critical posthumanism, FAIF questions the centrality of the human and shifts focus to hybrid modes of existence, guiding multispecies AI design for wildlife conservation and sustainable agriculture. With new materialism it emphasizes the agency of non-human actors and demands the abandonment of an exclusively anthropocentric perspective, ensuring material impact assessment for carbon-conscious cloud computing and circular economy systems. Engaging with indigenous ontologies, it challenges the rationalist anthropocentrism of naturalist knowledge production. At the same time, it promotes community control over data in projects that support

things like language preservation and traditional ecological monitoring. With more-than-human anthropology, it advances our understanding of the diversity of trans-species interconnectivities while creating culturally sensitive recommendations. Finally, FAIF's linking to the phenomenology of the living body draws attention to the corporeality of all world experience and 're-grounds' the subject in the physical world. At the same time, it directs our attention to domains that (for the time being) remain closed to the cold rationalist logic of AI: the bodily perception of one's existence and the individual experience (and creation) of a particular 'environment' (Uexküll 2023). This phenomenological dimension ensures that AI development acknowledges the embodied nature of human experience and challenges purely computational approaches to understanding and representing self and reality.

If our goal is indeed a more equitable, inclusive AI, we need to include these approaches in our development processes. FAIF therefore calls for a multi-perspective re-evaluation of the relationship between humans and technology, which asks at each stage of the development process: What does this step imply beyond the logics of "wouldn't it be cool if…" (Hollis 2023) and "move fast and break things" (Baer 2014) Table 4. 7

The mathematician John von Neumann, one of the pioneers of modern computer science, once asked what consequences the development of new technologies will have for humanity. He saw real danger to the continuation of human civilization in the fact that people could simply lose interest in their environment: "The interests of humanity may change, the present curiosities in science may cease, and entirely different things may occupy the human mind in the future" (Usam 1958).. Another mathematician, Michael Atiyah, raised the question of whether the worlds of living beings with different horizons of physical experience at least had a common logical foundation. For a long time, mathematical realism had taken for granted that mathematical truths were universal: "When two dinosaurs met two dinosaurs there were four dinosaurs. In this prehistoric tableau '2 + 2 = 4' was accurately modeled by the beasts, even though they were too stupid to know it and even though no humans were there to observe it" (Gardner 1981). That may be so. But what, Atiyah asks, if higher intelligence had not developed in humans, but in living beings who have completely different bodily experiences and whose lives take place in completely different environments? In a jellyfish, for example, inhabiting the depths of the Pacific Ocean. "Everything in its experience would be continuous, from the flow of the surrounding water to its fluctuating temperature and

pressure. In such an environment, lacking individual objects or indeed anything discrete, would the concept of number arise? If there were nothing to count, would numbers exist?" (Attiyah 1995).

All genuine knowledge of the world is grounded in perception. Living bodies are not vessels containing a mind; they constitute and shape the very environment in which a being moves and which it engages through its actions. While it remains impossible to predict what the environment of an artificial general intelligence might be, one thing is certain: any true AGI will necessarily represent a form of embodied intelligence.

The stakes could not be higher. Are we really prepared to submit to the rules of an artificial general intelligence whose behaviour we cannot even begin to predict? Six and a half years before ChatGPT shattered the world, we got a preview of how it might be to interact with a superior intelligence. On March 10, 2016, with the 37th move in its 4th game against Lee Sedol, one of the world's best Go players, the computer program AlphaGo surprised all experts. The move looked like a mistake. From a human point of view, it made no sense at all. Only in the retrospective analysis of the game did it prove decisive for the triumph of the machine over its human adversary. AlphaGo had independently developed a strategy that its creators could observe but were not able to reconstruct through reverse engineering. It was effective, but it was not transparent.

Already today, artificial intelligences can imitate human behaviour to an extent that was long believed impossible. They win strategy games, trade stocks, compose music in the styles of Bach or Nirvana, create works of art at the touch of a button, write texts, communicate and serve as companions and romantic partners. Hardly anything perceived as genuinely human for thousands of years is exempt - and in many cases, AI-generated results at least equal those of human activity.

After the three narcissistic injuries (Freud 1917) of heliocentrism, evolution, and psychoanalysis, this is yet another blow in the face of human exceptionalism. This can definitely be a positive thing. We should be careful, though. For, regardless of whether singularity is to occur next year or in a few, it presents us with changes that we would better reflect on in advance than post festum like the engineers at AlphaGo. FAIF gives us the means to do this.

The Feminist AI Framework (FAIF) therefore calls for a radical reassessment of the relationship between humans, the environment, and technology. Human existence can no longer serve as the 'natural' centre of all considerations. Rather, it is to be seen as part of a cornucopia of rhizomatic entanglements, in which the agencies of human and nonhuman actors are negotiated and re-negotiated in concrete contexts of action.

The integration of FAIF into AI development and implementation across research institutions, technology companies, and public institutions, as well as into regulatory frameworks such as the EU AI Act, necessitates both innovative governance methodologies and a fundamental cultural transformation in how we conceptualize the relationship between technology, society, and justice.
