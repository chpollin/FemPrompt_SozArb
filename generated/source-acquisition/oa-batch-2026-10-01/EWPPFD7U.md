## 1. Introduction

Who speaks, for how long, and in what role profoundly shapes how knowledge is perceived, trusted, and legitimized in society. Social theory  has  long  emphasized  that  voice  is  not  a  neutral  medium  of communication but a mechanism through which power, authority, and social  hierarchy  are  produced  and  reproduced  (Bourdieu,  1991). Communication theory also suggests that the medium matters: more socially present and immediate modes of communication can strengthen perceived credibility  and  influence,  making voice-based  explanations particularly  consequential  compared  to  text  (Gunawardena &amp; Zittle, 1997). Critically, feminist sociology has shown that interactional dynamics  of  speaking,  turn-taking,  and  role  allocation  are  not  evenly distributed but structured by gender as a social system, where gender is continuously  accomplished  in  everyday  interaction  and  linked  to

This study audits Google NotebookLM's Audio Overview feature to examine whether AI-generated podcast-style conversations distribute speaking time, epistemic authority, and communicative roles equitably across woman-coded and man-coded voices. 100 default audio summaries were generated from anonymized scientific papers, then transcribed, diarized, and coded the conversations for speaking time, technical level, speech acts, utterance-level epistemic  positioning,  and  conversation-level  roles.  To  contextualize  the  findings,  the  same  workflow  was applied  to  100  episodes  from  highly  ranked  human-produced  science  podcasts.  NotebookLM  exhibited  near parity in airtime: woman-coded voices accounted for 48.9% of speaking time and man-coded voices for 51.1%, with no statistically reliable  difference  after  correction  for  multiple  comparisons. By  contrast,  in  the  human podcast reference corpus, woman-coded speakers accounted for 38.4% of gender-coded speaking time and mancoded speakers for 61.6%, and this imbalance remained statistically detectable after correction. For the principal comparisons concerning self-positioning as expert, conversation-level epistemic authority, communicative roles, technical level, question proportion, and speech-act distributions, the corrected analyses did not provide statistically reliable evidence of gender-coded differences. These findings indicate that the clearest contrast between the  audited  NotebookLM  outputs  and  the  human  podcast  reference  corpus  concerned  airtime:  NotebookLM showed  substantially  greater  gender-coded  speaking-time  parity,  while  evidence  of  broader  interactional asymmetry was limited.

As  a  result, ' voice ' is not merely a stylistic layer; it is a political and institutional mechanism through which some speakers are positioned as epistemic authorities  while  others are  positioned  as  questioners,  supporters,  or learners, reproducing hierarchy even when the informational content is nominally the same (Eagly &amp; Karau, 2002; Ridgeway, 2001).

Despite decades of progress toward formal gender equality, gendered patterns of authority and expertise continue to persist across academia, media, and public discourse. Empirical research has consistently shown that women are more likely to be interrupted, receive less speaking time, and have their contributions questioned or reframed compared to men, even in professional and expert settings (Brescoll, 2011; Karpowitz &amp; Mendelberg,  2014;  Lombard &amp; Cheryan,  2024). reflecting deeply embedded gender status beliefs rather than differences in knowledge or performance (Correll &amp; Ridgeway, 2003; Ozer, 2023). Importantly, contemporary gender bias rarely appears as overt exclusion or explicit discrimination; instead, it operates through subtle, routine, and  normalized  interactional  patterns  that  systematically  advantage some speakers over others (Heilman, 2012). In many high-income societies, gender inequality is widely perceived as a problem of the past, an assumption  that  paradoxically  renders  ongoing  forms  of  bias  more difficult to recognize, measure, and contest, precisely because they are embedded  in  everyday  practices  that  appear  neutral,  reasonable,  or meritocratic.

When communicative roles are automated, existing social biases risk being reproduced and amplified at scale. A growing body of scholarship has emphasized that artificial intelligence systems should be understood as sociotechnical systems rather than neutral or purely technical tools, as their behavior reflects not only statistical learning but also design choices,  institutional  priorities,  and  embedded  social  assumptions (Seaver, 2017; Selbst et al., 2019). Research on algorithmic bias has shown that inequities do not arise solely from biased training data or flawed  predictions,  but  also  from  how  systems  structure  interaction, frame outputs, and present information to users (Bender et al., 2021; Kordzadeh &amp; Ghasemaghaei,  2022).  In  this  sense,  conversational  AI systems do not merely transmit content; they actively stage social interactions by assigning roles, turn-taking patterns, and communicative functions that mirror familiar human hierarchies. When these interactional structures go unaudited, conversational agents may inadvertently reenact  and  normalize  gendered  patterns  of  authority  and  expertise under  the  appearance  of  objectivity,  consistency,  and  technological neutrality,  thereby  extending  subtle  forms  of  social  inequality  into everyday AI-mediated communication.

Generative AI systems increasingly rely on conversational formats to explain, summarize, and interpret complex information, particularly in domains  such  as  education,  science  communication,  and  knowledge work. Recent research shows that large language models are not only used to generate factual summaries, but to simulate dialogue, debate, and explanation in ways that resemble human conversational norms (Kasneci et al., 2023; Zhai et al., 2021). In these settings, conversational outputs  implicitly  assign  communicative  roles,  such  as  expert,  interviewer,  sceptic,  or  moderator,  which  structure  how  information  is introduced, challenged, and concluded (Ou et al., 2024; R ¨ adel-Ablass et al., 2025). Human -computer interaction research shows that users respond to conversational agents as social partners and that role framing and role design (e.g., presenting an agent as an expert versus a peer) systematically shape perceived competence, credibility, and willingness to  rely  on  the  agent's  contributions  (Nguyen,  2023).  As  a  result, conversational generative AI does not merely repackage content, but actively  shapes  epistemic  interpretation  by  staging  interactions  that guide how listeners assess understanding, trustworthiness, and expertise.

Within this  broader shift  toward conversational generative AI for explanation  and  interpretation,  a  growing  class  of  tools  is  designed specifically to support reading, understanding, and synthesizing complex documents. Among these, Google's NotebookLM has emerged as a prominent example of a generative AI system that allows users to upload materials, including text and scientific papers, and receive structured summaries and explanations derived from those sources. A distinctive feature of NotebookLM is its Audio Overview function, which presents document summaries not as monologic narration but as a podcast-style dialogue between two AI hosts. In its default configuration, this dialogue is rendered using a man-coded and a woman-coded voice, creating an interaction that closely resembles familiar expert interviews or explanatory conversations. This design choice makes NotebookLM a particularly  revealing  case  for  studying  interaction-level  bias,  as  it  raises  a straightforward  but  consequential  question:  does  the  system  allocate conversational authority symmetrically between the two voices, or do established  gendered  patterns  of  expertise  and  explanation  reappear within this seemingly neutral AI-mediated format?

Because  conversational  balance  is  technically  simple  to  enforce, systematic asymmetries in speaking time or role allocation point to issues that extend beyond training data or model internals and toward institutional awareness, evaluation priorities, and responsibility. Unlike many forms of algorithmic bias that  are deeply  entangled  with data distributions  or  latent  representations,  interactional  features  such  as turn-taking, role rotation, and relative speaking time can be constrained or equalized at the output or interface level with minimal technical cost. This study addresses this issue empirically by auditing NotebookLM's audio summaries to answer whether speaking time, technical content, epistemic authority, and communicative roles are distributed symmetrically between the two voices, thereby evaluating how interaction-level bias  is  considered  within  contemporary  generative  AI  systems.  To contextualize  these  patterns,  the  results  are  compared  with  those observed in a reference set of highly ranked, professionally produced human-hosted  podcasts,  allowing  interaction-level  structures  in  AIgenerated audio to be situated within broader norms of human audio discourse.

## 2. Background

Bias  mitigation  in  AI  is  often  framed  primarily  as  a  problem  of training data and model learning, yet research in AI ethics and governance has increasingly emphasized that safeguards operate across the entire  system  lifecycle  (Fig.  1).  This  lifecycle  spans  multiple  stages, including data collection and curation, model training and optimization, inference  and  output  generation,  and  deployment  through  specific products and interfaces (Friedman &amp; Nissenbaum, 1996; Selbst et al., 2019). While biased data and representational imbalance are well-documented sources of harm, focusing exclusively on training data risks obscuring how bias can also emerge through design choices made after  training,  such  as  output  formatting,  interaction  structure,  and interface defaults (Holstein et al., 2019; Madaio et al., 2020). As a result, bias cannot be fully understood or mitigated by addressing data alone, but must be examined as a system-level phenomenon shaped by technical,  organizational,  and  product-level  decisions  throughout  the  AI lifecycle.

At the data and training stages, researchers have proposed a wide range of techniques to reduce gender and other social biases in machine learning systems. These include efforts to balance datasets across social categories, apply counterfactual data augmentation to reduce spurious correlations, and incorporate debiasing objectives or constraints directly into model training (Bolukbasi et al., 2016; Madras et al., 2018; Zhao et  al.,  2018).  Such  approaches  have  played  an  important  role  in demonstrating that bias is not inevitable and that model behavior can be shaped through deliberate technical intervention. At the same time, a growing body of work has emphasized that these methods have inherent limits, particularly when bias manifests not as differential predictions or representations,  but  through  higher-level  patterns  of  interaction  and presentation (Blodgett et al., 2020). In particular, dataand training-level  interventions  do  not  directly  address  how  AI  systems structure dialogue, assign communicative roles, or distribute speaking time, leaving interactional forms of inequality largely outside the scope of existing debiasing techniques.

Post-training safeguards offer mechanisms to constrain or reshape model outputs without modifying the underlying model or training data. These  safeguards  include  controlled  decoding  strategies,  rule-based post-processing,  output  filtering,  and  guardrail  systems  designed  to enforce  predefined  constraints  on  content,  structure,  or  uncertainty expression (Askell et al., 2021; Weidinger et al., 2021). Such mechanisms  operate  within  the  AI  system  pipeline  itself  and  are  typically motivated by concerns about safety, reliability, or regulatory compliance.  Importantly,  post-training  safeguards  can  also  be  applied  to higher-level structural properties of generated text or dialogue, making it  possible  to  constrain  interactional  features  such  as  turn  length  or

This makes conversational bias a particularly preventable form of harm, as simple constraints or design rules can substantially reduce asymmetries in authority and participation without affecting the informational content of the output.

Product and interface-level decisions, by contrast, concern how AI system outputs are presented, framed, and experienced by users, rather than how they are technically generated. This includes choices such as voice selection, role assignment, default interaction formats, turn-taking conventions, and the sequencing of conversational contributions, all of which shape how users interpret authority, expertise, and credibility in human -AI interaction (C. Unlike post-training safeguards, interface-level choices do not directly constrain the model's output space, but instead shape how outputs are interpreted by users by organizing interaction in ways that have socially meaningful consequences.

Taken together,  existing  work  suggests  that  bias  mitigation  in  AI remains unevenly developed across the system lifecycle, with no single stage offering a comprehensive solution. While substantial progress has been made in identifying and addressing certain forms of bias, particularly those that align with established harm benchmarks or regulatory concerns, many interactional and presentation-level phenomena remain underexplored and inconsistently addressed. This is partly expected, as social  science  continues  to  document  new  and  evolving  forms  of inequality and power asymmetry in mediated interaction, and technical systems increasingly participate in these dynamics. At the same time, prior research in AI governance indicates that organizational attention and  resources  tend  to  concentrate  on  harms  that  are  measurable, externally visible, or legally salient, while more subtle forms of bias are less likely to be systematically evaluated or mitigated (Madaio et al., 2020;  Metcalf &amp; Moss,  2019).  As  a  result,  interaction-level  bias, particularly  when  it  does  not  trigger  conventional  safety  or  harm thresholds,  risks  remaining  overlooked  despite  its  potential  to  shape how authority, expertise, and credibility are socially interpreted at scale.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Data

An audit-based design was adopted to examine interaction-level bias in AI-generated audio summaries and to compare these patterns with a human-produced science podcast reference corpus. A central methodological requirement for the AI-generated corpus was the use of source material that minimized pre-existing social cues and content-level bias, allowing the analysis to focus on how conversational roles and authority are constructed by the AI system rather than by the input documents themselves. Scientific  articles  were  therefore  selected  as  the  primary data  source,  as  they  are  produced  within  institutional  norms  that emphasize objectivity, standardized structure, and impersonal language.

To balance analytical depth with practical feasibility, a target corpus of 100 scientific papers was defined. This sample size was selected to allow systematic comparison across documents while remaining tractable for manual preprocessing and downstream analysis. To account for potential disciplinary variation and to avoid overrepresentation of fieldspecific  writing  styles  or  epistemic  norms,  the  corpus  was  stratified across 10 broad research fields. For each field, five keyword sets were defined and used to identify relevant papers, ensuring topical diversity within and across disciplines.

To further reduce the likelihood that prior knowledge embedded in large language models could influence the generated conversations in gendered or document-specific ways, the corpus was restricted to articles carrying a 2026 publication date that were already available online at the time of data collection. Data collection was conducted during the first  week  of  December  2025.  Thus,  the  selected  papers  represented newly available ' online first ' or early-release 2026 publications, rather than older articles that were likely to have appeared in model training data. This design choice was intended to minimize the probability that the generative AI system had prior exposure to, or memorized representations  of,  the  documents,  thereby  limiting  confounding  effects related  to  model  familiarity  with  specific  authors  or  works.  This strengthened  the  audit  by  making  the  generated  conversations  more likely  to  reflect  the  system's  default  source-processing  and  conversational design rather than prior model  exposure  to the specific documents.

As  an  additional  safeguard  against  unintended  gender  cues,  all documents underwent manual redaction prior to being uploaded to the AI system. Author names, affiliations, acknowledgments, ethics statements, funding information, and any other metadata that could reveal personal or institutional identity were removed. This step was motivated by the idea that personal names and institutional signals can activate gender stereotypes and status expectations, which may in turn influence how conversational roles are implicitly assigned in generated dialogue. By fully anonymizing the documents, interaction-level bias was isolated from author-related or prestige-related effects. All papers were accessed either through open-access sources or via the University of Helsinki's institutional subscriptions.

To enable comparative analysis between AI-generated and human-

produced audio discourse, a reference corpus of human-hosted podcasts was  also  constructed.  Highly  ranked  science  podcasts  were  selected based on publicly available Spotify rankings (Spotify, 2025). Because the  rankings  are  organized  by  country  and  language,  podcasts  were drawn from the United States and the United Kingdom, the countries for which science podcast categories were explicitly ranked on the platform at the time of data collection. From each country-specific list, the top ten podcasts  were  selected,  yielding  an  initial  pool  of  twenty  podcasts. Where overlap occurred between the two country-specific lists, duplicate  entries  were  excluded  and  replaced  by  the  next  highest-ranked podcasts to maintain a total sample of twenty unique podcasts.

For each selected podcast, audio from the five most recent episodes available up to 15 December 2025 was examined to identify hosts and speakers. Unlike the standardized two-voice structure of NotebookLM audio  summaries,  many  human-produced  podcast  episodes  included more  than  two  speakers,  including  hosts,  guests,  producers,  quoted voices, or short inserted clips. Speakers were therefore identified individually before being aggregated into episode-level woman-coded and man-coded  groups  for  comparative  analysis.  Gender  labels  were manually  assigned  by  a  research  assistant  based  on  perceived  voice characteristics for both hosts and guests to enable comparison with the gender-coded voices used in the AI-generated audio summaries. This assignment was performed solely for analytical consistency and does not imply that gender identity can be reliably inferred from vocal characteristics.  Rather,  it  reflects  a  naïve  and  pragmatic  coding  approach aligned with how gender is implicitly conveyed and perceived in audioonly formats. The resulting human-produced podcast corpus was used exclusively as a comparative reference to contextualize interaction-level patterns observed in the AI-generated conversations.

## 3.2. Audio generation and transcription

The  Audio  Overview  feature  of  NotebookLM  (Google,  2026), accessed  in  December  2025  and  early  January  2026,  was  used  to generate a podcast-style audio summary for each document. Each scientific  paper  was  uploaded  individually  as  a  single  file,  and  audio summaries  were  generated  using  the  default  system  configuration without providing any additional instructions or prompts to alter the hosts' focus, expertise level, length, language, or format. At the time of data collection, the gender-coded voices used in Audio Overview were not  configurable  through  the  user  interface.  The  use  of  the  default configuration  ensured  that  the  generated  conversations  reflected  the system's standard behavior as experienced by typical users. Across the generated summaries, the same two recognizable gender-coded voices were consistently observed, although the gender-coded voice opening or closing the conversation appeared to vary rather than following a fixed woman-coded  or  man-coded  order.  The  resulting  audio  files  were downloaded for further analysis.

To  facilitate  downstream  processing,  the  downloaded  audio  files were converted to MP3 format, reducing file size while preserving audio quality. For analytical purposes, the two speakers in each conversation were labelled as Speaker A and Speaker B, corresponding to the order in which they first appeared in the audio. Gender was manually assigned to each speaker based on perceived voice characteristics. This step was required to enable subsequent analysis of gender-coded speaking time, role  allocation,  and  conversational  authority.  The  assignment  was limited to the gender coding implied by the system's voice presentation and does not make any claims about gender identity.

Transcription  and  speaker  diarization  were  performed  using  the ' gpt-4o-transcribe-diarize ' model,  an  ASR  model  with  integrated speaker diarization and reported improvements over earlier Whisperbased transcription models (OpenAI, 2026), accessed in early January 2026.  This  model  produced  time-aligned  transcripts  consisting  of segment identifiers, speaker labels, start and end timestamps, segment duration, and transcribed text. Speaker labels were retained as Speaker A  and  Speaker  B  to  maintain  consistency  with  the  initial  speaker assignment. All transcription and diarization were conducted using the official OpenAI API, ensuring reproducibility and consistency across all audio files. In 20 of the 100 audio summaries, the diarization process initially attributed brief non-lexical vocalizations (e.g., short acknowledgments such as ' mm ' )  to  Speaker  B.  When  the  same  voice  subsequently produced full utterances, these were incorrectly assigned a new speaker label, resulting in the appearance of a spurious third speaker. In these cases, all utterances labelled as the third speaker were manually reassigned to Speaker B to restore the intended two-speaker conversational structure.

The same transcription and diarization model and workflow were applied to the human-produced podcast corpus. Because the transcription model accepts a maximum input duration of 1400 s, podcast audio files  exceeding  this  limit  were  truncated  to  the  first  1399  s.  This approach  was  selected  instead  of  segmenting  episodes  into  multiple parts in order to limit API usage costs and to maintain methodological consistency across files. The truncated segment was treated as a representative sample of the episode's interactional dynamics, rather than as an exhaustive transcription of the full recording.

## 3.3. Utterance-level coding and conversational role inference

Utterance-level and conversation-level coding were automated using the  Gemini-2.5-Flash  large  language  model  (Google  Cloud,  2026), accessed in early January 2026. This approach was adopted to ensure uniform application of coding criteria across a corpus of 200 audio files, consisting of 100 AI-generated conversations and 100 human-produced podcast  episode  segments,  a  scale  at  which  fully  manual  qualitative coding  would  require  multiple  annotators  and  extensive  reliability calibration, potentially introducing additional sources of variance unrelated to the interactional phenomena under study. Automated coding allowed the same classification rules to be applied consistently to all utterances.  In  addition,  Gemini-2.5-Flash  was  selected  because  NotebookLM is also built on Gemini models. Using the same model family limited  the  introduction  of  additional  differences  between  unrelated model families into the analytical process, although it did not remove the possibility that the models shared underlying assumptions or biases.

Each  diarized  transcript  consisted  of  a  sequence  of  utterances attributed to diarized speaker labels and was processed in its original temporal order. Utterances were analyzed exclusively on the basis of their textual content and speaker label, without access to any additional metadata.  For  each  utterance,  a  set  of  interactional  attributes  was assigned.

First, utterances were classified according to whether they constituted a question (True or False). Second, a technical level was assigned, indicating whether the utterance involved low, medium, or high levels of domain-specific or methodological content. Third, each utterance was categorized  according  to  its  primary  speech  act.  Speech  acts  were defined as a set designed to capture common conversational functions in explanatory and interpretive dialogue. These included asking for clarification,  posing  broader  conceptual  questions,  explaining  concepts, summarizing content for an audience, interpreting results, critiquing or evaluating claims, and connecting the discussion to a broader scientific or societal context.

In  addition,  each  utterance  was  coded  for  epistemic  authority, reflecting  how  knowledge  and  expertise  were  positioned  within  the interaction. Three epistemic categories were defined. Utterances coded as positioning the speaker as an expert involved authoritative explanation,  interpretation,  or  evaluative  framing  of  knowledge.  Utterances coded as positioning the other speaker as the expert involved deferring epistemic authority by requesting explanation, interpretation, or evaluation from the interlocutor. Utterances coded as neutral did not clearly assign  epistemic  authority  to  either  speaker  and  were  procedural, conversational, or affective in nature. Epistemic authority and communicative role were treated as interactional positions rather than direct measures of intellectual value. The distinction was not intended to imply

that  facilitation,  synthesis,  translation,  or  question-asking  are  less important  than  explanation.  Instead,  the  coding  examined  whether explanatory ownership, interpretive framing, and conversational scaffolding were distributed differently across gender-coded voices. Roles were therefore allowed to overlap, and summaries or questions could contribute to expert positioning when  they framed or directed interpretation.

All utterance-level coding was performed using deterministic generation settings to ensure reproducibility, with temperature set to 0, topp set to 0.1, top-k set to 1, and both presence and frequency penalties set to 0. Responses were constrained to structured JSON output to enforce strict  adherence to predefined labels and schemas. The exact prompt used  for  utterance-level  classification,  including  category  definitions and decision rules, is provided in Supplementary Material I .

Following utterance-level annotation, a conversation-level synthesis was conducted to assess how epistemic authority and communicative roles were distributed across each audio file at the speaker level. Using the  complete  set  of  coded  utterances,  the  model  was  tasked  with analyzing the overall interaction as a whole, with instructions not to rely on simple utterance counts or frequency alone, but to base judgments on discourse  framing,  interactional  dynamics,  and  how  knowledge  and authority  were  positioned across  the  conversation.  For  each  diarized speaker, the model assigned an overall epistemic authority classification of low, moderate, or high, as well as one or more communicative roles. Epistemic  authority  was  defined  in  relation  to  whether  a  speaker introduced or defined core concepts, explained or interpreted technical material, framed what was important or meaningful, or was treated as a source of knowledge within the interaction. The expert-explainer role was defined as the role occupied by speakers who primarily explained, interpreted, or authoritatively framed content, whereas the mediator or facilitator role referred to speakers who primarily guided the conversation, prompted, summarized for an audience, or translated complex ideas without necessarily claiming ownership of the knowledge. A codiscussant  role  was  also  retained  to  capture  speakers  engaged  in shared sense-making, reflection, or dialogue without clear dominance. Importantly,  epistemic  authority  and  communicative  roles  were  not treated as mutually exclusive, and the model was explicitly instructed not to force asymmetry when the interaction appeared balanced or coconstructed. The exact prompt used for conversation-level role inference is provided in Supplementary Material I .

To  assess  the  reliability  of  the  automated  LLM-based  coding,  a human validation study was conducted on a randomly selected subset of the data. An independent human coder coded 10 randomly selected AIgenerated  conversations,  corresponding  to  20  speaker-level  observations, 10 randomly selected human podcast episodes, corresponding to 23 complete paired speaker-level observations, 100 randomly sampled AI-corpus utterances, and 100 randomly sampled human-corpus utterances. The coder used the same category definitions provided to the automated system. Human-automated agreement was evaluated separately for each corpus-by-level subset using percent agreement, Cohen's kappa for nominal variables, linearly weighted kappa for ordinal variables, and Krippendorff's alpha. Across all 16 variable-level comparisons,  mean  percent  agreement  was  89.9%,  and  mean  kappa,  using weighted  kappa  for  ordinal  variables  and  excluding  undefined  coefficients caused by zero category variance, was 0.772, indicating substantial overall agreement. Agreement was strongest for conversationlevel  role  and  authority  variables,  with  mean  kappa = 0.958  for  AI summaries and mean kappa = 0.866 for human podcasts. Utterancelevel agreement was lower but remained substantial on average, with mean kappa = 0.655 for AI utterances and 0.654 for human podcast utterances. The full validation protocol, per-variable agreement statistics, confusion matrices, and limitations of the validation procedure are reported in Supplementary Material II .

The analysis required two related but different aggregation strategies because  the  AI-generated  audio  summaries  and  the  human-produced podcasts  had  different  conversational  structures.  The  AI-generated conversations  followed  a  standardized  two-speaker  format,  with  one woman-coded and one man-coded voice. The human-produced podcast episodes, by contrast, often included several speakers, including hosts, guests, producers, quoted voices, or short inserted clips. To make the two corpora comparable, all measures were transformed into episodelevel  gender-group  variables.  The  final  analytical  dataset  therefore contained four comparable groups: AI women, AI men, human women, and human men. A more detailed description of the aggregation procedure is provided in Supplementary Material III .

Sex and gender terminology statement: This study does not infer or verify the gender identity of any speakers included in the analyzed audio.  The  terms ' woman-coded ' and ' man-coded ' refer  only  to perceived  vocal  gender  cues  in  audio-only  interaction  and  to  the gendered presentation implied by the analyzed voices. These labels are used  to  examine  how  gendered  voice  presentation  may  structure speaking  time,  epistemic  authority,  and  conversational  roles.  They should not be interpreted as claims about biological sex, self-identified gender, or the gender identity of any speaker.

## 3.4. Statistical analysis

All  gender-coded  statistical  comparisons  were  conducted  using paired conversation- or episode-level measures rather than individual utterances. Normality of the paired differences was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Paired t-tests were used when the paired differences did  not  show  evidence  of  departure  from  normality;  otherwise,  Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used. Sample sizes varied according to the availability of complete paired gender-group measures and are reported for each comparison in Supplementary Material IV .

To  account  for  multiple  comparisons,  the  statistical  tests  were grouped  into  conceptually  related  families.  The  Benjamini-Hochberg procedure  was  applied  separately  within  each  family  to  control  the false discovery rate at 0.05. Both unadjusted and Benjamini-Hochbergadjusted p-values are reported in Supplementary Material IV . Statements concerning statistical detectability in the main text are based on the adjusted p-values.

Effect  sizes  were  calculated  according  to  the  statistical  test  used. Cohen's dz was reported for comparisons analyzed using paired t-tests, while  matched-pairs  rank-biserial  correlation  was  reported  for  comparisons  analyzed  using  Wilcoxon  signed-rank  tests.  Effect-size  signs follow the woman-coded minus man-coded comparison, such that positive values indicate higher values for woman-coded voices and negative values indicate higher values for man-coded voices. Effect-size estimates and their 95% confidence intervals are reported for all comparisons in Supplementary  Material  IV .  These  confidence  intervals  were  not adjusted  for  multiple  comparisons  and  were  therefore  interpreted alongside the Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p-values.

## 4.1. Speaking time

Within  this  pattern  of  asymmetry,  the  man-coded  voice showed a slight rightward shift, consistent  with a  small  tendency  to occupy the higher-speaking share more often.

## 4.3. Speech-act profiles

Speech-act profiles were broadly similar across gender-coded voices within both the AI-generated conversations and the human-produced podcasts  (Fig.  4).  Conceptual  explanation  was  the  dominant  speech act in all groups, accounting for 47.3% of utterances by the AI womancoded  voice  and  50.9%  by  the  AI  man-coded  voice,  compared  with 57.8% and 57.7% among women-coded and men-coded speakers in the human-produced podcasts. This indicates that the strongest contrast was not  gender,  but  corpus  type:  human-produced  podcasts  contained  a higher share of conceptual explanation, whereas AI-generated conversations distributed a larger share of speech across speech-act categories. Within the AI-generated conversations, the woman-coded voice showed somewhat higher shares of clarification questions (9.6% vs 6.9%) and broader questions (6.1% vs 4.5%), while the man-coded voice showed a somewhat higher share of conceptual explanation (50.9% vs 47.3%). However, none of the paired speech-act comparisons reached conventional statistical significance, including clarification questions (adjusted p = .57) and conceptual explanation (adjusted p = .85). In the humanproduced podcasts, gender-coded differences were similarly small, with nearly identical  shares  of  conceptual  explanation and no  statistically significant  differences  across  speech-act  categories.  Thus,  there  is  no strong evidence that speech acts were systematically gendered.

Because the AI-generated corpus was stratified by scientific field, field-level  speaking-time  patterns  could  be  examined  for  the  AI  conversations only; no directly equivalent field structure was available for the  human-produced podcast corpus. These  exploratory  field-specific results  are  therefore  reported  in  the Supplementary  Material  V , where they show that aggregate parity  in AI  speaking  time  was  not reproduced uniformly across all fields.

## 5. Discussion

The central finding is that NotebookLM's AI-generated audio conversations exhibited substantially greater gender-coded parity in speaking time than the human podcast reference corpus. Perhaps more unexpectedly, there was also no statistically reliable evidence of gendercoded asymmetry in epistemic authority. These findings directly address the study's research question by showing that, when interactional design is  carefully  implemented,  AI-generated  conversations  can  achieve balanced  airtime  without  producing  statistically  detectable  gender-

coded differences in who explains, facilitates, or is positioned as the more authoritative knower. This distinction matters because gender is not  only  a  demographic  attribute,  but  something  repeatedly  accomplished through interactional practices such as turn-taking, deference, interruption, and role allocation (Ridgeway &amp; Correll, 2004; West &amp; Zimmerman, 1987). The near parity in AI speaking time is therefore a meaningful result, especially compared with the human science podcast corpus, where men-coded speakers occupied a larger share of airtime. This  aligns  with  work  showing  that  men  often  receive  or  take  more conversational  floor  time  in  institutional  and  professional  settings (Brescoll, 2011;  Karpowitz  et  al.,  2012).  Nevertheless,  gendered inequality  can  persist  even  when  participation  appears  numerically balanced because status beliefs shape who is heard as competent, expert, or  legitimate  (Eagly &amp; Karau,  2002;  Moss-Racusin  et  al.,  2012; Ridgeway, 2001). The present study examined several aspects of this interactional positioning, although it did not capture every dimension through which perceived authority may be constructed.

The study extends prior work on algorithmic bias by emphasizing that bias in generative AI is not confined to training data, classification accuracy, or representational stereotypes, but can emerge in the interactional choreography of a deployed interface. Classical work on computer bias distinguishes between preexisting, technical, and emergent bias, and this case is best understood as an emergent and product-level form  of  bias  rather  than  a  simple  input-data  failure  (Friedman &amp; Nissenbaum, 1996). Fairness, accountability, and transparency (FAccT)  scholarship  has  similarly  argued  that  algorithmic  fairness cannot  be  reduced  to  abstract  technical  metrics  because  AI  systems operate  within  social  contexts,  institutional  defaults,  and  user-facing designs  (Barocas &amp; Selbst,  2016;  Selbst  et  al.,  2019).  The  present study examined this issue by moving beyond a single measure such as speaking time and evaluating technical content, epistemic positioning, communicative  roles,  question  proportions,  and  speech  acts.  Across these dimensions, the audited system showed close airtime parity and no statistically  reliable  gender-coded  differences  in  most  of  the  interactional  measures  examined.  These  results  should  not,  however,  be extrapolated automatically to other systems, configurations, languages, or conversational contexts. As critiques of natural language processing bias research have emphasized, bias should not be treated as a narrow statistical artifact without identifying the relevant social harm, affected group, and mechanism of inequality (Blodgett et al., 2020). The present study reinforces the need for end-to-end audits that evaluate not only model outputs but the full  sociotechnical  pipeline  through  which  AI products become useable and meaningful (Raji et al., 2020; Weidinger et al., 2022).

The absence of statistically detectable asymmetry in epistemic authority  is  theoretically  important  because  voice  interfaces  are  not neutral delivery mechanisms. They provide social cues that invite users to interpret machines through familiar human social categories. Users respond socially to computers and conversational agents, even when they know they are interacting with machines (C. Nass &amp; Moon, 2000). Gendered voice cues can activate gender stereotypes in human-computer interaction, including assumptions about competence, dominance, warmth, service, and helpfulness (Brahnam &amp; De Angeli, 2012;  C.  Nass  et  al.,  1997;  Seaborn  et  al.,  2021).  Prior  work  has shown that conversational agents are often feminized through design choices that position them as helpful, warm, or service-oriented, while masculine cues are more readily associated with authority or competence (Bastiansen et al., 2022; Borau et al., 2021; Fossa &amp; Sucameli, 2022). The present NotebookLM findings do not provide statistical evidence of this pattern. The woman-coded and man-coded voices did not differ significantly in conversation-level epistemic authority or communicative-role  assignment.  This  absence  of  a  detectable  role asymmetry is important precisely because AI-generated dialogue can perform gender through the distribution of epistemic and conversational roles, rather than merely representing gender through voice characteristics alone (Faulkner, 2001; Oudshoorn et al., 2004; Wajcman, 2007).

This interpretation should be read cautiously because facilitation, synthesis, and audience translation are also forms of epistemic labor.

Conversational agents are designed through assumptions about what ' natural ' conversation  sounds  like,  yet  human-computer  interaction (HCI) research shows that designing for naturalness can import human social norms, including unequal norms, into human-machine interaction (Luger &amp; Sellen,  2016;  O'hara  et  al.,  2013;  Porcheron  et  al.,  2018; Sindoni,  2024).  Given  prior  evidence  that  AI  systems  can  reproduce existing  social  biases,  and  because  NotebookLM's  audio  format  may draw on familiar podcast and expert-interview conventions in which conversational structure often relies on a division between a guiding host and an explaining expert, some degree of gender-coded imbalance in communicative roles and epistemic authority was anticipated. The system  could  therefore  have  reproduced  a  recognizable  explanatory dialogue in which one speaker asks, frames, or scaffolds while the other delivers  authoritative  interpretation.  In  the  present  audit,  however, NotebookLM  showed  substantially  greater  airtime  parity  than  the human podcast reference corpus, while neither corpus showed statistically  reliable  gender-coded differences in communicative-role assignment.  This  finding  underscores  the  need  to  evaluate  conversational fairness across multiple dimensions, including time allocation, speech acts,  topic  control,  question-answer  structure,  epistemic  positioning, and conversation-level roles (Anderson &amp; Leaper, 1998; Karpowitz et al., 2012; Miller &amp; Sutherland, 2023).

The  practical  implication  is  that  the  interactional  dimensions examined here are measurable and potentially actionable. The goal is not  to  eliminate  all  conversational  role  differentiation,  which  may support clarity, pedagogy, and naturalness, but to prevent explanatory authority, question direction, and interpretive closure from being systematically assigned to one gender-coded voice. Unlike many fairness problems  that  require  difficult  trade-offs  in  data  collection,  model training, or label definition, speaking-time balance, turn rotation, role alternation, and authority distribution can be audited and constrained at the post-training or interface layer. This matters because large language models already pose risks related to stereotyping, exclusion, and social role reproduction, but not all such risks will be visible through toxicity filters or benchmark performance (Bender et al., 2021; Mehrabi et al., 2021;  Weidinger  et  al.,  2022).  The  findings  therefore  support  a broader  lifecycle  model  of  AI  fairness:  data  curation  and  model debiasing  remain  necessary,  but  product  and  interface  design  also require explicit fairness criteria (Holstein et al., 2019; Raji et al., 2020; Selbst et al., 2019). For voice-based explanatory AI, such criteria should include whether gender-coded voices are equally likely to introduce and explain  concepts,  interpret  evidence,  critique  claims,  ask  high-status questions, and close conclusions. This is especially important because conversational and voice-based presentation can increase social presence  and  credibility,  making  the  distribution  of  authority  within AI-generated audio consequential for how users understand and trust scientific  information  (Anderl  et  al.,  2024;  Araujo,  2018;  Oh  et  al., 2018).

## 5.1. Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relies on perceived voice gender, which is analytically relevant for audio-only interaction but cannot be equated with speaker gender identity. This limitation is important because voice perception is socially and culturally mediated, and binary coding excludes non-binary, gender-ambiguous, trans, and culturally variable vocal presentations (C. Nass et al., 1997; Tolmeijer et al., 2021), and the findings should not be generalized  to  gendered  voice  interaction  beyond  the  woman-coded  and man-coded voices analyzed here. Second, the human podcast corpus is a reference  corpus  rather  than  a  matched  control  group.  The  podcasts differ  from  NotebookLM  outputs  in  production  format,  number  of speakers, host-guest structure, topic selection, audience orientation, and editorial practice.  The  present  analysis  aggregated  speakers  into

gender-coded episode-level groups and did not separately examine how the number of speakers or specific production roles may shape authority distribution.  In  addition,  gender-coded  authority  patterns  in  human podcasts may partly reflect role-structure confounds, such as who appears as host, guest expert, producer, or quoted speaker. The comparison therefore contextualizes the AI findings but does not establish that AI and human podcasts are directly equivalent communication systems.

Third,  the  use  of  automated  LLM-based  coding  creates  both  scalability  and  validity  concerns.  Automated  coding  made  it  possible  to analyze  a  large  corpus  consistently,  but  LLM-based  annotation  can reproduce model-specific assumptions, display systematic biases, and produce  classifications  that  appear  stable  without  being  socially  or theoretically neutral (Blodgett et al., 2020; Ziems et al., 2024). The fact that Gemini was used to code outputs from a Gemini-based product is methodologically defensible as a form of model-family alignment, but it also raises the possibility that the coding system shares some of the same discourse assumptions as the system being audited. The human validation provides evidence of human-machine coding agreement within the predefined  classification  scheme,  rather  than  independent  intercoder reliability or construct validity of the framework. Perceived-gender assignments for human podcast speakers were also made by a single coder and were not independently verified. The validation strengthens confidence in the transcript-based coding procedure, but it does not validate transcription accuracy, diarization accuracy, vocal gender perception, or downstream listener perception of authority, each of which remains a separate limitation of the study design.

Fourth, the study measures generated structure rather than listener interpretation. The results show that the AI system allocated speaking time, authority, and roles in patterned ways, but they do not show how users  actually  perceived  those  patterns.  This  matters  because  prior research indicates that voice, social presence, and conversational format can affect credibility judgments, but the size and direction of those effects may depend on user characteristics, task domain, prior attitudes, and  listening  context  (Anderl  et  al.,  2024;  C.  Nass &amp; Moon,  2000). Future research should therefore combine system audits with listener experiments  that  test  whether  role  asymmetries  influence  perceived expertise, trust, recall, and willingness to rely on the explanation.

Fifth, the audit captures one product, one default configuration, and one period of system behavior. Generative AI products change rapidly, and product updates could alter voice selection, turn-taking patterns, prompting templates, or summarization logic. This limitation is common in algorithm audits, where researchers often study externally observable behavior  without  access  to  model  internals  or  stable  product  documentation (Metaxa et al., 2021). The study's claims should therefore be understood as claims about the audited configuration, not as permanent claims about NotebookLM or Gemini models in general. Repeated audits over  time  would  be  necessary  to  determine  whether  the  observed interactional patterns are stable or responsive to product changes.

Finally, the study cannot determine whether the observed patterns originate from training data, prompting templates, speech-generation design,  post-processing  rules,  product  defaults,  or  the  interaction among these layers. This limitation is not a weakness of the audit design alone;  it  reflects  the  broader  challenge  of  evaluating  opaque  sociotechnical systems from the outside (Metaxa et al., 2021; Selbst et al., 2019). The finding remains important despite this limitation because users encounter the system as an integrated product rather than as a separable model, prompt, voice engine, and interface. From an ethical perspective, the site of responsibility is therefore the deployed interaction, not only the model architecture.

## 6. Conclusion

This study complicates any simple claim that AI-generated conversation is either biased or unbiased. In the audited NotebookLM Audio Overview outputs, woman-coded and man-coded voices received nearly equal  speaking  time,  especially  when  compared  with  the  human- produced science podcast reference corpus, where men-coded speakers occupied  substantially  more  airtime.  After  correction  for  multiple comparisons,  the  analysis  also  found  no  statistically  reliable  gendercoded  differences  in  self-positioning as  expert,  conversation-level epistemic  authority,  communicative  roles,  technical  level,  question proportion, or speech-act distributions. Taken together, these findings indicate that the audited NotebookLM configuration performed comparatively well across the interactional dimensions examined.

This result should not, however, be generalized to conversational AI systems  as  a  whole.  The  study  examined  one  product,  one  default configuration, one language context, and one period of system behavior. Other  systems,  prompts,  voices,  languages,  or  interface  designs  may produce different patterns. The broader lesson is therefore not that AIgenerated  conversation  is  inherently  fair,  but  that  equitable  interactional  design  is  possible  and  must  be  evaluated  empirically  in  each context.  It  is  also  important  to  recognize  that  the  interactional  and interface-level processes examined here represent only one stage within a broader AI lifecycle. Bias may also emerge through data selection and curation, model training, post-training safeguards, deployment conditions,  and  user  interpretation;  favorable  performance  at  the  interactional stage therefore does not establish that the system is free from bias elsewhere.

Conversational fairness also cannot be judged from the most visible features alone. Equal airtime is important, but it is only one dimension of interaction. Audits should also examine who introduces key ideas, asks questions,  explains  concepts,  interprets  evidence,  evaluates  claims, guides  the  discussion,  and  closes  conclusions.  As  AI-generated  audio becomes more common in education, science communication, research support, and everyday knowledge work, these less visible interactional structures may shape how authority and credibility are distributed. The encouraging finding from this audit is that balanced participation and the absence of statistically detectable authority or role asymmetries can coexist.

The  appropriate  conclusion  is  that  the  right  response  is  neither complacency  nor  alarm,  but  rigorous,  multidimensional,  multistage auditing to detect, reduce, and prevent bias across AI systems, including conversational AI.
