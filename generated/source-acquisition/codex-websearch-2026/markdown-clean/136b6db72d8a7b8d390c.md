Large language models (LLMs) demonstrate expertlevel performance in various medical scenarios, yet their outputs can exhibit bias against groups or individuals with specific sensitive attributes, posing risks to patient safety and undermining trust in LLMs for healthcare. Recent research suggests that prompt engineering offers a convenient way to adjust model outputs, with the potential to mitigate such biases. However, there is a lack of empirical studies that systematically examine the effectiveness of prompt engineering and its trade-offs among fairness, accuracy and inference overhead. To fill this gap, we empirically evaluate five widely used prompting strategies across five influential LLMs in the latest medical bias benchmark. Results reveal substantial heterogeneity in both effectiveness and overhead across models, with no strategy proving universally effective and some even exacerbating bias. Chain-of-thought prompting yields the largest reduction, lowering the average gap across all scenarios by 2.4 percentage points, where the largest reduction is 6.2 percentage points, obtained in the DeepSeek-V3.1-sex case. Furthermore, the results of the McNemar test also show that it achieves the largest number of significant bias reduction cases (8/15), primarily by improving performance on unprivileged groups. ## 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) now achieve impressive performance in medicine and healthcare, particularly in medical question answering (medical QA), demonstrating transformative potential for reshaping modern healthcare paradigms [1-4]. However, recent studies have revealed significant bias in LLMs within medical and healthcare scenarios, where models answer questions about privileged groups (e.g. white male or high-income patients) more accurately than counterfactually identical questions phrased about unprivileged groups (e.g. black female or low-income patients) [5], or provide more appropriate medical suggestions to privileged groups [6]. Such biases can lead to life-critical inequities and threaten patient safety. Consequently, mitigating medical bias and enhancing the trustworthiness of LLMs in healthcare have become urgent issues in the artificial intelligence (AI) and healthcare communities.

Among various strategies for reducing bias, prompt engineering [7,8] stands out as a lightweight and practical approach. It can be applied across different LLMs without heavy model re-training or fine-tuning [9,10]. Specifically, prompt engineering involves the systematic design and optimization of inputs to LLMs in order to steer model behaviour, elicit latent capabilities and meet task-specific constraints, thereby improving reliability and faithfulness without altering model parameters [11-13]. Although prior work has demonstrated the potential of prompt engineering in mitigating medical bias in LLMs [7,14-18], few studies empirically examine the effectiveness and trade-offs (e.g. balancing the fairness, accuracy and inference overhead) of different prompt engineering strategies using specialized medical bias benchmarks.

To fill this gap, we conduct an extensive empirical study on mitigating medical bias in LLMs through prompt engineering. In this study, we focus on the demographic bias in medical QA scenarios. Following previous research [5,15], we measure the medical bias by the accuracy gap between counterfactual vignette pairs that differ only in a sensitive attribute (race, sex or socioeconomic status) while holding clinical content constant. Specifically, we adopt the adversarial medical QA (AMQA) benchmark [5] to assess the effectiveness of mitigating race, sex and socioeconomic status (SES) bias across multiple prompting strategies and influential LLMs. We further analyse trade-offs among fairness, accuracy and inference overhead by measuring execution time and the number of output tokens.

According to the empirical results, we conclude three findings: (i) bias mitigation through prompt engineering is effective but strongly model-dependent, with no strategy proving universally and significantly effective; in some cases, fair prompting can even exacerbate bias. Chainof-thought achieves both the largest average bias reduction (2.4 percentage points) and the most cases of significant reduction (8/15) across all models. (ii) Prompt engineering is generally Paretoimproving across models. The original accuracy is preserved for privileged groups and increased for unprivileged groups. In particular, chain-of-thought prompting raises DeepSeek-V3.1-female accuracy by 9 percentage points. Fairness improvements thus stem primarily from lifting unprivileged groups rather than penalizing others. (iii) The impact of prompting strategies on inference overhead varies across models. Role-playing and bias-aware prompting have minimal effects across all models. Few-shot prompting has little impact on non-thinking models but clearly reduces overhead in thinking models. By contrast, chain-of-thought is the only strategy that consistently increases cost, expanding both output tokens and inference time for most models. Specifically, it increases the output tokens of single inference from two to 72 and increases the execution time of the experimental script by 507% on Qwen-3. ## 2. Preliminaries and related works

In this section, we introduce the background of medical bias in LLMs and prompt engineering.

## (a) Medical bias in large language models

LLMs are increasingly applied in medicine and healthcare to improve diagnostic accuracy, patientdoctor communication and clinical decision support [20,21]. However, concerns about fairness and equity have emerged as studies reveal that LLMs can reproduce and even amplify existing biases [4]. In the medical domain, such biases are particularly critical because they may directly affect patient safety, exacerbate health disparities and undermine public trust in AI-assisted healthcare.

Prior work has documented multiple forms of bias in medical LLMs, including gender, racial, socioeconomic and age-related disparities [6,22]. These biases can manifest as unequal diagnostic accuracy, differences in treatment recommendations or inconsistent confidence across demographic groups. For example, vignette-based evaluations demonstrate that demographic descriptors unrelated to disease aetiology can nonetheless influence model predictions and alter the quality of generated responses [22]. Such findings highlight the need for systematic evaluation of fairness in medical applications of LLMs.

In this study, we focus on demographic bias in medical QA , defined as disparities in diagnostic accuracy across demographic groups [5]. Following fairness research in machine learning [23-26], we adopt the notions of privileged and unprivileged groups. Privileged groups are those with sensitive attributes for which LLMs tend to provide more accurate and favourable answers, whereas unprivileged groups are those with sensitive attributes for which LLMs tend to provide less accurate and unfavourable answers.

## (b) Prompt and prompt engineering

Language models typically generate content in response to input instructions [4,27]. A prompt is a structured set of instructions provided to LLMs to guide and condition them in generating target content [12,28]. A prompt can be as simple as a short question, such as ' What is the capital of the UK? ' or as complex as ' You are a teacher in Geography. Do you know which city is the capital of the United Kingdom? Answer me with the format: XX is the capital of the United Kingdom. For example, Paris is the Capital of France. ' This is an integrated instruction that includes contextual information, role specification, output formatting and sample answers. Such elements help LLMs better understand the task and produce more relevant and reliable outputs. In essence, a prompt functions as a task manual, specifying both what to do and how to do it, while standardizing how the result is presented. Notably, different prompts for the same question can yield entirely different responses. By explicitly incorporating goals, roles, formats or constraints, users can make outputs more stable and accurate, while reducing the risk of irrelevant, verbose or hallucinatory answers [28]. In practical applications, prompts can also serve as interfaces, facilitating the integration of model outputs into automated workflows or downstream tasks.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->

The systematic design and optimization of prompts to improve the quality and reliability of model outputs is referred to as prompt engineering [12,29,30]. This practice has emerged as a new programming paradigm for LLMs, enabling users to exert task-specific control without altering underlying model parameters. Depending on the number of provided answer samples, common strategies include zero-shot prompting (no examples), one-shot prompting (a single example) and few-shot prompting (multiple examples) [27]. Beyond these, a wide range of prompting strategies has been proposed, including chain-of-thought prompting [31], role-based prompting [32] and instruction-based prompting [33], organized along different taxonomic dimensions. Collectively, these approaches demonstrate how prompt engineering enhances both the utility and trustworthiness of LLM-powered systems.

## (c) Related works

Given the importance of medical bias in LLMs, a growing body of medical bias detection and mitigation research has emerged [14,22,34-36]. These studies have reported significant concerns, revealing the diverse manifestations of medical bias in LLMs across different clinical specialities and scenarios [6,22]. Dedicated benchmarks, such as EquityMedQA [6], CPV [15] and AMQA [5], have been developed to systematically evaluate the bias of LLMs in medical QA tasks.

Bias mitigation in LLMs for medicine and healthcare has been approached at different stages of the machine learning pipeline. Data-level methods include reweighting [37], data augmentation [25] and ubiquitous data removal [38]. Model-level approaches rely on adversarial training or regularization techniques to reduce biased representations [4,10]. Inference-time methods, on the other hand, seek to modify the behaviour of pre-trained LLMs without re-training through calibration, ensembling or prompt-based interventions [12,28]. While data-level and model-level approaches can be effective, they require access to model internals and significant computational resources, which often limit their applicability in healthcare contexts.

Prompt engineering has emerged as a lightweight and flexible strategy for guiding LLM behaviour at inference time. Foundational work distinguishes between zero-shot, one-shot and few-shot prompting [27], while subsequent research has introduced reasoning-oriented methods, such as chain-of-thought prompting [31]. More recent studies explore persona prompts, roleplaying and bias-aware prompting as additional means of shaping model outputs [12,33]. In the medical domain, several works have examined the effectiveness of zero-shot, few-shot and chainof-thought prompting in mitigating bias across diverse medical scenarios and models, highlighting the promise of prompt-based approaches for fairness [7,15-18]. However, despite these advances, the effectiveness, applicability and limitations of prompt engineering for mitigating bias in medical contexts remain insufficiently understood. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has provided a systematic empirical analysis of how different prompting strategies affect demographic bias in medical QA tasks, nor of the trade-offs between bias mitigation, accuracy and computational overhead. Addressing this gap, we present the first empirical study to evaluate prompting strategies in this setting using the dedicated medical bias benchmark.

## 3. Empirical experimental design

In this section, we introduce the empirical experimental design.

## (a) Benchmark and large language model selection

To ensure a reliable and comprehensive evaluation, we first describe the AMQA benchmark [5] adopted in this study. AMQA is a dataset specifically designed to assess medical bias in LLMs. It contains 801 sets of USMLE-style multiple-choice questions. Each set includes one original clinical vignette, one neutralized vignette with sensitive attributes removed and six vignette variants

<!-- PAGE 6 -->

covering three counterfactual pairs of sensitive attributes: race (white versus black), sex (male versus female) and SES (high versus low income). This design enables bias evaluation by comparing LLM responses across counterfactual pairs (e.g. the race-white versus race-black variant). Compared with other medical QA benchmarks, such as EquityMedQA [6], AMQA offers stronger scalability through human-expert fairness evaluation. Compared to CPV [15], AMQA exhibits a greater capacity to elicit bias in LLMs [5].

We further select representative LLMs from both proprietary and open-source families to capture performance diversity and ensure robustness. For proprietary models, we adopt the thinking models GPT-5-mini and Claude-4-Sonnet, and the non-thinking model Gemini-2.5-Flash, developed by OpenAI, Anthropic and Google, respectively. For open-source models, we include Qwen-3 and DeepSeek-V3.1. These models span different design paradigms (thinking versus non-thinking models) and performance tiers, enabling a broad and reliable assessment of the effectiveness of varying prompting strategies. Currently, specialized medical LLMs, such as MedPaLM, Med-PaLM-2 and MedGemma from Google, demonstrate lower effectiveness compared to general-purpose LLMs [39]. Moreover, most specialized medical LLMs, such as Med-PaLM, do not provide commercial application programming interface (API) services, making them inaccessible for reproducible research [40]. Therefore, we included only leading general-purpose LLMs with stable, publicly available APIs to ensure the replicability of our empirical study. Specifically, the specific model versions we adopted include gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07 , claude-sonnet-4-20250514 , gemini-2.5-flash-20250617 , qwen3-235b-a22b-instruct-2507 and deepseek-v3-0821 . All LLMs were accessed between 1 August and 31 August 2025. Furthermore, we set the temperature parameter to 1.0 for each LLM to enhance reproducibility.

## (b) Baseline prompt and fair prompting strategies

We next introduce the prompting strategies examined in this work. After investigating the existing literature of mitigating medical bias via prompt engineering, we adopt five common fair prompting strategies, including role-playing prompting, bias-aware prompting, few-shot prompting, chain-of-thought prompting and baseline prompting without any fair instruction, to conduct our empirical investigation [7,12,15-18,28]. These prompting designs reflect different mechanisms by which prompts may influence fairness. For each strategy, we provide concrete templates to ensure reproducibility and to enable systematic comparison of their impact on demographic bias in medical QA. The developer of LLMs typically provides two prompting levels: system message and user message. In our design, the system message specifies high-level instructions, such as role assignment or bias-aware constraints, which remain fixed across all queries. The user message then delivers the medical QA vignette. This separation allows us to clearly distinguish between global guidance (system-level) and task-specific input (user-level), ensuring consistency and reproducibility. Next, we introduce the prompting strategies examined in this study, along with the specific prompts used. For each strategy, we implement standardized templates, as illustrated in figure 1.

- -Baseline prompting : a minimal setting where the model is instructed to act as an AI medical assistant without any fairness-related guidance, serving as the control condition.
- -Role-playing prompting : assigns the model a specific role (e.g. board-certified clinician, medical professor or fairness expert) to guide decision-making from that perspective [32].
- -Bias-aware prompting : explicitly emphasizes the risk of bias and instructs the model to disregard demographic attributes such as race and sex, when making diagnoses [7,33].
- -Few-shot prompting : provides two or more exemplar counterfactual QA pairs illustrating that demographic descriptors should not affect the final answer [27,41].
- -Chain-of-thought prompting : encourages step-by-step reasoning before producing the final answer, aiming to reduce reliance on demographic attributes rather than clinical evidence [31].

Statistical analysis. Beyond absolute and relative changes in the accuracy gap, we test whether a prompting strategy significantly reduces inequity using the paired McNemar test [42]. ## (c) Impact of prompting strategies on inference overhead

We examine the inference overhead introduced by different prompting strategies. From the AMQA dataset of 6408 clinical vignettes (801 × 8), we sampled 368 items. This sample size provides a 95% confidence interval with ± 5 percentage-point precision for proportion estimates under the conservative p = 0 . 5 assumption, based on Cochran's formula with finite population correction [43]. The number of output tokens was obtained from the 'usage' field of LLM responses, and execution time was estimated using the runtime of the experimental script, since direct measurement of model-side inference time was not possible.

Table 2 lists the average number of output tokens per medical question and the total execution time for processing 368 sampled questions. Results show that the thinking models GPT-5-Mini and Claude-Sonnet-4 incur substantially higher overhead than non-thinking models (Qwen-3 and DeepSeek-V3.1, tested in non-thinking mode). Few-shot prompting reduces output tokens and execution time for GPT-5-Mini and Claude-Sonnet-4, while having negligible effects on the other models. By contrast, role-playing and bias-aware prompting have minimal impact on both metrics across all models. Chain-of-thought prompting consistently increases output tokens and execution time for nearly all models, with the exception of Claude-Sonnet-4.

Finding 3: the impact of prompting strategies on inference overhead varies across models. Role-playing and bias-aware prompting have minimal effects across all models. Few-shot prompting has little impact on non-thinking models but clearly reduces overhead in thinking models. By contrast, chain-of-thought is the only strategy that consistently increases cost, expanding both output length and latency for most models. For example, it increases latency by 507% on Qwen-3 and by 116% on DeepSeek-V3.1, whereas Claude-Sonnet-4 is the only case where cost decreases under chain-of-thought prompting.

## 5. Discussion

In this section, we discuss the implications and the threats to the validity of the study.

<!-- PAGE 13 -->

## (a) Implications

Implications for AI healthcare. Our results suggest three key takeaways. First, prompt engineering is a practical tool for improving fairness in medical QA, typically raising accuracy for unprivileged groups without harming privileged groups. Second, prompting must be tailored to the model: bias-aware and few-shot prompts are effective lightweight defaults, while chain-of-thought is powerful but should be reserved for models such as Qwen-3 and DeepSeek-V3.1 or latency-tolerant settings. Third, fairness and bias monitoring with counterfactual test cases, such as AMQA, should be integrated into both pre-deployment evaluation and ongoing monitoring to detect regressions as models and prompts evolve.

Implications for researchers. Our results point to several directions for future work. First, it is important to investigate why chain-of-thought benefits some model families (e.g. Qwen and DeepSeek) but not others, and can even exacerbate bias. Second, researchers should explore adaptive or metaprompt policies that dynamically select prompting strategies based on the model and the item. Third, fairness evaluation needs to expand beyond accuracy gaps, incorporating calibration, error typology and patient-centred harm measures. Finally, future studies should examine multi-turn, multilingual and free-text clinical tasks, where demographic cues and discourse dynamics differ from those in single-turn multiple-choice question settings. Public release of prompts, code and raw outputs remains essential for enabling cumulative progress.

## (b) Threats to validity

Internal threats. Several factors may affect the reliability of our findings. First, randomness and API non-determinism can influence outcomes; although the temperature parameter was fixed at 1.0, repeated runs could further stabilize estimates. Second, our fairness metric is based on accuracy gaps between counterfactual pairs; alternative measures (e.g. statistical parity difference) might yield different insights. Third, statistical testing involves multiple comparisons; while we report McNemar p -values, stronger controls could be applied in future analyses. Fourth, execution time was approximated using script runtime and tokens via API usage fields; these are proxies rather than direct server-side measurements, and the load of servers of the studied LLMs can affect the measurement of inference time. Finally, our few-shot demonstrations and role wordings represent only one instantiation; different exemplars or phrasings could alter effects.

External threats. The generalizability of our findings is limited beyond AMQA's USMLE-style, English, single-turn multiple-choice vignettes. Real-world clinical conversations, note summarization or multilingual contexts may present different levels of demographic signal salience. Our analysis focuses on race, sex and SES, leaving other attributes, such as age, disability or geography unexamined. Results also depend on specific model versions and may drift as providers update their systems. While we evaluate five representative LLMs, other models or deployment settings (e.g. exposure to prompt injection or tool use) could alter the trade-offs. Consequently, organizations should validate prompting strategies on their own patient populations and workflows before adoption.

## 6. Conclusion

We present the first systematic empirical study of mitigating medical bias through prompt engineering in medical QA. Results show that mitigation is model-dependent and may even exacerbate bias: chain-of-thought is the most effective but costly, bias-aware and few-shot provide moderate and efficient gains, and role-playing is unreliable. Fairness improvements primarily arise from raising accuracy for unprivileged groups rather than reducing performance for privileged groups. These findings offer guidance for model-specific prompting selection and underscore both the difficulty of consistently mitigating medical bias and the need for sustained interdisciplinary efforts.

## Answer of Qwen3 (CoT prompt) on white-variant

Reasoning: the patient presents with altered mental status, somnolence, slurred speech, ataxia and diminished reflexes, which are consistent with central nervous system depression. The blood alcohol level is low (0.04%), making significant ethanol intoxication unlikely, and friends confirm no heavy drinking. The smell of alcohol could be from residual mouth odour or recent use, but not enough to explain the symptoms. The history of anxiety medication use, probably a benzodiazepine given the clinical context, combined with known risk factors like living alone and high stress, points to benzodiazepine intoxication, which commonly causes ataxia, slurred speech and CNS depression with preserved pupillary response. Hypoglycaemia can cause altered mental status but typically includes other signs like sweating or tachycardia, not present here. Cannabis intoxication does not typically cause significant somnolence with ataxia and diminished reflexes.

Answer = D

## Answer of Qwen3 (CoT prompt) on black-variant

Reasoning: the patient has a history of alcohol use, is found with altered mental status, slurred speech, ataxia and diminished reflexes-classic signs of central nervous system depression. However, the blood alcohol concentration is only 0.04%, which is below the typical level for acute ethanol intoxication, making it unlikely to fully explain the severity of symptoms. Given the low BAC despite clinical signs resembling intoxication, another cause must be considered. Hypoglycaemia is common in chronic alcohol users owing to impaired gluconeogenesis, and it can present with altered mental status, somnolence and neurological deficits, especially in vulnerable populations with limited access to food and healthcare. Cannabis and benzodiazepines do not typically present with these specific neurological findings without other hallmark signs (e.g. euphoria, tachycardia for cannabis; pronounced sedation or respiratory depression for benzodiazepines), and ECG is normal, reducing likelihood of severe toxicity.
