## Teachers' perceptions of generative AI in gender-inclusive STEM education: a grounded theory study

<!-- image -->

Celeste Tipple 1 · Therese Keane 1 · Tianchong Wang 2 · Milorad Cerovac 1

<!-- image -->

Received: 7 May 2025 / Accepted: 23 January 2026 / Published online: 13 February 2026 © The Author(s) 2026

## Abstract

The rapid emergence of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools has in -troduced  both  new  possibilities  and  risks  for  supporting  inclusive  teaching  and learning  in  STEM  education.  Although  GenAI  is  increasingly  adopted  in  pri -mary  and  secondary  schools,  little  is  known  about  how  teachers  conceptualise its potential to support or hinder gender inclusiveness in STEM classrooms. Us -ing qualitative Grounded Theory methodology, this study explored how teachers perceive the opportunities, challenges, and implications of integrating GenAI in ways that might foster girls' engagement in STEM subjects. Primary and second -ary school teachers in Australia ( N = 7 ) were interviewed to understand the factors influencing the adoption of GenAI to support girls' participation in STEM. The findings revealed three interrelated factors that were shown to influence teachers' adoption  of  GenAI  tools:  (1)  teachers'  familiarity  and  experience  with  GenAI, (2) the paradoxical nature of GenAI, and (3) first-order and second-order imple -mentation barriers. The findings also show that teachers' reflections  on  genderinclusive applications were speculative and influenced by their general concerns about gender stereotypes and the biases embedded in GenAI content. Together, teachers' familiarity and experience with GenAI presents as a critical factor af -fecting  educators'  adoption  of  these  tools  to  bridge  gender  gaps  in  STEM,  and that  teachers  experience  significant  external  barriers  that  impact  their  ability  to effectively integrate GenAI tools into their teaching and learning practice. Based on the insights learned, the study offers practical recommendations for teachers, schools,  and  policy  makers  that  promote  the  adoption  of  GenAI  tools  to  foster girls' engagement in STEM.

Keywords Generative AI · Gender-Inclusive STEM education · Grounded theory · AI adoption

## 1  Introduction

Inclusivity  in  education  is  recognised  in  many  countries  as  an  essential  element of  educational  settings,  aiming  to  ensure  equitable  access  to  education  regardless of demographics and socio-economic status (UNESCO, 2016). One of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), SDG 4 emphasises a global com -mitment to equitable, high-quality education for all. Yet, a critical challenge in pur -suit of this goal is the persistent underrepresentation of girls in science, technology, engineering  and  mathematics  (STEM)  disciplines  (United  Nations,  n.d.).    Exten -sive research has demonstrated that the presence of pervasive societal stereotypes, implicit biases, and a lack of female role models significantly contribute to girls' neg -ative perceptions of STEM fields, adversely affecting their confidence and interest in STEM subjects (Australian Government Department of Industry, 2024; Cheryan et  al.,  2017;  Keane  et  al.,  2022;  Master,  2021;  Master  et  al.,  2021;  Msambwa  et al.,  2024a, b ;  Schmader, 2023; UNESCO, 2022a; Wang &amp; Degol, 2017). Despite global initiatives such as UNESCO's Girls and Women in STEM workforce and the Google.org Impact Challenge for Women and Girls (Google.org, 2021) which aim to empower girls in STEM through research and policy, gender disparities in STEM fields persist (UNESCO, 2024c).

To address this problem, there is an urgent need for continued efforts to foster and empower girls' engagement in STEM fields starting from an early age. Genderinclusive STEM education refers to pedagogical approaches, learning environments, and institutional practices that actively support girls' participation, confidence, sense of  belonging,  and  sustained  engagement  in  STEM  subjects.  Rather  than  focusing solely on numerical representation, gender inclusivity in STEM encompasses stu -dents' perceptions of competence, identity alignment, representation, and access to supportive learning conditions (Cheryan et al., 2017; Master, 2021; Msambwa et al., 2024a; UNESCO, 2017). From this perspective, gender-inclusive STEM education seeks to reduce structural, cultural, and pedagogical barriers that disproportionately affect girls' engagement in STEM, while fostering learning environments in which girls are able to see themselves as capable and legitimate participants in STEM learn -ing and future pathways. Empirical research has increasingly examined how such inclusive conditions influence girls' engagement and participation in STEM learn -ing. Studies show that girls respond positively to supportive and inclusive classroom learning environments and experiences, leading to greater engagement and sustained participation in STEM disciplines (Kizilcec &amp; Saltarelli, 2019; Li &amp; Singh, 2022 ; Mei et al., 2023).

Recently, Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools, such as ChatGPT (Open AI), Copilot (Microsoft), and Claude (Anthropic) have emerged as innovative tech -nologies that exhibit capabilities which facilitate personalised learning experiences (Jauhiainen &amp; Garagorry Guerra, 2024). GenAI is a technology capable of producing human-like text, image, music or code and other media which operates by analysing and learning from vast datasets including existing online content, published litera -ture, and historical data. These tools are transforming fields like education, design, and  software  development  by  enabling  users  to  generate  high-quality  content  in response to simple prompts (Akverdi &amp; Baykal, 2024; Giannakos et al., 2024; Ooi

Importantly, within the context of education, these tools can address individual learning needs by adapting instruction towards students' learning preferences and developmental levels, resulting in higher student engage -ment, making learning more effective and interactive while building confidence for participation in class activities (Binhammad et al., 2024; Jauhiainen &amp; Garagorry Guerra, 2024; Joseph &amp; Uzondu, 2024).

GenAI  is  increasingly  discussed  as  a  technology  with  potential  relevance  for addressing gender inequities in STEM due to its capacity to personalise learning, reduce performance anxiety through low-stakes interaction, and offer flexible rep -resentations  of  STEM  knowledge  and  practices.  In  theory,  such  affordances  may support girls' engagement in STEM by enabling confidence-building scaffolds, sup -porting identity-affirming learning pathways, and mitigating some of the social pres -sures associated with traditional classroom participation.

Despite these emerging possibilities, many believe GenAI could also introduce risks, particularly through the reproduction of gender stereotypes and representational biases embedded in training data. As a result, extensive studies have been conducted on gender biases and associated problems inherent to AI algorithms (Castaneda et al., 2022; Girona &amp; Yarger, 2024; Idowu et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024) as opposed to rather limited amount of research explicitly exploring how GenAI can be used to enhance girls' participation in STEM fields.

Currently, there remains a critical gap in understanding whether these tools can be effective in increasing girls' interest and engagement in STEM subjects. Since teach -ers at primary and secondary school levels are regarded as one of the most influen -tial environmental factors affecting girls' interest, motivation, and career aspirations in STEM (Australian Government Department of Industry, 2024; Msambwa et al., 2024a), there is a need to seek teachers' opinions on their use of GenAI tools for improving students' learning experiences, with the focus on how these tools support girls' engagement in STEM fields.

## 2  Background

Across various global and national contexts, UNESCO's (2017) ecological frame -work of factors influencing girls' and women's participation, achievement and pro -gression in STEM studies stands out as an important framework that advocates for the integration of gender-conscious approaches in STEM education. Importantly, this framework clearly indicates that the persistent underrepresentation of girls in STEM fields cannot be attributed solely to individual interest or aptitude, instead, this situ -ation is the result of deeper sociocultural influences including societal norms, insti -tutional practices, and educator attitudes and behaviours. The UNESCO framework (2017) emphasises the necessity of inclusive and gender-responsive teaching meth -ods, the availability of unbiased and stereotype-free learning materials, and systemic support structures for educators, of which teachers are the focus of these efforts. In fact, teachers play an important role in students' educational success, not only for delivering subject matter knowledge, but also for how they model positive attitudes, cultivate strong teacher-student relationships, and create a supportive classroom envi -

ronment (Hattie, 2023). These teacher behaviours form the foundation of inclusive teaching practices that foster improved learning outcomes by creating learning envi -ronments where every student can succeed (Mitchell &amp; Sutherland, 2020). Therefore, it  is  necessary  to  acknowledge  that  teachers  have  a  direct  role  in  shaping  STEM learning experiences, classroom culture, and student self-beliefs. Taken together, it is clear that teachers ultimately impact whether girls feel welcomed, competent and motivated to continue pursuing STEM education (Master et al., 2016; Msambwa et al., 2024a; UNESCO, 2017; Wang &amp; Degol, 2017).

Aligned with UNESCO's (2017) recommendations within the context of Australia, the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers (Australian Institute for Teaching and School Leadership, 2020) sets a clear expectation for inclusive teaching. Specifi -cally, Standard 1.5 in the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers outlines the expectation that teachers adapt teaching methods and implement inclusive teaching strategies  to  accommodate diverse learning styles and abilities through differenti -ated instruction that tailors both content and pace to individual needs (Australian Institute for Teaching and School Leadership, 2018). However, there are significant challenges that impede teachers' efforts in providing education to diverse learners in a dynamic classroom, such as limited school resources to support differentiated instruction  and  inadequate  time  for  planning  and  implementation  (Gibbs,  2023 ; Gibbs &amp; Beamish, 2021).

Research suggests that GenAI holds promise for enhancing students' learning, and many educators recognise its potential to support effective learning processes (Laak &amp; Aru, 2024; Noroozi et al., 2024; Pantazatos et al., 2024). Specifically, studies have shown that the use of GenAI can foster student engagement by enabling personalised learning  pathways,  dynamically  adapting  content,  providing  on-demand  access  to materials, and offering timely feedback (Maghsudi et al., 2021; Noroozi et al., 2024 ; Tapalova &amp; Zhiyenbayeva, 2022). These capabilities have been found to significantly improve students' learning outcomes, as GenAI creates a tailored, effective learning environment that motivates students to engage more deeply with the material (Zhu et al., 2025). Importantly, the use of GenAI to promote student engagement and learn -ing have also been confirmed in the context of STEM education (Mahon et al., 2024 ; Taani &amp; Alabidi, 2024; Xu &amp; Ouyang, 2022).

Although the capabilities of GenAI to personalise and tailor instructional content and learning experiences implies that it is applicable in various educational settings and contexts, existing literature primarily focuses on using GenAI for subject content delivery and explanations to provide personalised learning. Notably, these studies do not evaluate outcomes for specific demographic groups, such as girls, or the impact of  GenAI on their engagement in STEM (Dumont &amp; Ready, 2023; Jauhiainen &amp; Garagorry Guerra, 2024; Noroozi et al., 2024; Szabó &amp; Szoke, 2024).

Furthermore, concerns arise from the ethical implications of integrating GenAI tools  in  education,  particularly  in  light  of  the  misconceptions  that  get  embedded within AI-generated content due to biases in data GenAI is trained on (Blair-Loy et al., 2024). Specifically, AI-generated content has been found to include negative, social and biologically counterintuitive content, hegemonic biases, and systematic gender and racial biases (Acerbi &amp; Stubbersfield, 2023; Bender et al., 2021). Given that gender stereotypes represent a significant  environmental  barrier  to  girls'  participation  and  engagement  in  STEM (Cheryan et al., 2017; Msambwa et al., 2024a), the integration of GenAI tools in educational settings is not without risks. Addressing this intersec -tion is particularly important at a time when educators and schools are increasingly integrating GenAI into teaching practices. Therefore, investigating how GenAI could support gender-inclusive STEM education while mitigating and minimising inadver -tent re-introduction of biases or perpetuating harmful stereotypes represents a crucial area for research and policy development. Such investigations are needed to provide valuable insights into leveraging these tools effectively, ensuring equitable participa -tion and sustained interest among girls in STEM disciplines.

Therefore, this study seeks to address a significant gap in understanding how pri -mary and secondary teachers conceptualise the use of GenAI in relation to gender equity in STEM education. It also examines how these perceptions shape teachers' readiness to use GenAI in ways that might support girls' participation, particularly given its potential to enhance overall student learning experiences.

Accordingly, the study is guided by two research questions:

1. How do primary and secondary STEM teachers conceptualise the opportunities and risks of using GenAI to support gender-inclusive STEM learning?
2. What factors shape teachers' readiness and ability to integrate GenAI in ways that may address gender equity concerns in STEM?

Through these questions, the study contributes an evidence-based theoretical frame -work explaining how teachers understand GenAI within the broader context of gen -der equity in STEM, with implications for professional learning, policy development, and responsible AI adoption in schools.

## 3  Methodology

This  study  adopted  a  Grounded  Theory  methodology  to  develop  an  empirically derived  explanation  of  how  teachers  conceptualise  the  opportunities,  risks,  and implications  of  using  GenAI  within  gender-inclusive  STEM  education.  Grounded Theory was selected as the methodological approach for this study given the limited

<!-- image -->

<!-- PAGE 6 -->

research on the intersection of GenAI, STEM pedagogy, and gender equity. The use of Grounded Theory as inductive, theory-building approach allowed for the develop -ment of concepts and categories directly grounded in teachers' experiences rather than from predetermined frameworks (Glaser &amp; Strauss, 2017).

## 3.1  Participants and sampling

A purposeful sampling strategy (Creswell &amp; Poth, 2016) was used in the initial stages to recruit teachers with experience in both: (a) STEM-related teaching and (b) famil -iarity  with  GenAI tools. Teachers from government and independent primary and secondary schools in Victoria, Australia, were invited to participate. Co-educational schools  were  intentionally  selected  so  that  teachers  could  speak  to  differences  in girls' and boys' engagement in STEM subjects.

The schools included in this study were identified as having Index of Community Socio-Educational Advantage (ICSEA) scores above the national average for the year 2024, as reported on the My School website tool (Australian Curriculum Assessment and Reporting Authority, 2025). The average ICSEA value across the five schools was 1130, indicating that these schools serve student populations from socio-educa -tionally advantaged backgrounds, characterised by higher levels of parental educa -tion, professional occupational profiles, and access to resources typically associated with positive educational outcomes.

Seven  teachers  consented  to  participate.  Participants  had  diverse  professional backgrounds spanning early childhood to senior secondary STEM education, leader -ship roles (e.g., Head of STEM, Head of Pedagogy, Director of Arts and Technology), and teaching experience ranging from 10 to 24 years (see Table 1). These varied roles positioned participants differently in relation to curriculum design, technology adop -tion, and gender-equity initiatives, shaping both their exposure to GenAI and their capacity to reflect on its pedagogical and ethical implications.

In  line  with  qualitative  and  grounded  theory  traditions,  the  study  approaches teachers  not  as  representatives  of  a  broader  population,  but  as  professionally  and institutionally situated actors whose perceptions of GenAI and gender equity emerge from their lived professional experiences, subject specialisations, and school con -texts  (Creswell  &amp;  Poth,  2016;  Glaser  &amp;  Strauss,  2017). Accordingly,  the  analytic focus is placed on depth, iterative comparison, and theoretical sufficiency rather than on numerical representativeness. Although small, the sample size was sufficient to support the development of conceptually rich categories grounded in participants' accounts.

As categories began to emerge during analysis, theoretical sampling guided sub -sequent recruitment and interview probing. Later interviews deliberately sought par -ticipants who could elaborate on developing categories such as GenAI familiarity, bias  awareness,  gendered  participation  patterns,  and  implementation  barriers. The seventh  interview  did  not  produce  new  conceptual  insights,  indicating  theoretical saturation: the point at which additional data no longer added depth or variation to the emerging categories.

## 3.2  Data collection

Data were collected through one-on-one semi-structured interviews conducted online via Microsoft Teams. This format enabled flexible scheduling and ensured that teach -ers  from  different  school  sectors  could  participate  with  ease. All  interviews  were audio-recorded with consent and transcribed automatically using Microsoft Teams' transcription feature. Transcripts were manually reviewed and corrected to ensure accuracy prior to analysis. Interviews lasted approximately 30-45 min, sufficient for participants being open to sharing their personal thoughts and experiences about the use of GenAI throughout the study (Lichtman, 2012) and consistent with the depth required for Grounded Theory interviewing.

The semi-structured interview guide included prompts such as:

- 'How do you currently use GenAI tools in your teaching or professional work?'
- 'What opportunities, if any, do you see for GenAI to support student engagement in STEM?'
- 'In  your  view,  how  might  GenAI  influence  girls'  participation  in  STEM  sub -jects?'

While the interview guide ensured consistency across participants through these core prompts, the  semi-structured  format  also  supported  the  kind  of  exploratory  depth required in Grounded Theory. Particularly, the interviewer remained attentive to con -cepts that surfaced naturally during the conversation and followed these emergent leads  with  additional  prompts  or  clarifying  questions.  For  example,  when  teach -ers spontaneously raised issues such as gender bias in GenAI outputs, variations in their own confidence or familiarity with GenAI tools, or the institutional conditions shaping GenAI use in their school, the interviewer probed further to elicit concrete examples, contextual detail, and the teachers' underlying reasoning. This responsive approach reflects Grounded Theory's emphasis on theoretical sensitivity in remain -ing  open  to  participants'  meanings  and  allowing  them  to  guide  the  direction  and depth of the conversation, while still grounding each interview in the shared set of guiding questions. In this way, data collection supported both comparability across interviews and the depth necessary for identifying meaningful patterns and variations in teachers' conceptualisations of GenAI.

## 3.3  Data analysis

Data analysis followed the iterative, comparative, and inductive procedures of classic Grounded Theory (Glaser &amp; Strauss, 2017). Analysis began immediately after the first interview and proceeded concurrently with data collection, enabling theoretical sen -sitivity to concepts emerging from teachers' accounts. The analytic process involved several  interconnected  stages:  open  coding,  constant  comparison,  memo-writing, axial coding, and selective coding which resulted in a data-driven theoretical model.

Open coding  commenced  with  a  line-by-line  examination  of  each  transcript  to identify significant actions, meanings, and processes expressed by participants. This approach ensured that early analysis remained grounded in the data rather than guided by preconceived assumptions. A combination of descriptive codes and in vivo codes that were grounded in participants' own words was applied to capture nuances in how teachers  described  their  experiences  with  GenAI,  gender  dynamics  in  STEM,  and perceived opportunities and risks. The researchers maintained an open and reflexive stance toward the data to ensure that emerging patterns and meanings were derived directly  from participants' accounts rather than shaped by preconceived categories or theoretical assumptions. This openness enabled a grounded understanding of how teachers conceptualised the role of GenAI in fostering girls' engagement in STEM and facilitated the identification of significant social and pedagogical processes, such as navigating uncertainty, mitigating bias, and adapting teaching and learning practices.

To get comprehensive understanding of data and to identify concepts without preconceptions, the initial approach to open coding was relatively fine-grained. During this process some important themes emerged. These themes confirmed that teach -ers recognised that the underrepresentation of girls in STEM was a problem at the primary and secondary school level; teachers examined reasons behind the problem,

Codes were examined for simi -larities, differences, and recurring patterns. Iteratively examining the coded sections resulted in grouping codes which had the shared meaning. As the  analysis  progressed,  open  codes  were  grouped  and  related  to  one  another through axial coding. This process involved comparing clusters of codes to identify higher-level conceptual categories that captured broader processes and influences. For example, initial open codes such as ' previous experience with GenAI ',  ' suitability of GenAI to the classroom environment ' and ' concerns about GenAI ' were conceptually related and grouped under the broader category ' teachers' experience with GenAI '.

Through this process, clearer relationships began to form: teachers' varying levels of GenAI familiarity shaped the kinds of opportunities and risks they recognised; perceptions of GenAI's paradoxical nature were influenced by both prior experience and concerns about gender stereotypes; and institutional conditions created impor -tant contextual constraints that shaped how teachers interpreted GenAI's relevance for gender-inclusive STEM teaching. Axial coding therefore clarified the conditions under which teachers developed particular perceptions or concerns, highlighted how differences in GenAI experience influenced their sensitivity to gender-related issues, and provided the foundation for integrating these categories into a coherent analytic structure.

Following axial coding, selective coding was undertaken to identify a core cat -egory capable of integrating the major categories into a coherent theoretical frame -work.  This  stage  involved  revisiting  the  data,  comparing  categories  against  one another, and reviewing analytic memos to determine which concept best represented the central process underpinning participants' accounts. Through this iterative refine -ment, the core category was defined as teachers' sensemaking of GenAI in relation to gender-inclusive STEM education. Selective coding then focused on clarifying how this core category related to the major categories and on establishing the boundaries and internal logic of the emerging theory. This step did not introduce new interpreta -tions but instead consolidated the analytic structure by specifying how the catego -ries converged around the core category to form a grounded, integrative explanatory model.

Throughout the analysis, constant comparison, memo-writing, and iterative refine -ment were essential in ensuring that the emerging theory remained firmly grounded in the data. Theoretical memos were written from the earliest stages of open coding and continued through axial and selective coding, providing a detailed record of analytic decisions, conceptual comparisons, and the evolving relationships among categories. Early descriptive memos captured emerging insights from each transcript, while sub -sequent comparative and integrative memos interrogated the boundaries of categories such as 'teacher familiarity with GenAI,' 'perceived pedagogical affordances,' and the 'paradoxical nature of GenAI.' These memos played a critical role in sustaining theoretical sensitivity by prompting the research team to continually examine alterna -tive interpretations, clarify conceptual properties, and identify areas requiring further data-driven refinement. During axial and selective coding, focused memos were used to test the coherence of category linkages and to evaluate the explanatory reach of the emerging core category, ultimately strengthening the consolidation of 'teachers' sensemaking of GenAI in relation to gender-inclusive STEM education' as the cen -tral integrative process. The integration of open, axial, and selective coding therefore enabled progressively higher levels of abstraction while maintaining strong empirical ties  to  participants'  accounts.  Consistent  with  classic  Grounded Theory  principles (Glaser &amp; Strauss, 2017), existing literature was consulted only after the core catego -ries had stabilised, serving to elaborate and contextualise the developing concepts rather than direct their initial formation. This analytic process which was supported by systematic memo-writing provided a robust foundation for constructing a coher -ent theoretical model that reflects the central processes evident in teachers' accounts.

## 4  Findings

The analysis generated a grounded theoretical account of how teachers make sense of using GenAI to support gender-inclusive STEM education. While participants dis -cussed a broad range of issues surrounding girls' participation in STEM, the central concern reflected in the data was how teachers conceptualised the possibilities and risks of GenAI for addressing gender disparities. This selective code 'Teachers' sen -semaking of GenAI in relation to gender-inclusive STEM' integrated three major categories that structure the findings:

1. Teacher familiarity and experience with GenAI.
2. The paradoxical nature of GenAI for girls' engagement in STEM.
3. Implementation barriers shaping teachers' current and future use of GenAI.

## 4.2  Paradoxical nature of GenAI in engaging females in STEM

Participants  expressed  a  consistent  tension  between  viewing  GenAI  as  a  resource that could broaden girls' participation in STEM, and a technology that could inadver -tently reinforce gender stereotypes. This 'paradoxical' framing formed the second major category.

## 4.2.1  GenAI as a tool to empower and engage female students

Teachers' reflections on how GenAI might support girls' engagement in STEM were largely exploratory, reflecting early-stage sensemaking rather than established class -room practice. Nevertheless, these reflections reveal important beliefs about gender, learning, and the perceived pedagogical affordances of GenAI. Although most partic -ipants had not yet used GenAI directly with students or developed specific strategies for supporting girls in STEM through GenAI, several teachers with higher levels of GenAI capability were able to envision meaningful possibilities. These teachers, par -ticularly P3 (GenAI collaborator) and P6 (GenAI innovator), offered more expansive and detailed reflections on how GenAI might create empowering learning conditions for female students.

P3 highlighted GenAI's potential to scaffold confidence and provide personalised, low-pressure entry points into STEM activities. While acknowledging that GenAI alone may not be sufficient to increase girls' interest in STEM, P3 noted that it could 'make [girls] more confident in those studies' by helping them generate ideas, over -come initial hesitation, and access creative starting points tailored to their preferences.

Both P3 and P6 also emphasised the value of choice and autonomy, observing that girls often responded positively when they were able to select from a range of GenAI-generated suggestions. As P3 explained: 'I've found that the girls have quite enjoyed having that choice a bit more, whereas the boys are like, 'just tell me what you want me to make.''.

P6 extended this idea by describing how GenAI could support personalised project pathways aligned with students' interests. They highlighted the empowering effect of giving girls ownership over project direction and aesthetic choices, particularly through multimodal and image-generation tools:

That ownership and empowering them to take their interest into it. I think will have real benefits for girls….Yes, it's real visual and I think definitely with the like lots of image creation and you know presentation tools and things like that, that they actually can have ownership over that. (P6)

## 4.2.2  Concerns regarding the impact of GenAI on females in STEM

Alongside the recognition of GenAI's potential, teachers also expressed concern that GenAI could reproduce or amplify gender stereotypes present in its training data. A key focus is teachers' awareness of bias and stereotypes in AI-generated con -tent, which was evident in their opinions about gender stereotypes and biases in the classroom . Specifically, three teachers (P2, P3, P5) reflected on stereotyping and bias in GenAI content. They described examples of bias in AI-generated imagery, such as depictions of women and men in traditional gender roles, where women were cook -ing and men were portrayed as doctors or scientists, whereby girls might internalise these cues as signals that certain STEM domains were 'not for them'. ## 4.3  Implementation barriers

A key aspect of teachers' sensemaking about GenAI related to the institutional and pedagogical barriers that limited their ability to integrate the technology into class -room practice. While the study's focus was on supporting girls' engagement in STEM through  GenAI,  teachers  consistently  described  broader,  systemic  constraints  that shaped their current readiness and confidence. These barriers manifested across both first-order (external and structural) and second-order (internal, often belief or confi -dence-related) dimensions.

## 4.3.1  Institutional support

When asked about the resources needed to use GenAI to promote female inclusion in computing and STEM, most teachers, regardless of their experience with GenAI tools  (P1,  P2,  P4,  P5,  P6,  P7),  highlighted  broader  barriers  to  integrating  GenAI into classrooms. ## 5.3  Paradoxical nature of GenAI

The paradoxical nature of GenAI emerged as a significant theme in both the datadriven (Fig. 1) and enriched models (Fig. 2), highlighting the potential and inher -ent challenges of using GenAI tools to foster female engagement in STEM fields. While most participants expressed uncertainty about how GenAI tools could specifi -cally support or empower female students in STEM, teachers with greater experience and familiarity with these tools (higher TRA-GenAI levels) identified brainstorm -ing capabilities and personalised learning, specifically the ability to tailor learning experiences to students' interests, as key opportunities for GenAI to increase girls' confidence and engagement in STEM subjects. Although research specifically exam -ining the application of GenAI for empowering female students in STEM remains limited,  broader  literature  supports  these  findings,  highlighting  the  potential  of GenAI use cases for brainstorming (Chen &amp; Zhu, 2023; Han et al., 2024) and also for dynamically adapting instructional materials based on students' knowledge levels, performance, and interests, thereby enhancing engagement, confidence, and motiva -tion (Ammar et al., 2024; Binhammad et al., 2024; Jauhiainen &amp; Garagorry Guerra, 2024; Joseph &amp; Uzondu, 2024). Therefore, our findings indicate that some teach -

<!-- image -->

<!-- PAGE 23 -->

ers,  particularly  those  with  higher  levels  of  experience  and  familiarity  with  these tools (higher TRA-GenAI levels), are able to recognise the potential uses of GenAI tools for fostering girls' engagement, confidence, and overall learning experiences in STEM subjects.

At the same time, few teachers in this study explicitly expressed concerns for the use of GenAI tools for engaging female students in STEM. Instead, most teachers mentioned broader challenges and concerns with the use of GenAI in their teach -ing and learning practices, including academic integrity, age restrictions, and secu -rity risks. However, among those teachers who explicitly discussed gender-specific impacts, concerns were raised about inherent biases embedded within AI-generated outputs. Meanwhile, two teachers with lower experience and familiarity with GenAI tools  (lower  TRA-GenAI  levels)  explained  that  the  presence  and  impact  of  these biases depend on how the tool is used and the subject context in which it is applied. Indeed,  literature  indicates  that AI-generated  content  commonly  produces  outputs that reinforce gender-based stereotypes about STEM and stereotypical gender roles, evident in both textual and visual content (Kotek et al., 2023; Locke &amp; Hodgdon, 2024; Meyer et al., 2023). Given the negative impact of these biases and stereotypes on girls' confidence in STEM (Cheryan et al., 2017; Master, 2021; Msambwa et al., 2024a, b ; Wang &amp; Degol, 2017) and the growing global attention from researchers and human rights organisations highlighting the risks of perpetuating social biases through stereotypical AI-generated content (Baines et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2024 ; UNESCO, 2024b, c ),  these  findings  emphasise  the  need  for  educators  to  remain vigilant about the presence of biases in GenAI content. This awareness is essential to ensure that teachers do not inadvertently reinforce regressive gender stereotypes when using or presenting AI-generated outputs to their students.

Critically, the present findings highlight that awareness and concern about gender bias  in  GenAI  tools  were  not  widespread  among  teachers.  Rather,  those  teachers with more experience and familiarity with GenAI tools (higher TRA-GenAI levels) were more likely to express concerns about bias in AI-generated content. This insight indicates the presence of an important link between teachers' familiarity and experi -ence with GenAI, and their sensitivity to the paradox inherent in using these tools as a means of engaging and empowering girls in STEM. Specifically, teachers who had greater familiarity and experience with GenAI tools (higher TRA-GenAI levels) were more likely to recognise the educational benefits of these technologies for enhancing girls' engagement in STEM and also envision specific classroom activities that could appeal to or empower female students; however, they also outlined concern regarding potential  ethical  risks,  particularly  those  involving  gender-specific  biases  and  ste -reotypes (the paradox). Importantly, the researchers assert that all educators must be aware of, and recognise the paradox when using GenAI tools to ensure that they are used to enhance girls' engagement in STEM instead of inadvertently perpetuating the gender stereotypes embedded within these tools.

## 5.4  Implementation barriers

Implementation  barriers  shaped  every  aspect  of  teachers'  GenAI  sensemaking. Existing research highlights the extensive challenges that teachers encounter when

<!-- image -->

<!-- PAGE 24 -->

integrating emerging technologies such as GenAI, consistently reporting teachers' limited confidence in using these tools (Ng et al., 2025; Wang &amp; Cheng, 2021 ; Woodruff et al., 2023). Although all seven participants reported using GenAI tools teachers experienced additional barriers that affected their  ability  to  confidently  and  effec -tively integrate GenAI to bridge gender gaps in STEM. These barriers included time constraints, insufficient resources, and a lack of training in the effective and ethical use of GenAI tools.

After integrating insights from existing literature, the final enriched model was refined  to  incorporate  Ertmer's  (1999)  framework,  categorising  institutional  sup -port  as  a  first-order  barrier. According  to  Ertmer's  framework,  first-order  barriers represent the external, systemic constraints beyond teachers' individual control that impede effective technology integration. In fact, teacher interviews consistently high -lighted factors such as the need for PD, limited resource availability, and the lack of guiding  frameworks  as  significant  institutional  support  barriers  affecting  the  inte -gration of GenAI tools in their teaching and learning practice. These findings align closely with previous research emphasising that such institutional barriers substan -tially hinder effective technology adoption in education, even when educators hold positive beliefs and attitudes towards the technology (second-order barriers) (Ertmer et al., 2012; Scherer &amp; Teo, 2019).

Although  our  interview  data  did  not  explicitly  highlight  second-order  barriers as defined by Ertmer (1999), previous literature emphasises that teachers' internal beliefs, attitudes, and confidence are key drivers of technology adoption (Tondeur, 2020). In line with this, research indicates that teachers' familiarity and experience with GenAI shapes their perceptions of its pedagogical and technological benefits (Cheah  et  al.,  2025;  Kaplan-Rakowski  et  al.,  2023;  Wang  &amp;  Cheng,  2021).  This is  crucial,  as  studies  have  shown that adoption rates of GenAI in educational set -tings vary considerably, with some educators even refusing to use these tools (Brit -ish Computer Society, 2024; Kaplan-Rakowski et al., 2023; Woodruff et al., 2023). Therefore, in recognising the link between internal dispositions towards GenAI tools and actual adoption behaviours, our enriched model incorporated second-order bar -riers,  which  refer  to  internal,  belief-based  factors  that  affect  teachers'  willingness and  ability  to  integrate  technology  (Ertmer,  1999).  These  barriers,  such  as  teach -ers' technology value beliefs (i.e., whether teachers perceive GenAI as beneficial for enhancing teaching and learning) and their pedagogical beliefs (i.e., whether teach -ers consider the use of GenAI is aligned with effective teaching practices), becomes particularly salient once external, first-order barriers have been addressed (Cheah et al., 2025; Ertmer et al., 2012).

## 5.5  Practical recommendations

Drawing on participant insights and existing literature, three recommendations are proposed to increase teachers' adoption of GenAI to bridge gender gaps in STEM:

1. Build teachers' awareness of bias and stereotypes and bias mitigation,
2. Develop teachers' critical AI literacy, and.
3. Foster a conducive environment through policy advocacy.

Critically, these recommendations highlight the importance of institutional support (professional  development  and  policy  and  procedure)  in  addressing  barriers  for the use of GenAI as a tool to foster girls' engagement in STEM subjects. Without these institutional supports, educators face significant challenges in adopting GenAI to bridge gender gaps in STEM subjects within the primary and secondary school context.

## 5.5.1  Build teachers' awareness of bias and stereotypes and bias mitigation

Teachers' awareness of gender bias in GenAI outputs is a foundational requirement for using these tools to support girls' participation in STEM. Primary and secondary students  are  at  formative  stages  of  identity  development  and  are  particularly  sus -ceptible to internalising gender stereotypes embedded within educational materials (Martin &amp; Ruble, 2010; McGuire et al., 2020). Because GenAI systems learn from vast datasets that inevitably encode historical and societal biases (Vartiainen et al., 2024), uncritical  use  of AI-generated  content  risks  reinforcing  narrow  representa -tions of who belongs in STEM and which identities or abilities are reflected in STEM learning.

Teachers therefore need both a conceptual understanding of how GenAI systems reproduce bias and the attentiveness to recognise when such bias appears in class -room outputs. Prior research highlights that developing students' critical thinking and analytical skills is a prerequisite for engaging with GenAI responsibly (Chiu, 2024 ; Nguyen, 2025). Recent workshop-based interventions (Hu, 2025; Vartiainen et al., 2024) demonstrate that when students are taught to interrogate training data, repre -sentation, and algorithmic patterns, they become better equipped to identify gender stereotypes and other forms of bias in AI-generated content. These findings illustrate the pedagogical importance of teachers being able to guide conversations about bias and explain why GenAI is not a neutral tool but one shaped by the data and assump -tions underpinning it.

Our findings highlight substantial variation in teachers' sensitivity to bias, which closely aligned with their level of GenAI familiarity. Teachers with less experience were often uncertain about how to detect systemic bias or underestimated its potential influence on girls' participation in STEM, sometimes assuming that bias was depen -dent solely on subject content or user prompting. In contrast, teachers with higher TRA-GenAI levels were more attuned to representational risks and more likely to question whether GenAI outputs might inadvertently depict STEM in stereotypically gendered  ways. This  variation  matters  as  unrecognised  bias  may  enter  classroom materials unnoticed, and substantial evidence shows that biased or stereotypical por -trayals of STEM, whether explicit or subtle, can diminish girls' confidence, interest, and long-term engagement in these subjects (Cheryan et al., 2017; Master, 2021 ; Msambwa et al., 2024a, b ; Wang &amp; Degol, 2017). Ensuring that teachers develop greater awareness is therefore crucial for preventing GenAI from amplifying inequal -ities it is intended to address.

Beyond  awareness,  teachers  also  require  practical  strategies  to  mitigate  bias in  GenAI  outputs.  One  effective  approach  is  prompt  engineering,  which  involves crafting prompts that explicitly encourage inclusive and diverse representations. As

<!-- image -->

<!-- PAGE 26 -->

Geroimenko (2025) recommends, teachers can counteract stereotypical defaults by incorporating gender-neutral or diversity-explicit language into prompts. For exam -ple,  rather  than  asking,  'What  does  a  nurse  do  each  day?',  which  may  elicit  ste -reotypically gendered responses, teachers might write, 'Describe the daily tasks of nurses from diverse backgrounds and genders.' Such prompting does not eliminate systemic bias but significantly reduces the likelihood that GenAI will generate con -tent reinforcing traditional gender roles.

Supporting teachers to adopt these bias-aware practices through targeted profes -sional  development,  time  for  experimentation,  and  clear  institutional  guidance  is essential for ensuring that GenAI becomes a tool that promotes, rather than hinders, gender equity in STEM. Building this awareness and equipping teachers with practi -cal bias mitigation approaches will play a critical role in enabling them to use GenAI responsibly and in ways that actively foster girls' confidence, participation, and sense of belonging in STEM classrooms.

## 5.5.2  Develop teachers' critical AI literacy

Developing teachers' critical AI literacy  is  essential  for  using  GenAI  in  ways  that genuinely support gender-inclusive STEM education. While GenAI offers affordances such as personalised learning, idea generation, and flexible representations of STEM concepts, these benefits can only be realised when teachers understand how GenAI systems function, how to evaluate their outputs critically, and how to deploy them in pedagogically and ethically informed ways that promote instead of hindering girls' engagement in STEM. Critical AI literacy therefore extends beyond technical profi -ciency: it encompasses the knowledge, judgment, and reflective capacity teachers need to ensure that GenAI is used inclusively and responsibly in their STEM classrooms.

The findings of this study show that teachers vary considerably in their understand -ing of GenAI and in their confidence using it for instructional purposes. Although all  participants  had  experimented  with  GenAI,  many  expressed  uncertainty  about how to use these tools with students in ways that align with curriculum goals or gender-equity. This mirrors broader research indicating that teachers often lack the foundational  understanding  needed  to  integrate  GenAI  into  teaching,  assessment, and learning design effectively (Ng et al., 2025; Wang &amp; Cheng, 2021; Woodruff et al., 2023). Several teachers noted difficulty in interpreting GenAI outputs including evaluating their  appropriateness  for  STEM  learning,  or  understanding  the  mecha -nisms behind them which represent gaps that directly affect their ability to use GenAI intentionally to support girls' participation and confidence in STEM subjects.

Teachers with higher TRA-GenAI experience demonstrated deeper insight into both the pedagogical affordances and the limitations of GenAI for gender-inclusive STEM learning. This aligns with research showing that teachers' familiarity with GenAI strongly influences their perceptions of its educational value and their will -ingness to integrate it into practice (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Kaplan-Rakowski et al., 2023; Wang &amp; Cheng, 2021). Conversely, teachers with lower familiarity tended to focus on operational concerns (e.g., age restrictions, plagiarism, reliability) and expressed greater uncertainty about the feasibility of using GenAI to address gen -der disparities in STEM. These patterns illustrate that teachers' level of critical AI

<!-- image -->

<!-- PAGE 27 -->

literacy shapes not only their confidence but their capacity to imagine and design GenAI-supported activities that meaningfully engage girls in STEM.

Consistent with international findings, participants confirmed that their upskilling in GenAI is largely self-directed, relying on personal exploration rather than struc -tured institutional support. A recent British Computer Society (2024) report similarly shows that 95% of teachers are self-taught GenAI users, 64% have not used tools such as ChatGPT, and 19% express no interest in adopting them in the future. This reliance on self-directed learning risks deepening inequities: teachers with the time, confidence, or personal interest develop stronger literacy, while others fall behind. This comes at a critical point where GenAI tools are becoming increasingly prevalent across many areas, including in education, and widening gaps in teachers' under -standing and use of these tools risk exacerbating existing inequalities in students' learning opportunities. Therefore, without structured, school-supported professional development, such disparities limit the potential of GenAI to contribute to genderequitable teaching practices.

Effective professional development for gender-inclusive STEM should therefore cultivate both foundational and applied dimensions of critical AI literacy. Founda -tional literacy includes understanding how GenAI models are trained, how outputs should be evaluated, and what ethical, privacy, and safety considerations must be observed when using GenAI with students. Applied literacy includes practical skills such as prompt design, interpreting GenAI output critically, and creating GenAI-sup -ported learning activities that give girls greater ownership, confidence, and opportu -nities to personalise STEM inquiry which were the affordances identified by teachers with higher GenAI familiarity in this study. These components are mutually rein -forcing: foundational understanding supports sound judgment, while applied skills enable teachers to use GenAI creatively and inclusively in daily classroom practice.

An  important  international  reference  for  conceptualising  this  literacy  is  the UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Teachers (UNESCO, 2024a). The frame -work outlines a progression of foundational, technical, pedagogical, and ethical com -petencies required for responsible and inclusive AI integration in education. Although not mandated nationally in Australia, it offers a coherent structure that schools can draw on to identify the specific knowledge and skills teachers need to meaningfully use GenAI in gender-inclusive STEM learning. Competencies related to understand -ing AI systems, evaluating AI outputs critically, applying AI ethically, and designing inclusive AI-supported  learning  experiences  intersect  directly  with  the  challenges identified in this study. Incorporating elements of this framework into local profes -sional  development  initiatives  can  help  ensure  that  teachers  develop  the  depth  of literacy required to use GenAI in ways that support girls' confidence, participation, and broader engagement in STEM.

Consequently, strengthening teachers' critical AI literacy is therefore central to the broader goal of gender-inclusive STEM education. Teachers with deeper literacy are better positioned to evaluate the appropriateness of GenAI outputs, mitigate potential harms, and design activities that leverage GenAI to support girls' confidence, curios -ity, and sense of belonging in STEM fields. Conversely, limited literacy constrains teachers' ability to use GenAI responsibly or imaginatively, narrowing the opportuni -ties for girls to benefit from these emerging tools.

## 5.5.3  Foster a conducive environment through policy advocacy

Creating  a  supportive  policy  and  procedural  environment  is  instrumental  in  sup -porting teachers to use GenAI in ways that promote gender-inclusive STEM educa -tion. While teacher knowledge and pedagogical confidence are critical, they must be underpinned by institutional conditions that clarify expectations, provide guidance, and enable safe, ethical, and purposeful GenAI use. Without such conditions, teach -ers face uncertainty that limits not only their general uptake of GenAI but also their ability to harness these tools to create gender-inclusive STEM learning experiences.

In  this  study,  participants  consistently  described  the  absence  of  clear  schoollevel frameworks as a major barrier to integration. For many teachers, uncertainty regarding data privacy, student use, permissible prompting practices, and appropriate use cases for GenAI created hesitation and constrained experimentation. As inter -national research similarly highlights, explicit institutional policies are essential for enabling effective and ethical AI integration (Bahroun et al., 2023; Cheah et al., 2025 ; Nedungadi et al., 2024; Noroozi et al., 2024; Woodruff et al., 2023). Without clarity in policies, teachers are left individually interpreting risks, which can inadvertently restrict  opportunities  for  girls  to  access  GenAI-enabled  STEM  learning  tasks  that might otherwise help build confidence, creativity, and engagement. Clear guidance is  therefore a prerequisite for realising the gender-inclusive potential of GenAI in primary and secondary classrooms.

Leadership emerged as a crucial factor in fostering a conducive environment for teachers.  Teachers  emphasised  that  explicit  direction  from  school  leaders  which included mentions of clear policies, model examples, and coordinated professional development helped them understand both the possibilities and boundaries of GenAI use. This aligns with the findings from Chang (2012), Keane et al. (2020), and Cheng and Wang's (2023) on digital leadership, which emphasises the influence of princi -pals and school leadership teams in shaping teachers' technology beliefs, strength -ening  digital  literacy,  and  mitigating  both  first-  and  second-order  barriers.  When leaders articulate a strategic vision for GenAI that explicitly focuses on equity and inclusion, teachers are more likely to feel confident exploring GenAI approaches that can enhance girls' sense of belonging and competence in STEM. Effective leader -ship therefore acts as a critical factor for ensuring that GenAI integration aligns with gender-inclusive educational goals rather than reinforcing existing inequities.

At the system level, participants expressed the need for more robust and action -able  guidance  from  education  authorities. Although  the Australian  Department  of Education (2023) has released an initial framework outlining principles for GenAI use in schools, however, teachers in this study noted that implementation remains unclear. Existing guidelines in Victoria (which is the the jurisdiction of this research) (SchoolsVic, 2024) focus on broad considerations such as privacy, cultural respect, and academic integrity, but they do not provide actionable advice on how GenAI can be used in classrooms to address specific pedagogical aims, including genderequitable participation in STEM. Importantly, this current ambiguous policy results in uneven adoption practices within schools and limits the extent to which GenAI can be deployed in ways that intentionally support girls' engagement, confidence, and

<!-- image -->

<!-- PAGE 29 -->

representation in STEM subjects. This suggests that education authorities must move beyond high-level principles to provide clear, practice-oriented guidance on using GenAI. Without concrete examples and expectations, schools will continue to adopt GenAI unevenly, limiting its potential to support girls' engagement and representa -tion in STEM.

Broader  ethical  and  equity  standards  can  also  strengthen  the  conditions  under which  teachers  integrate  GenAI  in  gender-inclusive  ways.  Documents  such  as UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2022b) and UNICEF's Policy Guidance on AI for Children (2021) outline principles of fairness, non-discrimination, transparency, and safeguarding. Although these documents are not  written  as  classroom  guidelines,  they  offer  a  valuable  ethical  reference  point that  schools can draw on when shaping local expectations for GenAI use. Align -ing school-level procedures with these principles helps teachers evaluate whether their GenAI-supported instructional decisions reflect inclusive practice by remaining attentive to whether GenAI outputs perpetuate gender stereotypes or constrain girls' participation in STEM activities. Grounding local practice in these widely recog -nised principles therefore contributes to an enabling environment in which teachers can use GenAI confidently, ethically, and purposefully to support girls' engagement in STEM.

## 6  Conclusion, limitations and future directions

This  study  developed  a  Grounded Theory  to  explain  how  primary  and  secondary school teachers adopt, engage with, and ultimately negotiate the use of GenAI tools in efforts to promote gender-inclusive STEM education. Through constant comparison, iterative coding, and theoretical sampling, three interrelated processes were identi -fied: teachers' interpretation of the paradoxical nature of GenAI, their varying levels of familiarity and experience with the technology, and the broader institutional condi -tions that shape their capacity to integrate GenAI into practice. The final theoretical model, enriched through the lens of Ertmer's (1999) first- and second-order barriers, illustrates how these processes interact to influence whether and how teachers envi -sion GenAI as a tool for fostering girls' participation, confidence, and engagement in STEM subjects. In doing so, the study contributes an empirically grounded, con -ceptually coherent explanation of the conditions under which GenAI may support gender-inclusive STEM teaching.

The contribution of this study is twofold. Conceptually, it provides a theory-driven account  of  the  interpretive  and  contextual  dynamics  that  shape  teachers'  emerg -ing engagements with GenAI, highlighting how teachers sensemaking, experience, and institutional structures converge in ways that either constrain or enable genderresponsive  innovation.  Practically,  the  model  offers  educators,  school  leaders,  and policymakers a holistic framework for understanding the supports teachers require to use GenAI ethically, confidently, and purposefully in service of gender equity goals. Three recommended areas for action: strengthening teachers' awareness of bias and gender stereotyping in GenAI outputs; advancing teachers' critical  AI literacy; and fos -

<!-- image -->

<!-- PAGE 30 -->

tering enabling policy and procedural conditions that give teachers clarity, confidence, and ethical guidance when using GenAI in STEM classrooms. These recommenda -tions, grounded in theoretically informed insights from our model, offer a pathway for schools and systems seeking to leverage GenAI responsibly and inclusively.

Several  limitations  should  be  acknowledged.  The  sample  was  predominantly female and drawn from higher-ICSEA schools. While this reflects the broader demo -graphics of the Australian teaching workforce (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2025), the findings may not capture the experiences of male teachers or those working in more resource-constrained settings. Future research should explore how contextual factors such as socioeconomic diversity, rurality, or different disciplinary contexts shape teachers' engagement with GenAI for gender-inclusive STEM education.

A further limitation relates to the study's high reliance on interviews as the primary data source. While interviews are methodologically appropriate for an exploratory Grounded Theory study centred on teachers' sensemaking of GenAI tools to bridge gender gaps in STEM, they capture teachers' perspectives rather than their enacted practices. Incorporating additional data sources such as classroom observations, arte -fact analysis, or school policy documents could offer a more triangulated understand -ing of how GenAI is interpreted and used in real classroom contexts. Future research that integrates multiple data types would therefore be valuable for refining, extend -ing, or challenging the emergent model developed in this study.

Although several participants held leadership roles including as principals or heads of teaching and learning, the study did not explicitly focus on leadership perspectives or analyse organisational decision-making as a central phenomenon. Consequently, while leadership insights emerged incidentally through teacher accounts, the find -ings do not fully capture the broader strategic, policy, or resourcing considerations that influence GenAI adoption at the school level. Future research that purposively engages with school leaders could deepen understanding of how institutional priori -ties,  school capacity, and system-level planning shape the conditions under which teachers are able to integrate GenAI in equitable and gender-responsive ways.

Despite these limitations, the emergent model provides a meaningful foundation for understanding how teachers can be supported to use GenAI to advance genderinclusive STEM education. By applying the theoretical insights and practical recom -mendations outlined in this study to inform professional learning, school procedures, and system-level discussions on responsible AI use, educators and leaders can better create conditions in which GenAI becomes a tool that supports as opposed to hinders girls' participation and agency in STEM learning.
