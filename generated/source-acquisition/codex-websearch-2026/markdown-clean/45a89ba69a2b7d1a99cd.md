## Algorithmic bias in clinical resource allocation by a large language model: a cross-sectional in-silico evaluation of 13,608 decisions

Siddharth Gandhi 1 and Michael Balas 2*

## Abstract

Background Large language models (LLMs) hold the potential to transform clinical medicine, but their capacity to amplify societal biases is a significant concern, particularly in the ethically sensitive domain of clinical resource allocation. A critical knowledge gap exists in the quantitative understanding of how these models weigh non-clinical factors in forced-choice ethical dilemmas. This study provides a large-scale, systematic evaluation of a leading LLM to measure its implicit biases in resource allocation decisions.

Methods We conducted a cross-sectional, in-silico analysis using OpenAI's GPT-5 model. Seven clinical vignettes representing scarce resource scenarios were paired with 3,888 unique patient profiles generated from a full-factorial combination of seven demographic and social variables (age, gender, race, income, dependents, education, social support). This resulted in 13,608 unique A/B patient comparisons. We used pooled logistic and linear regression models to identify the primary drivers of the model's allocation choices and quantify the magnitude of biases. The stability and internal consistency of the model's judgments were also assessed.

Results The LLM's decisions were governed by a distinct hierarchy of non-clinical factors. Patient age, income, and number of dependents were the most powerful predictors. Being 25 years old increased the odds of selection more than threefold compared to a 50-year-old (OR 3.31; 95% CI, 2.96-3.71; p &lt;  0.001), while earning over $5 million annually reduced the odds by 75% (OR 0.25; 95% CI, 0.22-0.28; p &lt;  0.001). The model also systematically favored racial minorities, women, and non-binary individuals over White men. Analysis revealed significant internal inconsistency, with the model's chosen patient matching its own higher-priority-scored patient in only 66.3% of cases overall. The influence of biases was highly context-dependent yet showed stochastic stability upon repeat testing.

Conclusions The tested model operates with a strong, opaque, and context-dependent set of non-clinical biases when faced with ethical dilemmas. These findings suggest that this architecture's inconsistent and unpredictable ethical framework makes its direct integration into clinical decision support for resource allocation untenable at this stage. Rigorous standards for safety, transparency, and ethical validation are required before these technologies can be responsibly deployed in high-stakes clinical settings.

Keywords Large Language Models, Algorithmic Bias, Medical Ethics, Resource Allocation, Clinical Decision Support Systems

## Background

Large  language  models  (LLMs)  have  the  potential  to transform clinical medicine, yet their capacity to embed and amplify societal biases remains a significant concern [1-7]. This risk becomes especially acute in the ethically fraught domain of clinical resource allocation. Decisions regarding  scarce  resources  like  organs,  ICU  beds,  or ventilators  carry  significant  consequences  and  must  be guided by principles of fairness and clinical urgency. The introduction of non-clinical patient characteristics, such as  race  or  income,  into  these  delicate  judgments  could undermine  core  medical  ethics  and  erode  public  trust. Before  these  models  can  be  safely  integrated  into  any aspect of healthcare, a deep and quantitative understanding of their inherent biases is essential [8-13].

While previous work has documented algorithmic bias in  medical  AI,  there  is  limited  empirical  data  on  how modern  LLMs  perform  when  faced  with  forced  choice ethical  dilemmas  [5,  14-18].  A  critical  knowledge  gap exists regarding the precise hierarchy of factors that influence their decisions. It is not enough to know that bias exists; it is necessary to quantify the relative influence of distinct demographic and social variables to understand the potential for harm. Without such a systematic evaluation, the integration of these models into decision support systems carries a substantial risk. Unchecked, they could  institutionalize  and  scale  inequitable  allocation patterns, further entrenching existing health disparities.

This study provides a large-scale, systematic evaluation of a leading LLM to measure its implicit biases in clinical resource allocation by analyzing 13,608 unique decisions generated  from  thousands  of  demographically  varied patient profiles.

## Methods

## Study design

We  conducted  a  cross-sectional,  in-silico  analysis  to systematically measure the implicit biases of an LLM in forced-choice,  clinical  resource  allocation  dilemmas.  In this  context,  we  defined  a  systematic  evaluation  as  the utilization  of  a  full-factorial  design  to  exhaustively  generate  every  possible  combination  of  the  chosen  demographic  variables.  These  profiles  were  then  randomly paired to ensure that the resulting biases are statistically attributable to specific patient characteristics rather than sampling artifacts or incomplete coverage of the demographic  interaction  space.  The  study  was  designed  to quantitatively  estimate  the  influence  of  specific  demographic and social variables on the model's decisions and to assess the stability of its judgments across a range of common ethical scenarios.

## Vignette and profile development

Seven  clinical  vignettes  were  developed  to  represent diverse and ethically challenging resource allocation scenarios  in  medicine  (Supplementary  Methods  1).  These included decisions regarding a single liver for transplant, an ICU bed during hospital overflow, a ventilator during a pandemic, a spot in an experimental cancer drug trial, an emergency operating room triage scenario, a priority vaccine dose, and a kidney dialysis machine.

To  evaluate  potential  bias,  each  vignette  was  paired with patient profiles generated from a full-factorial combination of seven demographic and social variables (Supplementary  Methods  2):  age  (25,  50,  75  years),  gender (man,  woman,  non-binary),  race  (White,  Black,  Asian, Indigenous), annual income ($30,000, $80,000, $150,000, $5,000,000+),  number  of  dependents  (0,  1-2,  3+),  education  attainment  (high  school,  college,  postgraduate), and  social  support  strength  (weak,  moderate,  strong). This full-factorial design produced 3,888 unique patient profiles  per  vignette.  We  utilized  fully  synthetic  profiles rather  than  de-identified  real-world  records  to  strictly isolate the influence of demographic variables. By using synthetic  data,  we  eliminated  the  confounding  noise inherent in real-world clinical records, such as variations in  documentation  quality,  unmeasured  comorbidities, or  provider-specific  phrasing,  thereby  ensuring  that  the model's preferences were driven exclusively by the variables under investigation.

## LLM prompting and data collection

We  used  OpenAI's  GPT-5-Chat  model  (OpenAI;  San Francisco,  California,  US)  for  all  experiments  [19].  For each  of  the  seven  vignettes,  the  3,888  unique  profiles were  randomly  shuffled  and  paired  sequentially,  creating 1,944 distinct patient pair comparisons. By randomly shuffling  the  profiles  prior  to  pairing,  we  ensured  that demographic characteristics were not systematically associated  with  either  the  Patient  A  or  Patient  B  position.  This  randomization  was  designed  to  mitigate  systematic  confounding  from  positional  bias,  which  is  the tendency of LLMs to favor the first option presented, by ensuring no specific demographic characteristic was disproportionately  associated  with  a  specific  position  [20]. To ensure that the model was forced to make meaningful trade-offs based on multiple characteristics, this list of pairs  was  algorithmically  audited;  any  pair  that  differed on fewer than two variables was removed and re-shuffled

<!-- PAGE 3 -->

until  all  1,944  pairs  in  each  vignette  differed  on  at  least two  demographic  characteristics.  This  process  resulted in  a  total  of  13,608  unique  prompts  (1,944  pairs  x  7 vignettes).

Each  prompt  was  submitted  to  the  GPT-5  model  via its  official  application  programming  interface  (API)  on October 8th, 2025. In alignment with established reporting standards for clinical AI evaluation, our methodology prioritized  transparency in  the  reporting  of  the  model's intended use and performance metrics [21]. Furthermore, to isolate and measure the model's inherent pre-trained biases,  we  utilized  a  zero-shot  prompting  strategy.  As noted in existing LLM ethics benchmarks, this approach prevents  'in-context  learning'  or  'sycophancy' ,  which  is where the model adapts its responses to match the perceived intent of few-shot examples, thereby ensuring the results  reflect  the  model's  baseline  distributive  preferences [22]. Each prompt presented the clinical vignette, the two patient profiles (Patient A and Patient B), and a standardized  set  of  instructions  explicitly  stating  that clinical urgency and predicted survival were identical and that the allocation decision should be based solely on the provided  information  (Supplementary  Methods  3).  For each pair, the model was given two separate independent tasks: (i) choose which patient (A or B) should receive the resource, and (ii) assign an allocation priority score from 0 to 100 to each patient, where a higher score indicated higher priority. We deliberately restricted the model from generating textual reasoning or chain of thought explanations. This constraint was applied to isolate the decision output  and  to  address  the  faithfulness  problem,  where models  produce  post-hoc  rationalizations  that  may  not accurately reflect the actual computational drivers of the decision [23]. By focusing on raw choices and scores, we aimed to measure the model's inherent priorities without the influence of potentially deceptive narrative justifications.  All  model  parameters  were  kept  at  their  default settings (temperature = 1), and each API call was stateless  to  prevent  conversational  history  from  influencing the results.

## Statistical analysis

All collected data were first subjected to a series of computational audits to confirm complete response coverage, valid parsing of choices and scores, the factorial balance of  demographic  variables  across  the  full  dataset,  and adherence to a minimum of at least two varying variables for all experimental pairs.

Following  this  verification,  we  fitted  a  pooled  logistic regression model with vignette-level fixed effects to identify the primary drivers of the model's allocation choices. The outcome was the binary choice of whichever person in each scenario was selected. All demographic variables were included as categorical fixed effects, and the model coefficients were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence  intervals  (CIs).  To  create  an  overall  importance hierarchy and compare the influence of multi-level factors,  we  ranked  each  patient  characteristic  by  the maximum absolute log-OR observed across its levels. As a  secondary  analysis,  we  fitted  separate  logistic  regression  models  for  each  of  the  seven  vignettes  to  examine the context-dependency of biases.

The clinical magnitude and real-world impact of the  observed  biases  were  quantified  by  calculating  the mean risk difference (RD) in the probability of selection between each demographic level and a pre-defined reference profile (a 50-year-old White man earning $80,000, with  no  dependents,  a  college  degree,  and  moderate social support). We generated 95% CIs for these risk differences using non-parametric bootstrapping with 1,000 replicates.

To validate the model's quantitative priority scores and assess their alignment with its explicit choices, we fitted a pooled linear regression model with the same predictors and fixed-effects structure, using the absolute difference in priority scores between the two simulated patients as the  continuous  outcome.  We  further  assessed  the  relationship between scores and choices by calculating their concordance,  defined  as  the  proportion  of  pairs  where the chosen patient also received the higher priority score.

Finally,  to  evaluate  the  model's  inherent  stochasticity, a stability analysis was performed. One fixed patient pair was randomly selected from each of the seven vignettes and repeatedly submitted to the model 30 times, generating an additional 210 responses for analysis. To assess the consistency of the LLM in making the same choice under the  same  circumstances,  we  calculated  two  metrics:  (i) the choice agreement, defined as the proportion of trials matching the modal (most common) choice, and (ii) the coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by the mean) for the priority scores of both patients to measure their relative dispersion and reliability.

All  analyses  were  conducted  using  R  version  4.3.2  (R Foundation  for  Statistical  Computing,  Vienna,  Austria), and  a  two-sided  p-value &lt; 0.05  was  considered  statistically significant.

## Ethics statement

This was an in-silico study that analyzed outputs from an LLM in response to fully synthetic clinical vignettes and patient profiles; no human participants were enrolled, no real  patient  data  or  medical  records  were  accessed,  and no identifiable or protected health information was used. The study was conducted in the spirit of the ethical principles articulated in the Declaration of Helsinki. Because the  work  constitutes  non-human-subjects research and posed no foreseeable risk to individuals, formal research ethics board/IRB review and informed consent were not

required  under  applicable  regulations  and  institutional policy.  All  prompts  contained  only  fictitious,  de-identified  information,  and  no  clinical  care  decisions  were influenced by this research.

## Results

## Hierarchical influence of demographic factors on allocation decisions

Our  analysis  of  the  13,608  unique  allocation  decisions revealed  that  the  LLM's  choices  were  consistently  governed by a distinct and powerful hierarchy of non-clinical patient  characteristics.  A  grouped  analysis  of  variable importance,  which  aggregates  the  maximum  effect  size within  each  category,  demonstrated  that  patient  age, income, and number of dependents were the most influential  characteristics.  These  three  factors  exerted  substantially  more  influence  on  the  model's  choices  than race, social support, gender, or education, which formed a secondary tier of less impactful, albeit still significant, predictors (Mann-Whitney U-test: p &lt;  0.05) (Fig. 1).

A  pooled  logistic  regression  model  with  fixed  effects for  each  vignette  allowed  for  a  precise  quantification  of these biases (Table 1). Age was the single most powerful predictor of allocation. Compared to a 50-year-old reference patient, a 75-year-old patient's odds of receiving the scarce resource were reduced by 78% (OR 0.22; 95% CI, 0.20-0.25; p &lt;  0.001). Conversely, being 25 years old was the model's most heavily favored attribute, increasing the odds of selection by more than threefold (OR 3.31; 95% CI,  2.96-3.71; p &lt;  0.001).  Income  was  the  second  most dominant factor, with the model exhibiting a strong and dose-dependent aversion to wealth. Beyond  the  dominant  factors,  the  model  exhibited consistent and statistically significant biases across other demographic and social lines (Table 1).  The  model  systematically favored racial minorities over White patients. Indigenous patients saw the largest advantage, with 53% higher odds of receiving the resource (OR 1.53; 95% CI, 1.36-1.72, p &lt;  0.001),  followed  by  Black  patients,  with 44% higher odds (OR 1.44; 95% CI, 1.28-1.62, p &lt;  0.001). Asian patients were also favored, though to a lesser extent (OR 1.21; 95% CI, 1.08-1.36, p &lt;  0.01).  A  similar pattern of bias was observed for gender. Both women (OR 1.32; 95% CI, 1.19-1.46, p &lt;  0.001) and non-binary individuals (OR 1.31; 95% CI, 1.18-1.45, p &lt;  0.001) were significantly favored  over  men.  The  model  also  systematically  preferred patients with weak social support over those with moderate support (OR 1.26; 95% CI, 1.14-1.40, p &lt;  0.001). Educational  attainment  had  the  smallest  overall  effect, though patients with a high school degree were slightly favored (OR 1.16; 95% CI, 1.05-1.29, p &lt;  0.01) and those with a postgraduate degree were penalized (OR 0.85; 95% CI,  0.77-0.94, p &lt;  0.01)  relative  to  those  with  a  college degree.

## Magnitude of disparities in absolute allocation probability

To translate these relative odds into absolute differences in  probability,  we  calculated  the  RD  for  each  characteristic  compared  to  a  standardized  reference  profile  (a 50-year-old  White  man  earning  $80,000  annually,  with no  dependents,  a  college  degree,  and  moderate  social support),  shown  in  Table  2.  This  analysis  validated  the profound  impact  of  the  primary  demographic  drivers. A  75-year-old  patient's  absolute  probability  of  receiving a resource was 31% lower than that of an otherwise identical 50-year-old (RD, -0.31; 95% CI, -0.33 to -0.29). When combined with the 17% advantage for a 25-yearold (RD, + 0.17; 95% CI, + 0.16 to + 0.19), the total swing in allocation probability based on age alone spanned 48%. Patients in the highest income bracket faced a 25% reduction in their chance of selection (RD, -0.25; 95% CI, -0.27 to  -0.22).  The  single  most  advantageous  patient  profile  was  a  25-year-old (+ 17%) indigenous (+ 7%) woman (+ 5%)  or  non-binary  (+ 5%)  person  earning  an  annual income of $30,000 (+ 6%), with three or more dependents (+ 14%), weak social supports (+ 0.03%) and a high school education (+ 3%); compounding to a 55% higher probability of being the recipient of a scarce, life-saving resource. Conversely, the most disadvantageous patient profile was a  75-year-old  (-31%)  White  man  (reference  standard, 0%) earning at least $5,000,000 annually (-25%) with no dependents,  moderate  social  supports  (reference  standards), and a postgraduate degree (-2%); compounding to 58% lower probability of being the recipient of a scarce, life-saving resource.

## Context-dependency of allocation decisions

In  our  per-vignette  regression  models,  the  influence  of demographic  effects  was  shown  to  vary  considerably across clinical scenarios (Supplementary Table 1). Some characteristics were highly influential in certain contexts but  not  statistically  significant  in  others. ## Internal model consistency

To assess the model's internal consistency, we measured the  concordance  between  its  explicit  choice  between patient A or patient B, and its assigned quantitative priority scores (Supplementary Table 2). Overall, the patient assigned  a  higher  priority  score  was  chosen  as  the  one who should receive the life-saving resource 66.3% of the time, suggesting a notable disconnect between the model's implicit valuation and its final decision. Furthermore, this  level  of  consistency  was  highly  dependent  on  the clinical context; concordance ranged dramatically from a low of 38.6% in the ventilator distribution vignette to a high of 90.3% in the vaccine priority vignette.

A  linear  regression  on  the  score  difference  validated our primary findings, demonstrating that the same demographic  factors  driving  the  allocation  choice  also  drove the  magnitude  of  the  priority  score  gap,  with  extreme wealth  (β  =  -6.00;  95%  CI,  -6.71  to  -5.30, p &lt;  0.001), 3 + dependents (β = 5.11; 95% CI, 4.50 to 5.72, p &lt;  0.001), Indigenous race (β = 4.98; 95% CI, 4.28 to 5.68, p &lt;  0.001), and older age (β = -4.93; 95% CI, -5.54 to -4.32, p &lt;  0.001) showing the largest effects (Table 3).

## Stochastic instability and context-dependency of bias

A stability analysis was performed to evaluate stochastic instability,  whereby  one  randomly  chosen,  fixed  patient pair from each vignette was repeatedly and independently evaluated 30 times. Five of the seven vignettes resulted in the same choice (patient A versus patient B) 100% of the time, while the remaining two vignettes chose the same patient  96.7%  ( n =  29/30)  and  86.7%  ( n =  26/30)  of  the time. Aggregating coefficients of variation of quantitative priority  scores  across  vignettes  and  patients,  the  mean coefficient was 0.138 (range 0.000-1.025). This means the model's priority scores were fairly consistent overall: on average they varied by about 14% relative to their mean across repeated runs, with most scenarios showing little to no fluctuation.

## Discussion

The ethical concern is not that any single prioritization principle, such as giving some weight to  youth or caregiving responsibility, is indefensible per se,  but  that  the  model  instantiated  a  rigid  and  opaque weighting of such principles absent explicit justification, deliberation,  or  accountability.  While  our  experimental  design  intentionally  suppressed  narrative  output  to ensure methodological  consistency, this highlights a fundamental  limitation  of  LLMs  related  to  explanatory

<!-- PAGE 8 -->

faithfulness:  generated  explanations  may  not  accurately reflect  the  true  reasons  for  a  model's  prediction  [23]. Consequently,  the  opacity  we  observed  is  an  inherent structural  property  of  the  model  architecture,  where ethical trade-offs are determined by probabilistic weights rather than by accessible and contestable logic. In health systems where distributive justice must be publicly reasoned  and  contestable,  an  unexplainable  value  function implicitly embedded in an AI system is incompatible with the norms of clinical ethics and governance [24, 25].

A cross-cutting observation is the pronounced contextdependence  of  these  preferences.  Effect  sizes  varied  by vignette,  with  certain  attributes  (for  example,  extreme wealth or number of dependents) penalized or rewarded more  strongly  in  vaccine  prioritization  than  in  ICU or  ventilator  scenarios.  This  pattern  suggests  that  the model's behavior is shaped by interactions between attribute cues and the moral framing of the scenario rather than  by  a  single,  globally  stable  objective.  Contemporary  allocation  frameworks  likewise  emphasize  that  no single principle suffices across settings, instead advocating transparent, scenario-appropriate, multi-principle approaches that can be publicly defended. Our findings therefore reinforce the need  for 'scenario-complete' pre-deployment  audits  that  span  the  relevant  space  of allocation  dilemmas  rather  than  sampling  one  or  two archetypes [24-26].

Equally important is internal coherence. We observed only moderate agreement between scalar priority scores and binary allocation choices: the higher-scored patient was selected 66.3% of the time overall, with concordance ranging  from  38.6%  in  the  ventilator  vignette  to  90.3% in  the  vaccine  vignette.  This  score-choice  discordance admits  at  least  two  interpretations:  the  model  may  be juggling multiple, partly conflicting  objectives  (some expressed in narrative  rationales  and  others  in  numeric scoring),  or  stochastic  elements  of  decoding  and  alignment may be introducing inconsistency. From a systemsengineering perspective, either mechanism is hazardous. Downstream tools that threshold or average scores may not reproduce the model's categorical recommendations, and clinicians may infer a level of calibration or cardinal meaning from the numbers that the model does not sustain. Risk-management guidance for AI in safety-critical domains  accordingly  stresses  explicit  definition  of  use cases,  measurement  of  reliability,  and  traceable  links between model outputs and decisions; requirements that generic LLMs, used off-the-shelf, rarely meet [27, 28].

At  the  same  time,  our  stability  analysis  showed  that, for  fixed  patient  pairs,  the  categorical  choice  was  often repeatable  (perfect  agreement  in  five  of  seven  vignettes and ≥ 86.7% in all), and the associated quantitative scores were  fairly  consistent  on  average  (mean  coefficient  of variation ~ 0.14). By maintaining a temperature of 1 (the default setting for varied output), we provided a more rigorous test of the model's biases; the high level of choice agreement  observed  suggests  that  the  identified  biases are deeply embedded and persist even in the presence of stochastic decoding. Stability alone, however, cannot fix an  opaque  value  function:  repeatability  without  legitimacy risks reliably reproducing hidden normative commitments. Where the stakes involve life-saving resources, procedural  legitimacy  -  publicly  defensible  rationales, avenues for appeal, and enforceable oversight - matters as much as statistical regularity [27, 29].

Notably, the directional pattern we observed did not  simply  replicate  prevailing  societal  inequities:  the model favored racial minority patients and women over the  reference  White  male  profile.  Several  mechanisms could underlie this pattern, including instruction-tuning choices, alignment heuristics that penalize harm to historically  disadvantaged  groups,  or  correlations  learned in pretraining data. Whether such behavior represents a desirable corrective or an overcorrection that introduces new  distortions  cannot  be  adjudicated  by  performance metrics  alone.  The  broader  literature  on  clinical  algorithms cautions that fairness cannot be evaluated in isolation from social context: construct choices (for example, using  cost  as  a  proxy  for  illness)  can  silently  encode structural  inequities,  and  'technical'  fixes  that  abstract away  context  may  misfire.  Our  results  underscore  that legitimacy in clinical governance demands explicit, contestable normative commitments, not implicit ones embedded in weights learned from data [29, 30].

These  findings carry three implications for policy and  design.  First,  models  intended  for  any  proximity to  allocation  decisions  should  be  constrained  by  hard guardrails  that  prevent  non-clinical  attributes,  or  their proxies, from influencing recommendations, with adversarial tests to probe leakage. Second, evaluation must be explicitly  context-sensitive:  audits  should  span  diverse allocation dilemmas, quantify both relative and absolute effects,  and  include  checks  for  score-choice  alignment and  calibration.  Third,  interfaces  should  avoid  exposing uncalibrated numeric scores as if they were cardinal utilities; when scores are displayed, systems should detect and  flag  discrepancies  between  scores  and  categorical recommendations  for  human  review.  These  proposals align with emerging governance frameworks that emphasize  transparency, risk controls, documentation ('model cards' and 'datasheets'), and human oversight, and with regulatory trajectories that classify clinical AI influencing care as high-risk [27, 28, 31-36].

This study has strengths supporting the credibility of its inferences. A full-factorial profile construction mitigated confounding among non-clinical attributes; vignette fixed effects controlled for framing; both relative (odds ratios) and absolute (risk differences with bootstrap confidence

<!-- PAGE 9 -->

intervals) impacts were estimated; and ancillary diagnostics  -  concordance  and  stability  -  provided  convergent validity. Prompts were stateless with default parameters, isolating the contribution of presented attributes.

Limitations warrant cautious interpretation due to several  design  constraints.  The  reliance  on  a  single  model architecture limits the generalizability of our conclusions, as the specific hierarchy of biases we identified might differ in models from other developers or those fine-tuned on different clinical corpora. While we adhered to reporting guidelines by  utilizing  zero-shot  prompting  and default  hyperparameters,  we  did  not  evaluate  whether specific  prompt  engineering  techniques  or  temperature adjustments could mitigate the observed disparities. Furthermore, while the use of synthetic profiles ensured high internal  validity,  this  approach  limits  ecological  realism by excluding comorbidities and institutional constraints found in  real-world  data.  The  forced-choice  design  and suppression  of  textual  explanations  prioritized  quantitative  measurement  over  qualitative  reasoning.  While this  precluded  an  analysis  of  the  model's  stated  ethical framework, it was a necessary methodological trade-off to avoid the faithfulness gap. Finally, our analysis diagnoses behavioral bias in outputs; it does not identify mechanism or provenance within the training pipeline.

Future research must prioritize cross-model replication to distinguish between architecture-specific artifacts and widespread training data biases. Work should extend to evaluating fairness-aware prompting, attribute masking, and post-hoc debiasing techniques, while exploring semisynthetic  designs  that  blend  controlled  demographic manipulations with de-identified clinical covariates under appropriate governance. Human-factors experiments are needed  to  study  how  clinicians  interpret  score-choice disagreements  and  whether  interface  design  can  mitigate misuse or over-trust. Any contemplated use should be  embedded  in  governance  that  sets  scope  narrowly, documents  intended  and  prohibited  uses,  and  ensures meaningful human control - principles now reflected in consensus guidance and regulatory frameworks [27, 28, 31, 37-39].

## Conclusion

In  sum,  when  confronted  with  ethically  consequential allocation dilemmas, a state-of-the-art LLM (OpenAI's GPT-5) expressed strong, systematic, and context-dependent  preferences  over  non-clinical  attributes and displayed only moderate internal coherence between numeric  priority  scores  and  final  choices.  While  these findings are specific to the tested architecture, the magnitude and opacity of the observed biases argue against the use of current general-purpose LLMs as arbiters, or even quiet tie-breakers, in the allocation of scarce clinical resources. Any role for such systems in healthcare should be narrow, auditable, and explicitly non-normative, embedded  within  governance  that  prioritizes  transparency, equity, and accountable human judgment.
