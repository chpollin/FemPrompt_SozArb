## Abstract

### Objective

To evaluate whether large language models (LLMs) exhibit implicit gender, racial, and ethnic biases when used to provide psychiatric diagnoses, and to understand how such biases may impact the accuracy and fairness of AI-assisted clinical decision support.

### Patients and Methods

We conducted a large-scale audit of 6 LLMs, including general-purpose and medical-specific models, using 97 Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders 5 psychiatric training cases, conducted between October 1, 2023, and June 23, 2025. Cases were systematically altered to suggest different gender and racial/ethnic identities—across 39 demographic groups—by changing names, pronouns, and descriptors while keeping clinical symptoms constant. We assessed diagnostic accuracy, additional or missed diagnoses, and language of diagnostic reasoning. Of the LLMs tested, Generative Pretrained Transformer 4o emerged as the most accurate model and was selected for deeper analysis.

### Results

Although Generative Pretrained Transformer 4o accurately identified at least 1 correct diagnosis in 82.8% of cases (15,346 of 18,527 cases), it often added non–ground truth diagnoses (70.3% of cases, or 13,017 of 18,527 cases), suggesting a tendency to overdiagnose. Accuracy varied by gender, with higher performance for female patients and lower for nonbinary individuals. Although overall accuracy did not differ significantly by race/ethnicity, biased diagnostic patterns emerged. For example, cultural bereavement and antisocial behavior—were diagnosed exclusively in patients of color, and terms such as disruptive were used more frequently for Black men.

### Conclusion

Our findings found that LLMs reproduce and reinforce clinical biases even when symptoms are constant. Moreover, AI-based tools must be audited not only for accuracy but also for bias in both diagnoses and explanatory language, especially when used in high-stakes mental health contexts.

---

Psychiatric classification and diagnoses play an outsized role in the experience of patients, including determining eligibility for insurance reimbursements or social services,[1](#bib1) the course and type of treatment that a clinician may prescribe,[2](#bib2) and how patients perceive their prognosis.[3](#bib3) However, diagnostic systems have a contentious history, characterized by debate around their validity and perceived objectivity.[3](#bib3) Empirical studies report poor test-retest reliability for diagnoses[4](#bib4) and have highlighted how diagnoses can largely be determined by clinicians’ subjective perception of patients,[5](#bib5),[6](#bib6) as described in [Supplemental Appendix 1](#appsec1) (available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>).

Highlighting these issues, some mental health professionals have argued that digital data may provide more objective and less biased[7](#bib7) assessments, particularly through leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) and large language model (LLM)-based tools as a part of clinical decision support. However, both diagnostic protocols[6](#bib6) and language models[8](#bib8) have been demonstrated to have considerable identity-based biases. The same societal and cultural beliefs that influence how clinicians relate to their patients and determine diagnoses are also embedded in the relational structures that underlie LLMs ([Supplemental Appendix 1](#appsec1)). Previous work has described the harms of these potential biases in nonmental health contexts[9](#bib9) and the importance of using them sensitively in health contexts.[10](#bib10), [11](#bib11), [12](#bib12) More work is needed to understand how societal biases embedded in LLM relational structures may influence psychiatric diagnoses outputted for minority individuals, particularly given increasing excitement around AI’s application to clinical decision support.[13](#bib13)

Previous audits of gender and racial bias have used a method called correspondence testing in nonclinical contexts and the case analog method[6](#bib6) in psychiatric contexts. This method systematically manipulates the race and gender of a simulated individual while maintaining consistency in all other aspects. Information about simulated individuals is then sent to real-world decision makers (such as hiring managers[14](#bib14) or psychiatrists[5](#bib5)), and patterns in their responses or decisions are analyzed based on the identity of the simulated individual. Recent work has demonstrated biases in the kinds of responses provided by LLMs to mental health-related informational queries from Black individuals[15](#bib15) and when a social factor (such as religious affiliation or age) is explicitly provided in a mental health prediction task,[16](#bib16) and qualitative analyses of AI-based psychiatric diagnosis similarly suggest potential implicit and explicit biases against Black individuals.[17](#bib17) A correspondence testing method can shed light on how subtle cues of identity (such as names or pronouns) may influence a model’s diagnostic outputs and help surface more subtle prejudices that may arise through patient descriptions in a clinical setting.

We apply a correspondence testing method to investigate implicit racial and gender biases in diagnoses provided by 6 general-purpose and medical LLMs frequently used in clinical decision support research.[18](#bib18) As new language models continue to be released, we also offer an extensible approach ([Supplemental Appendix 3 and 4](#appsec1), available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>), which can be applied to any LLM. Our study examined the following 2 key research questions (RQs):

**RQ1:** Across demographic groups, how accurate are psychiatric diagnoses from state-of-the-art LLMs?

**RQ2:** How does patient identity (gender, racial or ethnic, and intersectional) influence diagnoses and explanations given by LLMs?

Our approach leveraged 97 clinical cases from the American Psychiatric Association,[19](#bib19) used by mental health professionals in diagnosing patients, with each case containing a description of an individual and accompanying ground truth diagnoses. Following Bertrand and Mullainathan,[14](#bib14) we experimentally manipulated the implicit race/ethnicity and gender identity of the patient depicted in the cases. We then prompted state-of-the-art LLMs to provide a primary diagnosis (or diagnoses) and summative reasoning for its outputted diagnoses and compared differences across 39 demographic groups spanning 13 race/ethnic identity categories from the US Census and 3 distinct gender identities. Our investigation found that although different LLMs produced biases differentially, these biases were in line with societal stereotypes, indicating gaps in the use of LLMs in digital psychiatric applications.

## Patients and Methods

### Study Design

This observational study of racial and gender biases used 97 cases from Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-5 Clinical Cases.[19](#bib19) Cases generally tend to include a given name for a fictional patient, information about their relationships with other people, and their occupation and can include related aspects of their life history that might be relevant to a diagnosis ([Supplemental Figure 1](#appsec1), available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>). Cases occasionally include explicit demographic information about the fictional patient, including their race/ethnicity or gender identity; however, it is much more common for cases to include that information implicitly. Each case includes 1 or more ground truth diagnoses, which can include modifiers after the base diagnosis and a short summary of reasoning for the primary diagnosis.

We assessed biases through finding patterns in the diagnoses provided by LLMs when the implicit race/ethnicity and gender identity of the patient depicted in each case study was altered, whereas the rest of the case study was held constant. In [Supplemental Appendix 2](#appsec1), we describe our process of creating this data set of deidentified cases and that of systematically replacing identifying information.

To systematically change the implicit race/ethnicity and gender identity of the depicted patient in each case, we modified the depicted patient’s first and last name (following Bertrand and Mullainathan[14](#bib14)), their pronouns, and any other information that implicitly or explicitly suggested the patient’s identity ([Supplemental Figure 1](#appsec1)). We changed this information to be indicative of patients of 3 gender identities and 13 racial backgrounds/ethnicities, following race and ethnicity categories used by the US census (as discussed in [Supplemental Appendix 2](#appsec1)), which resulted in 39 intersectional demographic groups ([Supplemental Table 1](#appsec1), available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>). For each group, we identified 5 demographically distinct first and last names and modified each DSM training case with the corresponding demographic attributes (first name, last name, pronouns, descriptors, and any explicit mentions of race, ethnicity, or gender identity) for each name. We used 5 demographically distinct full names to ensure that results are not skewed toward bias associated with any single name, resulting in 195 simulated patients and 18,330 training cases with unique names and demographics. In [Supplemental Appendix 2](#appsec1), we describe our process of generating this data set of demographically distinct names and report the specific names we used in [Supplemental Table 2](#appsec1) (available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>).

To study the accuracy of LLMs in giving psychiatric diagnoses, we experimented with multiple state-of-the-art LLMs, including generative pretrained transformer (GPT)-4o and its predecessor GPT-4,[20](#bib20) Claude Sonnet 3.5,[21](#bib21) LLaMa 3.3 70b,[22](#bib22) as well as domain-specific LLMs like Med42-v2[23](#bib23) and OpenBioLLM,[24](#bib24) which have been trained on medical data, and conducted our analyses on the best performing model.

To prompt an LLM to provide a diagnosis, we leveraged the LLM prompt for medical case study diagnosis of Kanjee et al,[18](#bib18) adapting it to make the prompt specific to psychiatric case studies (original and modified prompts in [Supplemental Appendix 3](#appsec1)). We prompted the LLM to provide both the primary diagnosis (or diagnoses) and the reasoning for that primary diagnosis for each of the 18,330 training cases. For instance, example diagnoses included outputs such as bipolar II disorder, persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia) or major depressive disorder, and summaries of reasoning included descriptions such as, “[Name]’s’ clinical presentation includes a history of at least 5 discrete depressive episodes meeting the criteria for major depressive episodes, characterized by depressed mood. Additionally, [Name] exhibits hypomanic episodes that include increased energy and irritability.” The work by Zack et al,[8](#bib8) which studied bias in LLM-based diagnosis, found little influence of the temperature parameter (level of creativity or randomness in response) on level of bias in this study, we report our results for GPT’s default temperature of 0.7. Similar to Zack et al,[8](#bib8) we found minimal differences between outputs at temperature 0.0 and 0.7; these results are reported in [Supplemental Table 3](#appsec1) (available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>). This study was carried out over the course of October 1, 2023, to June 23, 2025.

### Analytic Approach

For RQ1, we specifically evaluated the incidence of several different outcomes when comparing our set of 18,527 LLM provided diagnoses and ground truth diagnoses from the training cases. We list these outcome measures in [Table 1](#tbl1). Our tested outcome measures include scenarios where the LLM gets all, some, or no ground truth diagnoses correctly, as well as when the LLM adds on additional diagnoses not described in the ground truth. We also investigated whether the language used to describe patients in the summary of reasoning provided by the LLM differed from that of the training cases through the use of sparse additive generative (SAGE) models of text.[25](#bib25) More information on nature of this approach and the parameters used are in [Supplemental Appendix 1](#appsec1). For RQ2, to understand identity-based differences, we compared each outcome measure’s values between all cases that were associated with each demographic category and all cases that were not associated with that category. For example, for our race/ethnicity dimension, one comparison was all cases that depicted patients who were White against all cases with non-White patients. These analyses were conducted with the best performing LLM (GPT-4o) found in RQ1. We used Welch *t* test for all comparisons, with *P* values adjusted for the false discovery rate for all comparisons within an identity dimension (eg, race/ethnicity, gender, and intersectional identities).

## Results

### Accuracy Across Demographic Groups (RQ1)

As shown in [Table 2](#tbl2), GPT-4o was the best performing model, correctly identifying at least 1 ground truth diagnosis in 82.83% of cases (15,346 of 18,527 cases). Moreover, GPT-4o was also able to exactly match the ground truth diagnoses in 20.30% of cases (3761 of 18,527 cases), followed by Claude Sonnet 3.5, which correctly identified at least 1 ground truth diagnosis in 81.92% of cases (15,178 of 18,527 cases). LLaMA 3.3 70B correctly identified at least 1 diagnosis in 79.81% of cases (14,787 of 18,527 cases), followed by GPT-4, which correctly identified at least 1 ground truth diagnosis in 76.21% of cases (14,110 of 18,527) and the correct number of diagnoses in 27.24% of the cases (5044 of 18,527 cases). Domain-specific (medical) LLMs, Med42-v2 70B and OpenBioLLM 70B, did not perform well, with correct identification of at least 1 ground truth diagnosis in 62.47% (11,573 of 18,527 cases) and 66.61% (8431 of 12,658) of the cases, respectively.

We found that GPT-4o was more likely than the other leading models to produce diagnostic evaluations that matched the ground truth diagnoses ([Table 2](#tbl2)). However, additional non–ground truth diagnoses were also added by GPT-4o, occurring in 48.73% of cases (9029 of 18,527 cases). This was indicative of a broader pattern we observed across diagnoses given by GPT-4o. Moreover, GPT-4o tended to provide far more diagnoses than the number of diagnoses in the ground truth case diagnoses. We found that this happened in 70.26% of cases (13,017 of 18,527 cases) that GPT-4o evaluated and that GPT-4o generally provided 1.92 (ω=0.88) times the number of ground truth diagnoses.

We found patterns in the kinds of additional non–ground truth diagnoses that were added. For example, the top 3 most provided additional diagnoses ([Table 4](#tbl4)D) were depression (8.90%, 1208 of 13,569 additional diagnoses), dysthymia (8.22%, 1116 of 13,569 additional diagnoses), and adjustment disorder (7.38%, 1001 of 13,569 additional diagnoses), which cumulatively comprised 24.50% (3325 of 13,569 additional diagnoses) of all additional diagnoses provided. As a comparison point, we compared the incidence of each additional diagnosis provided by GPT-4o with each diagnosis’s incidence across all cases in the casebook ([Table 4](#tbl4)F), to investigate what additional diagnoses GPT-4o was significantly more likely to provide; GPT-4o had a tendency to provide dysthymia and adjustment disorder diagnoses more often than in the casebook. Dysthymia comprised 8.22% (1116 of 13,569 additional diagnoses, ω=27.47%; *P*<4.94 × 10↑18) of the additional diagnoses provided by GPT-4o, compared with 0.69% (1 of 145 ground truth diagnoses, ω=8.28%) in the casebook. Similarly, adjustment disorder comprised 7.38% (1001 of 13,569 additional diagnoses, ω=26.14%; *P*<.0008) of additional diagnoses provided by GPT-4o, compared with 2.07% (3 of 145 ground truth diagnoses, ω=14.23%) of diagnoses in the casebook. Given that both diagnoses are often provided when symptoms are clinically significant but nonspecific, this may reflect a tendency by GPT-4o to express uncertainty by including broader or more ambiguous psychiatric conditions in its diagnostic output.

Further, GPT-4o was completely incorrect in approximately 1 in 5 cases it was provided ([Table 2](#tbl2)). It also failed to obtain any ground truth diagnoses in 17.17% of cases (3181 of 18,527 cases). Moreover, GPT-4o missed at least 1 diagnosis in over a third of cases (30.97%, or 5737 of 18,527 cases). We observed that GPT-4o was often unable to correctly diagnose generalized anxiety disorder ([Table 3](#tbl3)E). Although generalized anxiety disorder accounted for only 1.38% (2 of 145 ground truth diagnoses; ω=11.66%) of diagnoses in the casebook, it made up 8.16% (515 of 6,312 missed diagnoses; ω=27.37%; *P*<2.06 × 10−8) of diagnoses that GPT-4o missed. In many cases, incorrect diagnoses were the result of GPT-4o providing a spurious dysthymia or depression diagnosis ([Table 3](#tbl3)D).

It was common for GPT-4o to generate diagnoses not in the ground truth set ([Table 4](#tbl4)D-F). Of all unique diagnoses given by GPT-4o, 76.3% (265 of 348 unique diagnoses) were not in the ground truth set ([Supplemental Table 4](#appsec1), available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>). Conversely, of the unique diagnoses in the ground truth set, 1.2% (1 of 83 unique diagnoses) were never provided by GPT-4o. A full listing of the diagnoses provided by GPT-4o not in the casebook set and diagnoses in the casebook set that were missed by GPT-4o are in [Supplemental Table 5](#appsec1) (available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>).

Further, GPT-4o provided diagnoses that are not in use today or are not necessarily disorders. It provided the diagnoses paranoid schizophrenia (0.29% of diagnoses, or 137 of 47,214 diagnoses) and sexual aversion disorder (0.076% of diagnoses, or 36 of 47,214 diagnoses). Both diagnoses were eliminated between the DSM-IV and DSM-5.[26](#bib26) Moreover, GPT-4o pathologized nonclinical experiences, including diagnoses of intimacy avoidance, complicated grief, and unresolved childhood trauma (none of which are clinically recognized mental health conditions). This may demonstrate the potential for GPT-4o to overpathologize everyday human emotions and experiences, in line with the observed tendency to add diagnoses to ground truth diagnoses. Additionally, GPT-4o often provided nonpsychiatric clinical diagnoses alongside ground truth diagnoses to explain the overall condition of the patient, including diagnoses of constipation, malnutrition, and cystic fibrosis (none of which are in the DSM-5).

### Identity-Based Differences (RQ2)

#### Gender Differences

Looking next to gender-based differences, we found significant gender biases in our most accurate model’s (GPT-4o’s) diagnostic accuracy across different gender identities. Nevertheless, GPT-4o was more accurate in diagnosing cases involving female patients, with at least 1 diagnosis correct in 84.59% (5334 of 15,346 female-presenting cases; ω=36.10%) cases, compared with 81.92% (10,012 of 15,346 non-female presenting cases; ω=38.49%) for nonfemale patients (*P*<.000009) ([Table 5](#tbl5)). However, diagnoses of nonbinary patients were less accurate: 81.34% (5129 of 15,346 nonbinary cases; ω=38.96%) of cases presenting nonbinary individuals had at least 0 diagnosis correct, whereas 83.60% (10,217 of 15,346 male or female cases; ω=37.03%) presenting as male individuals and female individuals had at least 1 diagnosis correct (*P*<.0002). These patterns held for cases that were completely incorrect, in which no ground truth diagnoses were identified by GPT-4o as well as cases that were partially correct, with some correct diagnoses identified by GPT-4o.

We also observed biases with regards to the types of diagnoses that were only ever provided to people of a certain gender identity. For example, the (now defunct, DSM-IV) diagnosis of sexual aversion disorder was only provided by GPT-4o in cases presenting female patients. The diagnosis of malingering was only ever provided when the patient was nonbinary or did not have a specific gender identity listed, which may reflect a bias on the part of GPT-4o stereotyping nonbinary individuals as deceitful or attention seeking, an echo of societal stereotypes of people whose lived gender identity differs from their sex at birth.[27](#bib27)

We observed distinct gender biases associated with how GPT-4o described nonbinary patients in summaries of reasoning—for example, the words conflict (SAGE=0.34) and bizarre (SAGE=0.19) were distinctly used in nonbinary patient cases, suggesting higher stigmatization.

#### Race and Ethnic Differences

When examining race and ethnic differences, we did not observe significant differences between race/ethnic identity groups for the outcome measures ([Table 1](#tbl1)). However, we did observe biases when investigating the types of diagnoses that GPT-4o exclusively provided for cases presenting patients of color, with the top 15 diagnoses only given to people of color ([Supplemental Table 6](#appsec1), available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>). The diagnosis of anorexia nervosa was exclusively provided to patients of Asian and Pacific Islander ethnicities (and rarely to cases depicting male patients, only ever being provided once in a case depicting a male patient). In contrast, similar cases with different racial identities did not receive this diagnosis from GPT-4o. The presence of this bias may be the consequence of GPT models overcorrecting for earlier research showing an increasing rate of identified eating disorders among people in Asian countries in 1990s-2000s.[28](#bib28) This is particularly highlighted by text in the summaries of reasoning provided by GPT-4o, which highlights the cultural considerations in diagnosis among cases of eating disorder diagnoses among Asian and Pacific Islander individuals—for instance, “her restrictive eating behaviors and inadequate caloric intake are consistent with the diagnosis of Anorexia Nervosa. Other potential contributing factors, such as her *cultural and socioeconomic background*, should also be considered in the context of her symptoms,” in a case depicting a Korean individual.

However, GPT-4o also gave people of color culturally aware diagnoses that point to struggles grounded in racial identity, such as in the form of cultural bereavement or acculturation stress. We also observe a pathologization of normal human experiences, including antisocial behavior or religious or spiritual problem as diagnoses. In line with Zack et al,[8](#bib8) we observed the role of race in GPT-4o’s diagnostic processes, including an increased provision of paraneoplastic syndrome and paraneoplastic encephalitis by GPT-4o, an illness that has higher rates among African American populations.[29](#bib29)

#### Intersectional Differences

We did not observe statistically significant differences in diagnostic patterns among intersectional cases. However, we did notice qualitative differences in how patients were described in summaries of reasoning. For example, via the SAGE analysis ([Supplemental Table 7](#appsec1), available online at <https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/>), the most distinctly used word in summaries of reasoning for cases with White men was the word vulnerability (SAGE=0.42). In contrast, the most distinctly used word in summaries of reasoning for Black men was disruptive (SAGE=0.42), a bias following the stereotype of Black men as aggressive in health care contexts (described further in [Supplemental Appendix 1](#appsec1)).

## Discussion

Through our analysis, the forms of differential treatment we surfaced can be understood to be biases with clear harms, including distinct use of the word disruptive by GPT-4o when describing Black male patients, in line with research demonstrating higher rates of involuntary hospitalization due to societal perceptions of Black men as aggressive or dangerous. These descriptions could also subtly influence clinical decision-making processes of a clinician using GPT-4o as a decision-support tool and lead to minority patients being provided less empathetic care or more coercive interventions, further compounding offline trends.

While the observed differences have harms associated with them, it is also important to consider the substantial cultural diversity in how people present symptoms of mental illness. As part of our methodological approach, we hold the symptoms presented in each case constant and manipulate the identities of the depicted patients. However, the same symptom presented by patients of 2 diverse identities may be indicative of extremely different disorders or may not even be indicative of a disorder. In evaluating the accuracy of automated diagnosis, there is thus an important challenge in determining when diagnostic trends are indicative of a sensitivity to culturally informed variations in symptom presentation, compared with being indicative of prejudices that are deeply ingrained within an LLM’s training data. Put more simply, what constitutes bias, and how can technology-mediated clinical decision processes be more aware of it? Defining bias as simple quantitative differences in accuracy may not tell the full story of how the experience of an individual in distress might be altered or invalidated owing to an institutional or computational trend.

For example, we do not find differences in simple measures of accuracy across different racial/ethnic identities, but we do observe differences in the types of diagnoses that are provided to patients and how patients are described in summaries of reasoning. Within this context, we thus understand a bias to be when individuals are systematically provided diagnoses that are not in line with their lived experiences and further complicate their access to care that is acceptable and effective. For example, GPT 4o’s provision of additional adjustment disorder diagnoses could mirror real-world scenarios where an adjustment disorder diagnosis causes a delay in treatment for attention-deficit hyperactive disorder for Black children (a bias)[30](#bib30) or could allow for greater health care for patients who need a diagnosis for insurance coverage of therapy[31](#bib31) (contextual sensitivity). We understand LLM-based clinical decision support tools to be a beginning point for stronger conversations between patient and provider, rather than a stand-in for human clinicians.

This study has some limitations. Some names for American Indian or Alaska Native individuals reflected stereotypes; future work should draw from more accurate, tribally specific data sets or leverage direct consultation with tribal communities. We excluded gender transition cases because of challenges in systematically varying gender identity and did not examine sexual orientation, referring to partners generically. Finally, we focused on US racial and ethnic categories. Future work could expand to include non-US demographic frameworks (such as caste, tribe, or religion), varying sexual orientations, and people in states of gender transition, and examine how these changes affect model diagnoses. Additionally, future work could additionally vary the cultural presentations of the symptoms within the case studies because this may be more aligned with how mental illness may present in real-world contexts and help distinguish between cultural sensitivity and stereotyping.

## Conclusion

In this study, we systematically examined trends in psychiatric diagnoses provided by various state-of-the-art LLMs after systematically manipulating the implicit gender and race/ethnic identity of the patients depicted in training cases. We not only found that GPT-4o performed the best when compared with the ground truth in the training cases but also found trends in overdiagnosis, in diagnosis across different gender identities, and in the types of diagnoses and descriptions provided to cases with racial or ethnic minority patients. Given these biases, we urge caution and vigilance when using LLMs in psychiatric clinical decision support.
