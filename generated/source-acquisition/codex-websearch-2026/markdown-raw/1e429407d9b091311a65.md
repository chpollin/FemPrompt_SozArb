## New model, old risks: sociodemographic bias and adversarial hallucinations vulnerability in GPT-5

Mahmud Omar, Reem Agbareia, Donald U. Apakama, Carol R. Horowitz, Robert Freeman, Alexander W. Charney, Girish N. Nadkarni &amp; Eyal Klang

We re-evaluated GPT-5 using our published pipelines: 500 emergency vignettes across 32 sociodemographic labels for bias, and adversarial prompts with fabricated details. GPT-5 showed no measurable improvement over GPT-4o in sociodemographic-linked decision variation, with several LGBTQIA + groups /uniFB02 agged for mentalhealth screening in 100% of cases. Adversarial hallucination rates were higher (65% vs 53% for GPT-4o); a mitigation prompt reduced this to 7.67%.

Clinically grounded audits have shown recurring risks in large language models (LLMs). These include systematic variation in decisions by sociodemographic labels, often large enough to suggest bias and vulnerability to fabricated details embedded in prompts 1 -4 . These patterns may translate into avoidable referrals or escalation, unnecessary mental-health screening, and the propagation of chart errors into orders.

The past few weeks intensi /uniFB01 ed interest. OpenAI announced GPT-5 with ' built-in thinking, ' highlighted healthcare applications and new health evaluations (e.g., HealthBench), and promoted the model as safer and more capable for medical use. Public communications also suggested that individuals can go directly to GPT-5 for medical advice 5 . At the same time, coverage from health-tech reporters and recent case reports continue to question whether marketing about ' safer ' health answers is matched by independent assurance and real-world behavior 6 . Together, this creates a strong need to re-test safety on /uniFB01 xed, clinically grounded pipelines whenever models upgrade.

Accordingly, we revisited our published pipelines on GPT-5 to ask a decision-relevant question: do the new model actually reduce possible disparities and adversarial vulnerability in common clinical tasks, or do the same risks persist (or amplify) under identical clinical content and scoring?

## Evaluation Design

We re-ran our previously published pipelines on GPT-5 (chat-completion endpoint) via API. For the bias analysis, we used our validated pipeline with 500 emergency-department vignettes. Each vignette was iterated across 32 versions (control plus 31 sociodemographic labels). The model answered the samefourdecisionpoints-triagepriority, further testing, treatment level, and mental-health assessment- using the same scoring, multiple-testing

## Key Findings

In this snapshot evaluation, GPT-5 exhibited similar patterns of systematic variation despite identical clinical content. We observed variation in treatment escalation (recommended level of care: outpatient → observation → ward → ICU), the need for urgent mental-health screening (screen vs not), and smaller but consistent shifts in triage urgency (non-urgent vs urgent) and testing choice (none → basic labs/ECG → basic imaging → advanced imaging). Similar to the original investigation and to GPT-4o ' s results, the largest variations were in /uniFB02 agging for urgent mental-health screening. Several historically marginalized groups -including Black unhoused, multiple LGBTQIA + identities, and unhoused overall -wererecommendedfor screening in 100% of runs despite identical clinical details (individual subgroup results for all 31 labels are provided in the Supplementary Materials, Table Q4). Triage urgency changes were modest but directional (peaking at + 7.4% for Black transgender women). Testing choice showed a familiar socioeconomicgradient,wherelower-incomegroupsreceivedlessadvanced testing (e.g., fewer MRI and CT studies): Low-income -7.0%, Middleincome -6.8%, and High-income + 2.2% (Fig. 1). Intersectional identities consistently ranked highest for escalation and mental-health recommendations while receiving less advanced testing (e.g., CT/MRI), indicating concentrated effects not explained by the clinical presentation.

## Interpretation and Context

Adversarially, GPT-5 elaborated (hallucinated) on planted fabrications in 65% of runs under the standard prompt (95% CI 61.1 -68.7). The same mitigation prompt used previously reduced this to 7.67% (95% CI 5.16 -11.24; χ ² = 262.45, p &lt; 0.0001; OR ≈ 22), demonstrating that guardrails are highly effective but do not eliminate risk. These results mirror a simple operational truth: without enforced mitigation, the model readily propagates false chart elements; with mitigation, residual error remains non-zero.

The observed patterns closely parallel our earlier results. Intersectional groups such as unhoused populations -particularly Black unhoused more than White or unhoused in general -and several transgender/LGBTQIA + identities show the largest increases in recommended urgency and in mental-health screening. ## Implications for LLM Governance

Clinically, these patterns may translate into over-triage, potentially avoidable admissions, and unnecessary escalation clustered in speci /uniFB01 c identities, alongside blanket mental-health screening where the charted details alone might not justify it 7 . In practice, that combination could strain ED /uniFB02 ow, increase exposure to low-value or harmful interventions, and might introduce stigma or distract from the primary problem list. Based on typical emergency department volumes (~50,000 annual visits), these patterns could result in approximately 1,200 additional mental health referrals and 800 inappropriate treatment escalations annually 8 . The adversarial results indicate a second work /uniFB02 owrisk: under standard prompts, a single fabricated detail can be carried forward and elaborated into con /uniFB01 dent narrative or structured recommendations unless an explicit defense is in place. This risk increases when patients seek advice directly from GPT-5, as they may unknowingly include inaccurate or misleading details that the model then propagates. Mitigation prompts reduce this substantially but do not eliminate it. Taken together, this evaluation suggests that stakeholders and developers, whose models may reach daily practice quickly, should invest in additional research and implement stricter, creative safeguards targeted at these limitations, because newer models still appear to reproduce the same trends. We acknowledge that variation in clinical judgment across groups can be appropriate and sometimes warranted, including considerations of social, ethnic, demographic, and economic adversity 9 . Even so, with clinical content held constant, the magnitude and consistency we observed indicate that more work is needed in future models to ensure differences re /uniFB02 ect actual clinical need rather than bias by demography.

These /uniFB01 ndings carry direct implications for LLM governance in healthcare. Any model update that alters clinical output distributions should trigger re-evaluation on standardized benchmarks. Because providers frequently implement backend changes without public versioning, eventtriggered auditing is more appropriate than calendar-based review. This requires automated pipelines analogous to CI/CD in software engineering; our published pipeline illustrates such reusable infrastructure. Responsibility should be shared: developers must disclose update scopes and run internal safety suites; independent researchers should maintain /uniFB01 xed benchmarksnotoptimizedagainstbydevelopers 10 . Domain-speci /uniFB01 cmodels such as MedGemmamayexhibitdifferent bias pro /uniFB01 les, but /uniFB01 ne-tuning does not inherently eliminate sociodemographic variation 11 . We view this work as a stance for continuous, institutionalized safety auditing of LLMs in healthcare.

Although our pipeline uses emergency department vignettes, the underlying decision points (triage urgency, testing intensity, treatment escalation, and mental-health screening) are common to outpatient work/uniFB02 ows including primary care, urgent care, and specialty referrals. If sociodemographic labels in /uniFB02 uence these decisions under standardized ED content, similar patterns may manifest in outpatient settings where LLMs are increasingly explored for decision support and triage. Dedicated evaluation in these settings is warranted.

Furthermore, our study uses clinician-facing tasks with structured, physician-validated prompts. In patient-facing scenarios, prompts are unstructured and may include inaccurate details. If sociodemographic variation and adversarial vulnerability appear under controlled clinical prompts, the risks are likely ampli /uniFB01 ed when patients generate prompts without the expertise to evaluate outputs. Future studies should evaluate LLMs using patient-generated prompts across diverse sociodemographic pro /uniFB01 les.

## Methodological Considerations

We note several methodological considerations. Our results represent a single-timepoint evaluation using vendor default parameters. LLM outputs are inherently stochastic, and backend changes or different sampling strategies could yield different results at another time, underscoring the need for continuous evaluation. The current analysis is descriptive by design; the formal statistical framework is detailed in our original publications.

In conclusion, despite seemingly greater capability and agentic tooling, GPT-5 family models continued to exhibit systematic subgroup decision variation and susceptibility to adversarial inserts under /uniFB01 xed audits in this evaluation. These /uniFB01 ndings reinforce the need for continuous, automated, and model-agnostic safety benchmarking as a standing requirement for any LLM deployed in clinical contexts.
