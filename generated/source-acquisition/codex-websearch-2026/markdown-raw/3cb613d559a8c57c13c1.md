## Introduction

When the printing press emerged in the mid-15th century, it triggered a wave of societal concern and moral panic. Religious and political authorities feared the consequences of unregulated access to scripture and political tracts (Eisenstein, 1979). Anxiety over the spread of 'dangerous books' corrupting youth and promoting heretical ideas was widespread (Febvre &amp; Martin, 1997), while 17th-century scholars lamented the overwhelming 'flood of books' and the confusion it provoked among readers (Blair, 2010). Although print technology enabled mass literacy and reform, it also fueled ideological conflict and deepened social divides (Chartier, 1994). Fast forward to the present day, and we can observe parallels in the societal reaction to artificial intelligence. AI systems now generate persuasive yet often inaccurate or biased content, challenging traditional understandings of authorship, expertise, and academic integrity (Floridi, 2022). Likewise, social media and AI are associated with online radicalization, digital dependency, and rising levels of youth anxiety (Twenge, 2017; Zuboff, 2019). Simultaneously, governments and corporate actors harvest vast amounts of user data, intensifying public concern about surveillance capitalism and algorithmic governance (Benjamin, 2019; Zuboff, 2019).

In  the  context  of  social  work,  Chan  and  Li  (2023)  argue  that  digital  technologies, including  AI  chatbots,  are  reshaping  the  profession  in  China.  Hodgson  et  al. Despite  these developments,  there  remains  a  noticeable  lack  of  in-depth  theoretical  and  empirical research addressing the implications of AI within social work pedagogy.

What  kinds  of  knowledge  should  social  work  education  now  prioritize?  In  a  time marked by rapid technological change and growing social complexity, the answer lies in cultivating knowledge that is experiential, relational, community-connected, and responsive to the lived realities of practice. To prepare students for the ethical and contextual challenges they will face, social work education must adopt pedagogies that engage with uncertainty, develop critical reflection, and strengthen meaningful connections between students and the communities they aim to support.

In this context, digital literacy becomes essential-not simply as technical competence, but as a deeper capacity to understand, question, and navigate the technologies shaping social work. As James et al. (2023, p. 1551) argue, digital literacy must be conceived in its fullest sense: as encompassing both technical proficiency and a critical understanding of the  social  and  political  implications  of  digital  tools  'as  they  contribute  to  making  and remaking our world.' Digital literacy, then, is not a peripheral skill but a central framework through which students can assess and shape the role of artificial intelligence (AI) in their future practice.

This paper is guided by the following research aim: to develop a conceptual framework for critical digital literacy in social work education, grounded in decolonial theory, ethical AI  critique,  and  pedagogical  reform.  To  guide  this  analysis,  the  paper  addresses  the following research question: How can digital literacy in social work education be conceptualized  in  ways  that  critically  engage  with  artificial  intelligence,  while  remaining grounded in decolonial, ethical, and pedagogical principles?

While many scholars have emphasized the need for robust ethical frameworks to guide this engagement (e.g. Barsky, 2023), this paper argues that such ethical imperatives must be  accompanied  by  strong  theoretical  frameworks  capable  of  informing  pedagogy  in socially just, inclusive, and transformative ways.

## Social work and technology

The  term algorithm originates  from  the  name  of  the  Persian  mathematician  alKhwārizmī, which was Latinized as Algoritmi in medieval European texts. The evolution of  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  can  be  broadly  traced  through  four  distinct  phases:  rulebased  systems,  statistical  learning,  deep  learning,  and  reinforcement  learning  (Silver et  al.,  2016).  From  the  1950s  to  the  1980s,  AI  development  was  dominated  by  rule-


<!-- PAGE 4 -->


based  systems  designed  to  emulate  human  reasoning  through  predefined  logical  rules (Russell &amp; Norvig, 2010).

The  second  major  phase  began  in  the  1980s  with  the  rise  of  statistical  learning, a paradigm shift that enabled machines to extract patterns from data without relying on rigid rules. This advancement significantly propelled progress in natural language processing,  speech  recognition,  and  computer  vision  (Hastie  et  al.,  2009).  The  third phase-deep  learning-emerged  in  the  mid-2000s,  marked  by  the  use  of  artificial neural  networks  capable  of  processing  vast  and  complex  datasets.  Deep  learning revolutionized  AI's  capacity  for  language  generation  and  image  recognition  (LeCun et  al.,  2015).  Most  recently,  reinforcement  learning  has  pushed  the  boundaries  of  AI decision-making  by  enabling  systems  to  learn  from  trial  and  error  in  dynamic environments.

Social  work,  although  rooted  in  human-centered  practice,  has  a  long  history  of technological integration-from the typewriter and telephone to early computer systems adopted  in  the  1960s  for  documentation  and  administrative  support.  Today,  AI  is reshaping the field further by enhancing predictive analytics, automating routine tasks, and aiding decision-making in domains such as child welfare, resource allocation, and mental health care. These developments in AI are not isolated from earlier technological shifts within social work practice. Technologies such as typewriters, telephones, and early computer systems introduced new forms of documentation, communication, and information  management.  While  these  tools  primarily  supported  administrative  efficiency, they also laid the groundwork for contemporary AI systems that extend these functions into predictive analytics, decision-support systems, and automated forms of knowledge production. In this sense, AI can be understood not as a rupture, but as an intensification of existing technological trajectories within the profession.

However, the application of AI in social work-and more broadly-demands a critical and ethical lens. As Cathy O'Neil (2016) reminds us, algorithms are not neutral instruments;  they  are  constructed  by  humans  and  therefore  carry  embedded  assumptions, values, and biases. Adams (2021) similarly urges that we 'read power and control back into the history of AI,' recognizing how technological systems often reproduce existing social hierarchies. This perspective reveals deep concerns regarding surveillance capitalism (Zuboff, 2019), data colonialism, and systemic racialization.

These concerns are not abstract. The roots of modern biometric systems, now widely used in AI applications, can be traced to colonial administrations. Hantel (2018) documents how such systems were originally introduced in British colonies to monitor and control  colonized  populations.  These  early  technologies  classified  people  based  on biological  traits  and  later  on  genetic  determinism-mechanisms  that  persist  today  in algorithmic categorizations shaped by both biology and neoliberal market logics.

A central  challenge  in  current  AI  systems  is  their  opacity.  The  internal  mechanisms-often  referred  to  as  what  lies  'under  the  hood'-of  many  generative  AI models  remain  inaccessible  to  public  scrutiny.  As  Park  (2024)  notes,  'data  sets  to train  many  generative  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  models  are  not  made  available  to the public and their operating mechanisms are opaque to human reasoning, making it  difficult  to  predict  their  potential  harm'  (p.  36).  This  lack  of  transparency  not only  complicates  accountability  but  also  reinforces  existing  power  asymmetries. Since  many  large-scale  AI  models  are  trained  primarily  on  U.S.-based  data,  their

This  includes  problematic  patterns  such  as  the  exoticisation  of  non-White ethnicities-evident,  for  instance,  in  the  sexualization  of  Asian  women  (Birhane, 2021)-and  the  reinforcement  of  whiteness  as  the  normative  standard  in  digital content  (Bianchi  et  al.,  2023).

The consequences of racially biased data are particularly evident in facial recognition technologies.  One  such  example  involves  a  Chinese  company  that  obtained  access  to Zimbabwe's national population registry, using facial images of millions of citizens to improve AI recognition  of  Black  faces  (U.S.  Department  of  the  Treasury,  2023).  This practice  not  only  raises  severe  concerns  about  consent  and  data  sovereignty  but  also echoes colonial-era resource exploitation, where bodies and knowledge were extracted without agency or recompense.

This  phenomenon  is  part  of  what  scholars  describe  as ethics  dumping :  the practice  of  exporting  experimental  technologies  to  contexts  with  weak  regulatory frameworks  or  limited  political  resistance.  For  example,  Cambridge  Analytica reportedly  tested  data-driven  political  influence  strategies  in  countries  such  as Nigeria and Kenya before applying similar approaches in Western electoral contexts. While  these  practices  relied  primarily  on  large-scale  data  analytics  and  psychographic  profiling  rather  than  contemporary  forms  of  artificial  intelligence,  they nevertheless  illustrate  how  digital  technologies  can  be  trialed  in  contexts  with fewer  regulatory  constraints.  As  Tilley  (2011)  argues,  Africa  has  historically  been positioned  as  a  'laboratory'  for  Western  experimentation-an  enduring  legacy  of colonial  science  that  can  be  extended  to  contemporary  forms  of  data-driven  technological  intervention.

Together,  these  insights  point  to  the  urgent  need  for  a  socially  critical  and historically  informed  engagement  with  AI.  For  social  work  education,  this  means moving  beyond  narrow  interpretations  of  digital  literacy  to  include  awareness  of the political, cultural, and racialized dimensions of technological systems. Understanding AI not merely as a tool but as a socio-technical formation-rooted in  specific  histories,  values,  and  power  relations-is  essential  to  ensuring  its integration aligns with the profession's commitments to justice, equity, and ethical practice.

## Methodology: integrative literature review

This  study  employs  an  integrative  literature  review  (ILR),  following  the  framework of  Snyder  (2019).  The  ILR  method  enables  the  synthesis  of  diverse  scholarly  contributions-including  empirical  studies,  conceptual  essays,  and  policy  reports-into a  coherent  analytical  framework.  It  is  particularly  well  suited  to  complex  and interdisciplinary  topics,  such  as  the  intersection  of  artificial  intelligence  (AI),  ethics, decolonial  theory,  and  social  work  education.  This  approach  facilitates  not  only descriptive  mapping  but  also  critical  interpretation  of  emerging  trends  and  conceptual  tensions  within  the  field.

## Inclusion criteria

To ensure conceptual relevance and thematic coherence, the following inclusion criteria were applied:

- Sources must be peer-reviewed journal articles, conceptual essays, or authoritative policy reports.
- Publications must be dated between 2020 and 2025, reflecting the rapidly evolving discourse on generative AI.
- Only English-language publications were included due to scope constraints.

## Search strategy

Searches were conducted in the following databases:

- Web of Science
- Google Scholar

A  combination  of  Boolean  operators  and  targeted  terms  was  used  to  refine  the search:

ALL=(('social  work  education'  OR  'social  work  pedagogy'  OR  'social  work curriculum')  AND  ('artificial  intelligence'  OR  'AI'  OR  'machine  learning'  OR 'algorithmic  decision-making')  AND  (2020  OR  2021  OR  2022  OR  2023  OR  2024 OR  2025))

The use of 'AND' was intentional to narrow the scope and identify literature situated explicitly at the intersection of the stated themes. This approach prioritized conceptual precision over breadth, in line with the goals of an integrative review.

Searches were conducted in both Web of Science and Google Scholar. Web of Science was used to identify peer-reviewed literature within established journals, while Google Scholar was used to capture broader interdisciplinary work, including gray literature and preprints. The Boolean string was adapted to fit the requirements of each database.

## Selection and analytical procedure

An initial pool of 60 sources was retrieved. After removing duplicates and applying the inclusion  criteria,  22  sources  were  selected  for  full  review  (see  Table  1).  Screening included  title  and  abstract  review,  followed  by  a  close  reading  to  assess  relevance, methodological quality, and contribution to the central research aim.

To  guide  synthesis,  a  thematic  coding  strategy  was  applied.  Codes  were  developed both deductively (from the research question and theoretical framework) and inductively (from patterns emerging in the literature). Three dominant themes were identified:

- (1) Ethical concerns and algorithmic bias
- (2) Implications for pedagogy and practice in social work education
- (3) Critical perspectives grounded in decolonial theory

## Limitations

This review has several limitations. The focus on English-language sources may exclude valuable perspectives, particularly from regions most affected by algorithmic inequities. In addition, the rapidly evolving AI landscape means that newer publications may not yet have been captured. Finally, the choice to use restrictive Boolean logic ('AND' rather than 'OR') may have narrowed the range of literature identified. However, this was a deliberate methodological decision to ensure conceptual coherence within a highly interdisciplinary and emergent field.

## Results &amp; analysis

## Ethical considerations and algorithmic bias in social work education

As artificial intelligence (AI) technologies increasingly permeate education and professional training, social work faces a critical inflection point. The promise of AI to streamline decision-making, generate teaching content, and personalize learning environments is often matched by ethical concerns. These concerns are not merely hypothetical-they are  embedded  in  the  very  design,  deployment,  and  function  of  AI  tools  that  are  now finding their way into social work classrooms and curricula. This section explores these dilemmas  by  analyzing  academic  literature  that  illuminates  the  ethical  tensions  and algorithmic biases shaping this technological transformation.

Reamer  (2023)  is  among  the  leading  voices  calling  attention  to  the  unique  ethical issues AI raises in social work. Highlighting that AI systems can misrepresent or oversimplify  complex  human  behaviors,  leading  to  decisions  that  may  violate  core  ethical principles such as client autonomy, informed consent, and justice. In social work, where client  context  and  emotional  nuance  are  critical,  the  use  of  automated  tools  risks reducing  individuals  to  mere  data  points.  As  Reamer  emphasizes,  practitioners,  and students  of  social  work,  must  understand  that  AI,  while  technically  efficient,  often lacks  the  capacity  for  moral  discernment  and  socio-cultural  understanding-elements that are foundational to ethical social work practice.

This critique is deepened by Bills (2025), who identifies clear examples of AI misuse in student submissions within social work courses. In analyzing flagged student papers, four dominant  themes  emerged:  misinformation,  citation  inaccuracies,  plagiarism,  and a  striking  homogeneity  in  sources.  This  illustrates  not  only  academic  dishonesty  but also a lack of understanding of what constitutes ethical and informed use of AI technologies.  When  AI-generated  text  is  uncritically  accepted  as  authoritative  or  original,  it undermines academic integrity and risks producing graduates who are ill-equipped to navigate ethical dilemmas in professional practice.

Mohamed et al. (2020) advocate for a decolonial framework for AI ethics. They argue that  contemporary  AI  systems  often  operate  under  assumptions  rooted  in  colonial epistemologies-those that treat Western knowledge systems as universal while marginalizing or excluding others. Their concept of 'algorithmic coloniality' critiques the hidden assumptions within algorithms that reflect racial and geopolitical power imbalances. For example, Western-designed AI systems may reflect normative values of efficiency and control,  which  are  not  necessarily  shared  across  cultures.  In  social  work  education, adopting these tools without critique risks reproducing those very systems of dominance that the field seeks to dismantle.

AI's technical capabilities are also embedded with historical and cultural biases. Chan and Li (2023), in a case study on AI chatbot use for social work training, revealed that while chatbots may help simulate client conversations or roleplays, they lack the ability to empathize, understand trauma, or respond appropriately to nuanced social cues. This limitation  has  serious  ethical  implications.  AI-generated  outputs  may  inadvertently perpetuate racial or gender stereotypes or fail to capture the complexity of lived experiences-particularly those of marginalized populations.

James et al. (2023) bring these critiques together through their discussion of what they call the 'competency/critique dilemma' in social work education. They argue that social


<!-- PAGE 9 -->


work pedagogy is caught between two competing demands: the need to train students to operate within a technologically advanced professional environment and the imperative to foster critical consciousness about the societal structures that shape and are shaped by those technologies. Their proposal for 'digital literacy in its fullest sense' includes both technical  skills  and  a  deep  critical  understanding  of  the  socio-political  implications  of digital tools. In other words, being digitally literate is not just about knowing how to use AI, but also understanding what it does, to whom , and why .

## Pedagogical transformation and curriculum innovation in social work education through AI

The  classroom,  once  defined  by  human-centered  interaction,  reflection,  and  dialogic learning,  is  increasingly  shaped  by  digital  technologies  that  promise  personalization, efficiency,  and  adaptability.  However,  as  this  transformation  unfolds,  it  brings  with  it both pedagogical innovation and significant challenges. The literature reveals a growing consensus that while AI can support teaching and learning in social work education, its uncritical integration risks diluting the relational, ethical, and critical dimensions of the profession.

A  foundational  concern  among  educators  is  the  lack  of  technical  proficiency  and institutional preparedness to implement AI tools meaningfully. Al-Rantisi et al. (2025) conducted a study that highlighted a common frustration: educators often do not feel equipped to use AI in classroom settings. Despite AI's growing relevance, many faculty members lack the training to  integrate  it  into  assignments,  assessments,  or  classroom discourse. This creates a disconnect between the evolving digital demands of the profession and the capacity of academic programs to respond effectively.

This  skill  gap  is  compounded  by  rapidly  shifting  expectations.  Funding  agencies, university administrations, and professional bodies increasingly view AI not as optional but  as  essential  to  educational  innovation.  As  James  et  al.  (2023)  argue,  social  work education is  now  positioned  between  two  mandates:  to  prepare students for a  technologically  saturated  practice  environment  and  to  critically  evaluate  the  sociopolitical  implications  of  these  tools.  Navigating  this  dual  imperative  demands  a  more comprehensive approach to faculty development and curriculum reform.

One of the most debated tools in current educational practice is ChatGPT, a generative AI language model that has rapidly made its way into classrooms. Shklarski and Ray (2024) explored social work educators' perceptions of ChatGPT in teaching. While some educators expressed concern over plagiarism and the superficiality of AI-generated text, others found  that  ChatGPT  could  support  student  learning-especially  for  non-native  English speakers  and  students  needing  assistance  in  articulating  complex  ideas.  The  authors caution, however, that ChatGPT can create a false sense of mastery, where students appear proficient in writing without truly understanding the underlying concepts.

Singer et al. (2023) propose a set of ethical guidelines for integrating ChatGPT into coursework.  They  argue  that  when  framed  correctly,  ChatGPT  can  be  a  productive writing  and  editing  tool.  For  instance,  students  can  use  it  to  generate  project  ideas, improve  grammar,  and  revise  manuscripts.  Likewise,  instructors  can  benefit  by  using ChatGPT  to  create  quizzes,  lectures,  or  feedback  drafts.  Crucially,  the  authors


<!-- PAGE 10 -->


recommend that students be taught how to cross-check and verify AI-generated outputs with peer-reviewed literature to ensure accuracy and relevance.

The  literature  also  highlights  how  AI  is  reshaping  curriculum  design  and  assessment  practices.  Reimer  (2024)  examines  how  generative  AI  tools  impact  assessment formats,  particularly  the  contrast  between  traditional,  face-to-face  viva-style  assessments  and  AI-supported,  text-heavy  assignments.  Social  work,  as  a  discipline,  relies heavily on written communication-for example, in case notes, advocacy documents, or  court  reports.  Therefore,  while  oral  assessments  are  more  resistant  to  AI  misuse, written  assignments  remain  crucial.  Reimer  argues  that  AI  can  assist  students  in improving  their  writing  but  should  be  framed  as  a  supporting  tool  rather  than a  substitute  for  learning.

Báez et al. (2025) take this a step further by documenting how educators are using ChatGPT for curriculum development, grading, lecture preparation, and student mentoring.  While  their  exploratory  study  captures  enthusiasm  around  AI's  potential  to reduce  administrative  workload  and  improve  teaching  materials,  it  also  underscores the  need  for  clear  ethical  boundaries.  The  use  of  AI  in  mentoring  and  grading,  in particular, raises concerns about objectivity, bias, and the erosion of human judgment in educational evaluation.

Rodriguez et al. (2024) make a forward-looking contribution by proposing the integration of AI into the 2029 Educational Policy and Accreditation Standards for social work  education.  They  argue  that  generative  AI  could  enhance  resource  allocation, document  generation,  and  administrative  processing  within  social  work  practice. Consequently, students must be prepared not only to use these tools competently but also to reflect critically on their implications. Their proposal seeks to align accreditation processes  with  the  digital  realities  of  social  work,  recognizing  that  AI  literacy  is  fast becoming a core professional competency.

Perron et al.  (2025) shift  the  focus  from general  AI  use  to the  more  precise  implementation  of  retrieval-augmented  generation  (RAG)  systems.  These  systems  combine generative  AI  with  database  retrieval  functions,  allowing  students  to  access  accurate, context-relevant information more reliably than general-purpose chatbots. The authors argue that RAG-enhanced tools can improve educational quality by grounding responses in trusted sources, thereby reducing the risk of misinformation. Their findings support the  idea  that  AI's  pedagogical  value  increases  significantly  when  it  is  constrained  and curated to match the epistemological standards of the profession.

Dali and Caidi (2022) investigate how accreditation standards in different countries integrate digital literacy. Their comparative study finds inconsistencies between what is expected at the accreditation level and what is actually implemented in classrooms. In many cases, educational institutions promote digital competence in theory but fall short in providing faculty and students with the infrastructure, training, or curricular frameworks  necessary  to  achieve  it.  This  disconnect  signals  a  broader  issue:  that  AI  implementation is often policy-driven but pedagogically underdeveloped.

Dalziel et al. (2024) provide a multidisciplinary perspective on how AI simulations and real-time  feedback  mechanisms  are  being  used  to  support  student  skill  development. Their review notes that students generally respond positively to AI-integrated learning environments, especially those offering tailored feedback and low-stakes opportunities for  skill  rehearsal.  However,  they  also  caution  that  AI  simulations,  if  uncritically

This finding is crucial for educators: as students learn to navigate AI systems, they must  also  be  taught  to  question  and  resist  their  limitations,  particularly  when  those systems conflict with social work's ethical imperatives.

## Decoloniality, data colonialism, and social justice in AI and social work education

As artificial intelligence (AI) technologies permeate educational spaces, a critical question arises: Whose knowledge and values are encoded into these systems? For social work -a discipline historically shaped by colonial structures yet committed to justice, inclusion, and anti-oppression-the growing use of AI presents both challenges and opportunities. This theme explores how AI systems, often developed in the Global North, can reproduce colonial logics in subtle yet powerful ways, and how a decolonial and socially just approach to technology is urgently needed in social work education.

BlackDeer  and  Beeler  (2024)  frame  data  colonialism  as  a  contemporary  mode  of dispossession, warning that data relations are increasingly modeled on the logic of colonial extraction.  For  example,  algorithmic  tools  used  in  child  protection  often  rely  on  biased datasets, leading to disproportionate surveillance and intervention in Indigenous or racialized families. Social work education must prepare students to recognize and resist these patterns. In response to these challenges, scholars and activists are developing decolonial frameworks for AI design and use. One landmark initiative is the Indigenous Protocol and Artificial Intelligence Position Paper authored by Lewis (2020). This document, the result of a convening of First Nations scholars and technologists, presents a vision for AI grounded in Indigenous epistemologies. It challenges dominant, Western techno-scientific assumptions-such as linear progress, objectivity, and human dominance-and instead promotes values such as relationality, reciprocity, and collective well-being.

Lewis's work exemplifies a radical shift in thinking about AI: not merely as a neutral tool, but as a cultural artifact shaped by particular worldviews. When AI tools are built on Indigenous protocols, they resist extraction and promote ethical inclusion. Social work educators can integrate such principles into curricula, ensuring that students are not only technologically literate but also culturally attuned and ethically grounded.

Zembylas (2021) further develops the notion of 'digital-territorial coloniality,' arguing that  digital  platforms  and  AI  technologies  replicate  colonial  hierarchies  through  their structure, logic, and global deployment. He calls for a three-part decolonial approach to AI in education: (1) historicising AI systems as assemblages of colonial knowledge and power,  (2)  cultivating  ethical  solidarity  with  those  harmed  by  AI,  and  (3)  building affective  and  political  communities committed to redesigning AI on decolonial terms. For social work education, this means creating spaces where students can reflect critically on how AI is embedded in systems of inequality and explore alternatives grounded in justice and solidarity.

AI's biases are not limited to data collection-they are also visible in the outputs and representations it  generates.  Phirangee  and  Foster  (2024)  highlight  how  generative  AI tools  like  ChatGPT  and  Google  Bard  have  been  found  to  propagate  racially  biased medical information, particularly concerning Black patients. These biases are not incidental-they stem from the training data, which overwhelmingly reflects white, Western medical models. The consequences can be severe, including the reinforcement of health disparities  and  the  re-inscription  of  medical  racism.  These  findings  expose  how  facial recognition software exhibited much higher error rates for dark-skinned women than for white  men.  The  pattern  is  clear:  AI  systems  trained  on  non-diverse  data  inevitably marginalize  non-dominant  groups.  In  education,  this  manifests  when  AI-generated images or texts center whiteness as normative or portray Black professionals in limited, stereotypical roles. Such representations shape student perception and risk reproducing racial hierarchies within the classroom.

For social work, a field  committed to anti-racist and culturally competent practice, these  dynamics  are  ethically  unacceptable.  AI  tools  must  be  interrogated  not  only  for their functionality but for their ideological content-the assumptions they encode and the futures they make imaginable or foreclosed.

So what does a decolonial AI pedagogy look like? BlackDeer and Beeler (2024) offer several suggestions. First, educators must move beyond deficit-based statistical models that treat individuals as isolated data points. Instead, they should emphasize collective knowledge  production,  local  languages,  and  community-based  narratives.  This  shift would  allow  social  work  students  to  engage  with  data  not  as  a  top-down  exercise  in control, but as a dialogical process rooted in relationship and accountability.

Second,  curricula  should  include  real-time,  community-driven  datasets  rather  than relying exclusively on administrative or institutional data. This encourages students to value lived experience and recognize the political nature of knowledge production. It also supports  the  development  of  critical  scholars  who  can  navigate  both  quantitative  and qualitative methods while maintaining a commitment to justice.

Third, educators must foreground the colonial origins of statistical thinking, which were historically used to classify, rank, and control populations. Reamer (2023) and Cave and Dihal (2020) both point to figures like Francis Galton, whose work on eugenics laid the foundation for many modern algorithmic systems. By teaching students to question the philosophical and historical underpinnings of AI, social work programs can equip them to challenge the very assumptions embedded in datafication itself.

Some scholars  propose  concrete  pedagogical  models  to  resist  the  coloniality  of  AI. Boetto (2025), for example, introduces the EPIC model, consisting of Ethics and justice (E),  Policy  development  and  advocacy  (P),  Intersectoral  collaboration  (I),  and Community engagement (C). This model aims to equip social workers  with both the critical  tools  and  practical  strategies  to  navigate  AI-augmented  practice  environments ethically and inclusively. It provides a structure for resisting the replication of harm while leveraging the potential of technology to support communities.

This aligns with Rodriguez et al. (2024), who emphasize the importance of embedding AI into accreditation standards for social work programs. If used critically and ethically, AI could help students build advocacy campaigns, conduct risk assessments, or analyze policy impact. However, this can only occur if the education they receive trains them to

see AI not as a neutral facilitator, but as a site of struggle, deeply embedded in social, racial, and geopolitical hierarchies.

## Illustrative case: generating digital literacy

To explore the potential bias underlying AI interpretations, this section provides examples of images generated by three different AI language systems. The aim is not to offer an exhaustive or detailed contextual analysis, as seen in Park's (2024) work in art education. Park references critical content theorist Salvaggio (2022), who argues that AI images are 'data  patterns  inscribed  into  pictures.'  As  with  text,  we  must  analyze  the  implications behind these images and the assumptions inherent in the generated content.

Aligning  with  Salvaggio  (2022,  p.  50),  we  can  identify  strong  connections  between images by examining 'certain compositions/arrangements, colour schemes, lighting effects, figures  or  poses,  or  other  expressive  elements'  prominent  in  the  sample  set.  We  should consider  what  is  missing,  the  weaknesses  present,  and  the  assumptions  and  biases  conveyed. While Salvaggio does not focus specifically on social work, this perspective is useful for analyzing how professional identities may be represented-and potentially distortedwithin AI-generated imagery. Specifically, we can question how social work is represented in images generated by AI. By critically engaging with these images, we can 'open the hood' and investigate  the  training  data  and  knowledge  sources  that  generative  AI  models  like ChatGPT 4 utilize. We must assess what the AI captures effectively and what it overlooks.

According to Salvaggio (2022, p. 51), we should contemplate how social work can play a  role  in  ensuring  that  AI  systems  are  designed  to  address  biases  and  omissions.  As 'cultural,  social,  economic,  and  political  artefacts,'  Salvaggio  emphasized  that  AIgenerated  images  should  be  interpreted  as  maps  of  biases.  While  this  paper  does  not aim to provide an extensive analysis of AI bias in social  work  imagery, the  generated images  serve  as  a  reminder  of  the  biases  identified  by  other  researchers  and  can  be a useful way for educators to create discussions with their students on these issues within AI. By  applying  the  following  questions,  we  can  begin  to  critically  interrogate  the images  generated  by  AI  above.  First,  we  can  ask  the  most  obvious  question: What do you see? Who is the image of, and how would you describe them ? Of the five images, three  portray  a  white  person,  generated  in  response  to  prompts  such  as boss , overworked ,  and well-paid .  When  the  command  was  changed  to  generate  an  image  of a  Black  social  worker-following  earlier  prompts  that  produced  images  of  white professionals-the  output  generated  by  ChatGPT-4  depicted  a  Black  woman  in what  appears  to  be  a  classroom  setting.  This  contrasts  with  images  produced  by other  AI  systems  in  the  sample,  highlighting  how  different  models  may  reproduce bias  in  distinct  ways.

Secondly, we can look for patterns in how social work practice is represented in these images. Are there recurring visual cues such as posture, clipboards, clothing styles, or name badges? We can continue to deepen the critical perspectives by asking the following questions:

- Who or what is present or dominant in the image?
- Who or what is missing, marginalized, or misrepresented?
- How are these patterns connected to existing social norms or stereotypes in contemporary social work?

These  questions  lead  us  to  reflect  on  why,  for  instance,  the  AI  placed  a  Black  social worker in a classroom setting, or why it generated  an image of a  Black social worker exclusively  within  a  Black  community.  This  is  not  to  say  these  representations  are inherently  wrong,  but  through  a  decolonial  lens  we  can  unpack  them  with  greater nuance. Why didn't the AI depict a Black social worker within a white community-or consider other possible combinations?

AI systems generate images based on available datasets, which are themselves drawn from the internet and existing cultural norms. These kinds of activities can help students begin to see that AI is not a neutral tool. It reflects and reproduces the biases embedded in the digital archives it draws upon-including racial discrimination that has existed since colonial times.

This is not to say that AI-especially language and image generation models-cannot be  trained  with  more  inclusive  and  culturally  informed  datasets.  But  such  efforts  are time-consuming and resource-intensive. While the images shown here are just a simple way to illustrate these complexities, there are other important considerations for social work educators. These include, but not limited to, ethics, dividing practices, and whiteness-three  key  areas  that  have  been  explored  by,  for  example,  Adams  (2021)  but  also relate to the images generated above.

## Conclusion

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into social work education does not simply represent  a  technological  shift-it  signifies  a  profound  epistemic  and  ethical  turning point. As this paper has argued, the urgency is not merely in adapting to new tools, but in asking what  kinds  of  knowledge  should  now  be  prioritised in  preparing  future  social workers. At stake is more than technological fluency. The real task is to develop a form


<!-- PAGE 15 -->


<!-- image -->

of digital literacy that is experiential, relational, community-connected, and responsive to the deep complexities of social work practice.

The  three  thematic  areas  explored  in  this  paper-ethical  and  algorithmic  accountability,  pedagogical  transformation,  and  decolonial  and  social  justice  perspectivestogether with the illustrative case example, offer a generative framework for reimagining what digital literacy can look like in social work education. Each theme contributes a vital piece to this puzzle, and when taken together, they point toward a transformative agenda.

As discussed in the first theme, ethics cannot remain an afterthought or a checklist once AI is implemented. Ethical engagement must be foundational-infused into how we design , teach with , and evaluate digital technologies. Many scholars (e.g. Mohamed et al., 2020;  Reamer,  2023)  warn  that  ethical  discourse  in  AI  is  often  shallow-reduced  to abstract principles or vague corporate responsibility statements. Instead, ethical digital literacy must encompass questions of power, context, and harm. Students must learn to interrogate  the  assumptions  that  shape  algorithms,  such  as  which  populations  are considered 'high risk' and why, or what biases are embedded in training data. As James et al. (2023) argue, this kind of critical digital literacy extends beyond tool use to include the social form and technical implications of AI as it remakes the world.

This approach calls for embedding ethics into practice-not only in content but in form.  Educators  must  model  ethical  engagement  by  choosing  platforms  responsibly, discussing surveillance and consent openly, and centering marginalized voices in digital discussions. Only then can students be prepared not just to comply with ethical norms, but to act as agents of ethical transformation in their professional lives.

The second theme in this paper emphasized the potential and challenges of pedagogical innovation in an AI-saturated environment. Social work pedagogy has traditionally emphasized relationality, dialogue, empathy, and critical reflection. These values must not be abandoned in the face of digital change. Instead, they should be reinterpreted and reinforced through intentional integration of AI into learning environments.

AI presents powerful opportunities: from personalized feedback and adaptive simulations (Dalziel et al., 2024) to support with administrative tasks and content generation (Báez et al., 2025). When used well, these tools can free educators to focus more deeply on fostering student growth and contextual reflection. However, as Shklarski and Ray (2024) and Reimer (2024) caution, uncritical reliance on AI tools risks promoting superficial engagement and a false sense of mastery. Writing that appears fluent may mask a lack of conceptual depth. Algorithmic feedback may overlook the affective and ethical dimensions of social work work.

The goal is not to reject AI, but to engage with it thoughtfully, critically, and creatively. Educators  need institutional  support  to  experiment  with  AI  tools  in  pedagogically  sound ways. This includes curriculum space to discuss AI explicitly, guidance on academic integrity, and room to test new forms of assessment that resist automation-such as reflective journals, community-based  projects,  and  oral  vivas.  Most  importantly,  students  must  be  coparticipants in shaping how AI is used in their learning, not passive consumers of technology.

The third theme explored the importance of decolonial theory and social justice in framing digital literacy. As scholars such as Zembylas (2021), and Lewis (2020) remind us, AI systems do not emerge in a vacuum. They are built on and often reproduce longstanding  colonial  structures  of  classification,  surveillance,  and  control.  In  this  sense, digital literacy must be not only technical and ethical, but also epistemological.

<!-- image -->

A  decolonial  approach  to  AI  education  challenges  who  gets  to  define  intelligence, truth, and evidence. It critiques the whiteness of AI (Cave &amp; Dihal, 2020) and confronts the ways in which Indigenous, Black, and other marginalized knowledges are excluded, flattened, or misrepresented in AI training data and outputs. For social work education, this  means  teaching  students  to  not  only  recognize  bias,  but  to  reimagine  technology from the margins-centering lived experience, linguistic fidelity, and collective knowledge creation.

Pedagogically, this could involve drawing on Indigenous data sovereignty protocols (Lewis, 2020), engaging students in critiques of data colonialism (BlackDeer &amp; Beeler, 2024),  or  examining  how  algorithmic  systems  disproportionately  impact  racialized communities.  It  also  means  building  digital  assignments  that  are  context-rich,  localized, and community-accountable-resisting the logic of scale and abstraction that so often  defines  edtech.

What becomes clear is that increasing digital literacy in social work education is not only possible-it is imperative. But this literacy must be reconceived as a transformative priority,  not  a  technical  afterthought.  Each  of  the  themes  discussed  in  this  paper contributes to that reimagining:

- From ethical  engagement,  we  learn  to  ask: What are the  values  and  assumptions embedded in the tools we use?
- From pedagogical innovation, we ask: How can AI be used to support deep, relational, and critically reflective learning?
- From decolonial and justice-based approaches, we ask: Whose knowledge is included or excluded, and who benefits or is harmed?

Answering these questions together cultivates a digital literacy that is aligned with the core values of social work-empathy, justice, community, and care.

Future research should build on this conceptual framework through empirical studies that examine how digital literacy is enacted in social work classrooms and practice settings. In particular, there is a need for, (1) comparative studies across different national and institutional  contexts;  (2)  research  on  how  students  critically  engage  with  AI  tools  in  real-time learning  environments;  and  (3)  the  development  and  evaluation  of  pedagogical  models grounded in decolonial and justice-oriented  approaches  to  digital  technology.  Such  work would strengthen the evidence base and support the ongoing transformation of social work education in the age of artificial intelligence.

In  the  end,  AI  technologies  are  not  inherently  oppressive  or  liberatory-they  are shaped by the intentions, worldviews, and politics of those who build and implement them.  For  social  work  education,  the  challenge  is  to  ensure  that  these  tools  do  not reproduce  harm  or  erode  professional  ethics  but  are  reclaimed  and  reconfigured  in service of equity, dignity, and liberation.
