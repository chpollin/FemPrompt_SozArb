Equity-oriented uses of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) remain largely underexplored. To address the persistent gender inequities in girls' representation and participation in computing, we present a case study of an all-girls initiative incorporating two GenAI tools in a European makerspace. We analysed qualitative, multimodal data,  including  15  interviews  with  girls,  parents,  and  practitioners,  four  reflective  sessions,  two  observation fieldnotes,  and  44  GenAI-generated  images.  Harding's  (1986)  multi-level  framework  and  feminist  pedagogy provide critical lenses through which to examine and discuss the practice and practitioners' reflections. Stakeholders generally expressed positive views of the all-girls workshop format. Practitioners enacted three steps to support girls' participation and engagement in GenAI workshops, namely representation, relationship building, and a responsive approach. However, several issues and challenges emerged in practice, including an inappropriate workshop topic, limited peer collaboration, the inaccessibility of the selected GenAI tools, and language barriers  associated  with  prompt-writing.  These  challenges  revealed  underlying  issues  related  to  the  marginalisation of girls' voices, agency, and assets. We argue that gender-equity interventions in computing need to move beyond the superficial ' painting in pink ' of girlification and instead centre girls' voices, agency, and assets through changes across symbolic, structural, social, and individual levels. The study proposes a girl-centred, multi-level framework and practical implications for the use of GenAI to promote gender equity in computing, while also calling for further research and practice on equity-oriented applications of GenAI. This paper deepens understanding of what it means to centre girls in computing and contributes new empirical and critical insights into how GenAI may be leveraged to advance gender equity and inform the design of related interventions.

Drawing on  insights  from  critical  feminist  theory  and  feminist  pedagogy,  we conceptualise gender equity not merely as equitable opportunities and access for girls and women, but as a deeper commitment to identifying, challenging, and transforming systemic and structural barriers, power relations,  and  cultural  norms and to empowering girls to enact their agency for active, meaningful, and sustained participation in computing.

One of these computing intervention efforts has been the delivery of all-girls programmes. The all-girls format enables the creation of space in  which  only  girls  participate,  deliberately  designed  to  redress  the male-dominated  field's  tendency  to  marginalise  and  exclude  girls/ women, by reducing girls' situational stress in mixed gender environments  and  supporting  girls'  interest,  confidence,  and  participation (Fuller et al., 2013). This format arguably allows for a  more gender-inclusive, welcoming, and safe environment for girls (Lang et al., 2020; Sultan et al., 2024). Previous studies indicate that all-girls initiatives can support girls' interests, confidence, practical applications of technology, sense of belonging, and identity construction in computing (e.g., Fuller et al., 2013; Lang et al., 2020; Pinkard et al., 2017; Sultan et  al.,  2024).  In  this  paper,  we  aim  to  critically  analyse  an  all-girls initiative comprising two series of in-person workshops conducted by a  makerspace  in  a  European  urban  setting.  These  workshops  were explicitly designed by women practitioners to increase girls' representation and participation in their programmes. The first series focused on 3D modelling, while the second series was about using GenAI in relation to climate change (see more about the case context in Section 3.1). Two GenAI tools (i.e., Playground and Deforum; see a brief introduction to the  tools  in  Section  3.1)  were  integrated  into  the  second  series  of workshops. GenAI refers to a type of AI that uses advanced technologies, such as neural networks, large language models, and image generators, to create original outputs (e.g., text, images, or videos) based on patterns learned from extensive training data (Lasker, 2024; Lim et al., 2023).

While  emerging  evidence  has  begun  to  show  positive  impacts  of using GenAI on learning outcomes in computing education (e.g., Shirafuji et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024; Yilmaz &amp; Karaoglan Yilmaz, 2023), very little attention has been given to the equity-oriented use of GenAI for girls and women. Given an increasing interest and rapid expansion of GenAI applications in education, it is essential to critically assess how the technology and related tools may influence diverse, inclusive, and equitable  participation  of  marginalised  groups  of  learners  (Varsik &amp; Vosberg, 2024). Only a few studies have recently suggested the potential use of GenAI for equitable participation, such as offering diverse role models and personalised and adaptive support (e.g., Nixon et al., 2024). Notably, many current GenAI tools were not originally developed for educational purposes, often overlooking diverse individual needs. They are also likely to reproduce and reinforce gender biases and stereotypes through  their  training  data  and  algorithms  (Browne,  2023;  Dwivedi et al., 2023). Without careful examination and equitable considerations, GenAI use in educational practice, particularly when requiring certain digital skills and access costs, may just serve privileged groups ' needs and interests and may reinforce their strengths. Thus, there remains a pressing need for empirical and practical insights into how practitioners can harness GenAI to foster gender equity in computing.

In response to the identified research gap and critical considerations, our study aims to explore how stakeholders perceive the opportunities and challenges of running all-girls workshops with GenAI for gender equity in computing. Thus, we asked two research questions:

- How  do  multiple  stakeholders  perceive  the  all-girls  workshop format?
- What are  the  practitioners'  reflections  on  fostering  gender  equity through the delivery of the all-girls workshops with certain GenAI tools?

The  second  research  question,  which  focuses  on  GenAI  practice, seeks to understand the perceived good practice, challenges, and areas for  improvement  specifically  from  the  perspective  of  practitioners. Through our analysis of the GenAI workshops focusing on identifying key steps taken by practitioners, alongside practitioners ' post-practice reflections,  we aim to offer critical and practical insights into GenAI use  for  gender  equity.  Building  on  the  analytical  insights,  feminist pedagogy principles (Webb et al., 2002), and relevant research (e.g., Nag Chowdhuri et al., 2024; Vossoughi, 2017), this paper concludes with  implications  for  practitioners,  parents,  and  AI  developers  to develop gender equity-oriented GenAI practice.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Gender inequity in computing participation

Computing is a field marked by persistent gender inequities in access, representation, participation, and progression. In this male-dominated field,  the  underrepresentation  of  girls  and  women  in  education  and employment has been a key global concern (e.g., OECD Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation, 2018; UNESCO, 2021). The underrepresentation issue often worsens as girls progress to more advanced levels of computing education (Holmegaard et al., 2024; Kemp et al., 2019),  resulting  in  a leaky  pipeline (Alper,  1993)  towards  careers  in computing. The recent Global Gender Gap Report indicates that women make up just 28% of the technology workforce (World Economic Forum, 2024).

Several  factors  contribute  to  this  chronic  gender  imbalance  in computing. First, gender stereotypes and biases within the computing field 2 (Bourdieu, 1990) discourage and exclude girls and young women. For instance, an ' ideal student ' (Wong &amp; Chiu, 2021) in computing is often stereotypically associated with masculine traits, such as ' brainy ' and ' geeky ' (Gaucher et al., 2011). Such a stereotype is associated with the construction of computing as a field concerning what is recognised and valued and who it is for (Holmegaard et al., 2024; Vitores &amp; Gil-Ju ´ arez, 2016). Research has further found that stereotypes negatively influence girls' and women's self-efficacy, internal attribution of failure, sense of belonging, and identity construction (e.g., Cheryan et al., 2017; Koch et al., 2008; McGuire et al., 2020; S ´ ainz &amp; Eccles, 2012; Wong, 2016). Holmegaard et al. (2024) argue that young people experiencing a misalignment  between  identity,  the  field  of  computing,  and  capital (cultural, economic, and social resources) tend to feel ' the weight of water ' , and are less likely to experience computing as being ' for me ' (p. 5).

Second, masculine-dominated culture makes the climate chilly (Hall &amp; Sandler, 1982) and hostile (Stout et al., 2017) to girls and women. Similarly, the genderisation of machines, materials, tools, and the language used by practitioners and participants could also exclude girls' and women's participation (Eckhardt et al., 2021). In such non-inclusive environments,  girls  and  women  are  more  likely  to  experience  peer sexism and gendered microaggressions, such as having their contributions undervalued, judged, or overlooked by male peers (e.g., DuBow &amp; Kaminsky, 2019; Shinnick, 2019). The negative effects are likely to be amplified for those experiencing intersectional (Crenshaw, 1989) challenges  associated  with  socio-economic  status,  disability,  sexuality, race/ethnicity, religion, and social class (Perez-Felkner et al., 2024).

Third, a lack of access is a key barrier to gender equity in digital/

## 2.2. Supporting gender equity: empirical evidence and theoretical insights

There has been a growing number of educational interventions to increase girls' and women's participation in computing. Many


<!-- PAGE 4 -->


interventions have focused on supporting individuals in a motivational domain (e.g., interest, confidence, self-efficacy, aspiration) through the enactment of changes in computing content and pedagogical approaches (Perez-Felkner et al., 2024). However, these interventions tend to lack sustainable impact. For example, the Computer Clubs for Girls (CC4G) in England (for ages 10 -14) increased participating girls' interest; however,  it  did  not  significantly  increase  the  number  of  girls  taking  up  IT courses or careers after the age of 16 and change their perceptions of IT as  a  career  for  women  (Fuller  et  al.,  2013).  This  suggests  that  interventions framed by deficit approaches aiming to change young people  are  less  helpful  in  developing  equitable  practice  and  sustaining participation of girls and women in STEM (Archer et al., 2021, 2025). The chilly climate and systemic barriers persist (Vitores &amp; Gil-Ju ´ arez, 2016) without changes in social and structural domains. This is supported by critical feminist theory that emphasises the need to work towards change to power hierarchies,  systemic inequalities,  and  social structures that produce and sustain inequities in women's participation (Martin,  2003;  Stevens &amp; Martell,  2019).  Hence,  critical  attention should be paid to changing the computing field and norms (e.g., Archer et al., 2021; Fuller et al., 2013), as reflected in those environmental-level interventions to make girls and women feel more included and valued (HG Foundation, 2024).

To foster a gender-inclusive computing environment, the ' girls-only/ all-girls ' format has been recently employed in computing/technology initiatives, such as the Digital Divas Club in Australia (Fisher et al., 2016; Lang et al., 2020), CC4G in the UK (Fuller et al., 2013), a technology camp in Sweden (Sultan et al., 2024), and Digital Youth Divas (Pinkard et al., 2017) and Team Mensa of SciGirls Code (Barksdale et al., 2022) in the US. Table 1 presents key contextual information and effects of these interventions. Synthesised from these examples, the all-girls initiatives were found to support girls ' interest, engagement, confidence, sense of belonging, and identity construction in computing and technology, as well as the development of problem-solving and teamwork skills. This may be because the all-girls format creates a safer and less competitive environment for girls away from boys (Lang et al., 2020; Sultan et al., 2024). It also creates supportive communities where girls gain support from female role models and peer mentors.

Furthermore,  with  a  focus  on  social  interaction  and  community building, research has reported positive effects of collaborative activities, such as group projects and pair programming, on girls and women. For example, group work is an essential component of Team Mensa (i.e., a  nine-month  out-of-school  computer  science  programme  for  middle school girls in the US; see more details in Table 1) and has been shown to support the development of girls ' problem-solving and teamwork skills, broaden their peer networks, and facilitate the exchange of diverse ideas (Barksdale et al., 2022). Related to computational perspectives (Brennan &amp; Resnick, 2012), Barksdale et al. (2022) further note that group work particularly fosters  the  connecting dimension, which emphasises recognising the value and power of creating with and for others. Engaging in computing collaboratively can change the stereotype that computing is a solitary activity (Diekman et al., 2011). In addition, collaborative learning and working opportunities can highlight the interdisciplinary nature  of  computing  (HG  Foundation,  2024),  thereby  demonstrating multiple pathways into the field. The benefits of collaboration can also be understood from a connected learning perspective (Ito et al., 2013), which suggests that collaborative activities enable girls to connect their interests, relationships, and opportunities to the learning in question. Essentially, computing has been conceptualised as a social and collaborative practice that needs to ' offer a context for making applications of significance  for  others,  communities  in  which  design  sharing  and collaboration with others are paramount ' (Kafai, 2016, p. 26).

Despite these positive effects, challenges and limitations are worthy of attention and critical reflection. Harding (1986) argues that gender in relation to science and technology is produced on three interconnected levels: the symbolic level (e.g., linguistic expressions, media representation, cultural norms), the institutional/structural level (e.g., policy, the structure and organisation of technology teaching, entry requirements for an internship), and the individual level (e.g., identity, self-efficacy, sense of belonging). This framework is useful for understanding how the  relationship  between  gender  and  technology  is  socially  situated, fluid,  and  context-dependent  (Wajcman,  2010).  Sultan  et  al.  (2024) further propose the inclusion of a social level and argue that this newly added level helps understand the interconnections between the three levels  within  a  social  context.  They  found  that  a  supportive  social environment in an out-of-school technology camp enabled girls to see themselves as technical and develop a sense of social belonging. The four interconnected levels suggest that addressing gender inequalities at one level  alone  is  insufficient.  While  the  all-girls  interventions  focus  on supporting  girls  at  the  individual  level  and  making  changes  at  the symbolic level, they tend to overlook systemic and social structures that perpetuate  gender  inequalities.  Drawing  on  critical  feminist  theory, Beck et al. (2022) similarly note that many mentoring programmes have a narrow focus on gender by introducing peer mentors of women rather than challenging deeper structural inequalities. As noted by Fuller et al. (2013), their CC4G intervention that focuses on tackling issues of access, girls ' confidence, and negative attitudes about IT lacks sustained and significant impact. They also argue that interventions ' seeing girls as the problem ' (p.  499)  from  a  deficit-based  perspective  are  problematic; instead, they call for interventions that critically challenge the social and cultural discourses underpinning persistent gender divides.

Moreover, Sultan et al. (2024) introduced the concept of girlification and critically examined girlified activities and technology in their study of  an  all-girls  technology  camp  in  Sweden.  Girlification refers  to the transformation of traditionally male-dominated or gender-neutral content into explicitly feminine-coded forms to attract girls (Sultan et al., 2024).  Such  girlified  activities  (e.g.,  fashion,  lipstick  making)  and materials/tools, while intended to attract and sustain girls' interest, were found  to  give  girls  a  partial  or  unrealistic  image  of  educational  and career paths and areas in computing (Sultan et al., 2024). Aligned with Harding's (1986) framework, girlification can be understood as a superficial strategy, namely painting something in pink (Heybach &amp; Pickup, 2017) or providing computing in a female wrapper (Fuller et al., 2013). Such approaches reflect intentional yet limited efforts by practitioners to engage girls,  but  they  often  fail  to  challenge  deep-rooted  gender  inequalities at the structural level or to transform the inherently gendered nature of the field. Additionally, girlification may be shaped by intervention designers' and practitioners' assumptions about what appeals to girls, rather than genuinely centring girls' own voices and perspectives. These assumptions may be incorrect and fail to reflect the diversity of girls' interests (Salas-Morera et al., 2021). For example, girlified activities  that  lack  problem-solving  elements  but  emphasise  social  games may reinforce the stereotype that girls prefer social over technical aspects  (Sultan  et  al.,  2019).  Therefore,  girlification  may  reproduce  or even  reinforce  gender  stereotypes  in  computing  (Fuller  et  al.,  2013; Sultan et al., 2024).

In response to this, centring the assets/strengths of girls and women is  fundamentally  important.  The  asset-based  pedagogical  approach, which values the expertise, lived experiences, and community wisdom (Calabrese Barton &amp; Tan, 2018) of excluded and minoritised groups of young people, can shift perspectives away from a deficit view of girls and women needing more interest, skills,  mentoring,  or  role  models (Dawson et al., 2024). Jiang et al. ' s (2024) research found that marginalised female students outperformed their peers in machine learning practices that integrated diverse cultural perspectives. These students demonstrated unique strengths in holistic language analysis, noticing writers' intentions and cultural nuances. The distinct perspectives and linguistic and cultural insights of marginalised girls and women may not only  transform  their  own  learning  experiences,  but  also  potentially revolutionise conventional paradigms in computing (Jiang et al., 2024; Vrieler &amp; Salminen-Karlsson, 2022).

Moreover, youth voice and agency should be foregrounded in practice. As one of the key principles of equitable STEM practice (Archer


<!-- PAGE 5 -->


et al., 2021; Nag Chowdhuri et al., 2024), fostering youth agency can support them in deciding on what computing learning and career should be like for them. Co-production is a practical approach that values youth agency;  young  people  work  together  with  practitioners  and  peers  to co-create  and  co-deliver  an  activity,  programme  or  output  (Nag Chowdhuri et al., 2024). In this way, they become decision-makers and change agents rather  than  recipients  or  consumers  of  predetermined content. The aforementioned insights identified from previous successful interventions echo some of the key principles of feminist pedagogy, including power sharing, collaboration, community building, empowerment, and privileging individual voice (Webb et al., 2002). Feminist pedagogy emphasises the need to create a participatory, inclusive, and experiential  learning  community  that  values  students ' voice  (Musil, 2008) and empowers them to challenge traditional norms (Koseoglu, 2020). Importantly, Cannizzo (2021) claims that feminist pedagogy is not only a pedagogical approach but also a way of thinking, learning and teaching aimed at fostering personal growth and driving social change.

## 2.3. Generative AI applications for equity: opportunities and concerns

Since the release of ChatGPT in November 2022, GenAI has received increasing attention and heated discussion in education. GenAI tools do not  necessarily  require  technical  knowledge  and  programming  skills, relying  instead  on  user  prompts  to  generate  diverse  outputs.  Their human-like capabilities and user-friendly design have also sparked great interest among researchers and practitioners exploring educational applications.  In  the  computing  education  context,  emerging  evidence shows several benefits and positive outcomes of using GenAI, including improved computational thinking and understanding of programming concepts, programming self-efficacy and motivation, code restructuring and formatting, and metacognitive and self-regulated learning skills (e. g., Phung et al., 2023; Shirafuji et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024; Yilmaz &amp; Karaoglan Yilmaz, 2023). However, existing studies primarily focus on formal education settings (Zhang et al., 2024). Evidence is limited in informal learning contexts, such as makerspaces, which may have more flexibility for educational innovation and transformation. Furthermore, historically marginalised groups of young people, such as girls and racial minorities, remain overlooked in GenAI educational research. It remains unclear  whether  these  applications  of  GenAI  in  computing  may  also support, benefit, or harm girls and women. As Zhang et al. (2024) note in their  systematic  review,  existing  research  predominantly  focuses  on improving  teaching  and  learning  effectiveness,  lacking  attention  to justice-oriented  uses  of  GenAI  to  support  minoritised  youth  in  more equitable ways.

Only a few recent studies have begun to explore GenAI's potential to foster more diverse, inclusive, and equitable participation in STEM (e.g., Nixon et al., 2024). For example, Nixon et al. (2024) provide a roadmap outlining  several  promising  applications  of  GenAI,  such  as  AI-driven simulations  of  diverse  role  models,  human-AI  teaming  (e.g.,  positioning GenAI as a collaborative teammate), GenAI-integrated collaborative problem-solving, and adaptive, personalised support tailored to diverse participants. These applications are argued to have the potential to challenge gender stereotypes, mitigate biased participation patterns and  the  negative  peer  evaluation  often  experienced  by  women  in mixed-gender  teams  by  supporting  more  equitable  turn-taking,  feedback,  and  interaction.  However,  such  proposed  applications  remain largely conceptual and have not yet been robustly evidenced in empirical research, particularly in situated educational contexts. This suggests a need for more empirical research into the effects of using GenAI for gender equity in STEM. Moreover, Nixon et al. (2024) primarily focus on proposing solutions, while not explicitly considering the inherent gender biases embedded within GenAI itself (e.g., Smith &amp; Rustagi, 2021).

Probably unlike other digital technologies, critical attention should be  particularly  drawn  to  GenAI's  inherent  inequity  issues  related  to training  data,  algorithm  design,  and  optimisation  processes,  when attempting to harness it for gender equity. Firstly, training data in GenAI

systems from real life often contain gender biases and discriminatory information,  potentially  leading  to  fake  or  inappropriate  outputs (Dwivedi et al., 2023). Data labelling involves subjective human judgments, further embedding these biases (Smith &amp; Rustagi, 2021). Second, algorithms may reflect gender biases inherent in their datasets, variables,  and  algorithmic  rules.  44%  of  133  AI  systems  across  different industries demonstrate gender bias (Smith &amp; Rustagi, 2021). Notably, decisions regarding training datasets, variables, and rules are predominantly made by developers; however, there are only 30 per cent women currently working in AI (World Economic Forum, 2023). This suggests that women's voices in decision-making processes are underrepresented. The  aforementioned  inherent  issues  reflect  a  mutually  shaping  relationship between technology and gender. On the one hand, technology, as a sociotechnical product, is shaped by the operation of gender interests or identities; on the other hand, technologies implying certain uses can afford or constrain gender power relations (Wajcman, 2010).

In addition, the untraceable process of algorithmic decision-making has been identified as structural injustice, systematically disadvantaging marginalised individuals (Browne, 2023). Responding to these concerns, critical  equity-focused  evaluations  of  GenAI  tools  are  necessary  to ' avoid exacerbating existing disparities or creating new ones ' (Varsik &amp; Vosberg, 2024, p. 7). Calls are being made to critically look at who develops AI tools, what data is used and how it is applied, and how the algorithm is used (UN Women, 2024).

Furthermore, research has called for critical attention to ensuring equal access to GenAI and its related infrastructure (e.g., digital devices, internet connection) (Varsik &amp; Vosberg, 2024; Yang et al., 2024). Subscription fees for premium GenAI services create financial barriers for individuals from low socio-economic backgrounds. Language barriers also  pose  significant  challenges;  prompts  written  in  English  produce better responses than those in other languages (Dey et al., 2024). Language proficiency and visual literacy also influence the quality of images generated by AI models (Lee et al., 2024). These various barriers to accessing and effectively using GenAI may hinder the participation of girls  and  women,  especially  those  facing  intersectional  (Crenshaw, 1989) challenges. Despite growing attention to equal access, little  is known about how girls and women experience the use of GenAI tools in practice, what intersectional challenges they encounter, and how these experiences shape their participation and identity in computing.

Overall, while the existing literature highlights GenAI's pedagogical benefits in learning outcomes (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024) and suggests potential applications for promoting equity in STEM (e.g., Nixon et al., 2024), three key research gaps remain: (i) a limited understanding of GenAI effects in informal education contexts, such as makerspaces; (ii) little empirical evidence concerning the experience of girls and women in using GenAI; and relatedly (iii) insufficient critical examination of GenAI  and  its  use  from  the  perspective  of  gender  power  relations, particularly  in  ways  that  could  inform  equity-oriented  practices  in computing  education.  Therefore,  our  study  intends  to  address  the research gaps and focuses on critically analysing an all-girls initiative in a  European  makerspace  that  incorporated  GenAI  to  advance  gender equity in computing. Explicitly driven by a commitment to gender equity, it is important to bring support for girls and women to the forefront while exploring and examining how to leverage GenAI meaningfully. This is not just to address the current research gaps, but, more importantly,  to  avoid  reproducing  or  even  exacerbating  gender  inequities within the computing field.

## 3. Methods

We  employed  a  qualitative  case  study  approach  (Baxter &amp; Jack, 2008; Yin, 2014) to report and analyse the all-girls initiative run by a makerspace in an urban European city. This makerspace was one of six makerspaces  from  five  countries  in  our  two-year,  research-practice partnership project. This project, foregrounding equity and social justice,  aimed  to  co-develop  equitable  practices  that  can  support  the


<!-- PAGE 6 -->


engagement  of  marginalised  young  people  in  STEM.  Guided  by  the research  questions,  a  variety  of  data  sources  were  used  for  analysis, including  interviews,  observations,  reflective  sessions  with  practitioners,  and  workshop  artefacts  (see  more  in  Section  3.2).  The  data showing different perspectives enable the comparison, corroboration, and  convergence  of  data,  thereby  allowing  for  multiple  facets  of  a phenomenon to be revealed and better understood (Baxter &amp; Jack, 2008; Yin, 2014). We first introduce the case context, including the makerspace and initiative.

## 3.1. Case context

This makerspace has been developing for over twenty years, supporting young people in exploring intersections between science, art, technology, and society and developing their own making. As a nonprofit and non-governmental cultural organisation, its development relies on a range of sources of funding from the public, private, and third sectors. The physical space for running youth programmes is located in a warehouse, occupying an entire floor with a small laboratory, a workshop, a relaxation area with refreshments, an office, and a gallery area showing artists ' work. Advanced equipment, such as 3D printers and laser cutting machines, are placed in the laboratory.

The makerspace has demonstrated its commitment to gender equity through initiatives such as developing a community for girls, women and  non-binary  individuals  working  in  technology,  science,  and  art (including  intermedia,  digital  art).  Free  workshops  are  provided  for children and young people (aged 8 -16) to develop STEM skills, creative thinking, and innovations. Their youth programmes advocate for handson experimentation, trans-disciplinary work, do-it-together and do-ityourself methods, and an open sharing ethos. However, practitioners noticed that their youth programmes were predominantly attended by White boys from local, middle-class families. They hoped to recruit and involve more girls, aligning with their gender equity commitment.

As part of the research-practice partner project, the makerspace trialled  all-girls  workshops.  Two  series  of  in-person  workshops  were delivered between April and September 2023. The first series consisted of three workshops that focused on 3D modelling, followed by the second series involving two workshops with GenAI. The GenAI workshops were adapted from the 3D modelling, based on girls' and their parents ' feedback 3 concerning  the  all-girls  workshop  format  and  experiences. Their responses to questions, such as ' what ran well? ' and ' what could be  changed/improved? ' ,  were  analysed  by  researchers  and  then  discussed with practitioners in a group reflective session, informing the design of GenAI workshops.

The three 3D modelling workshops were held between April and May 2023, each lasting around 3 hours. The objective was to collaboratively construct a modular robot using 3D modelling and printing. The robot was composed of separate limbs, with each part designed and created by an  individual  girl  participant.  In  addition  to  the  technical  activities, mentors introduced women scientists and invited the girls to share their opinions about women's roles in science. The girls were also encouraged to reflect on broader implications of 3D printing technology, particularly in relation to medical applications and wider social issues.

The  second  series  focusing  on  using  GenAI  tools  to  support  idea expression  about  climate  change  issues  was  delivered  in  September 2023. Two GenAI tools were employed: Playground (a free AI image generator platform that supports both text-to-image and image-to-image generation) and Deforum (a free and open GenAI platform for producing animated content through text-to-image and image-to-image functions). Practitioners hoped that girls would not only experience a process of generating content with GenAI but also recognise that it is a purposeful process shaped by creators ' intentions. ## 3.3. Analysis

Reflexive  thematic  analysis  (TA)  (Braun &amp; Clarke,  2021)  was employed to analyse the data. Braun and Clarke (2019, 2021) distinguish reflexive TA from other TA approaches, such as coding reliability TA and codebook TA, in terms of their underlying paradigmatic and epistemological assumptions and analytic procedures. Coding reliability TA  and  codebook  TA  seek  to  ensure  the  accuracy  and  reliability  of coding,  reflecting  a  more  positivist  orientation,  while  reflexive  TA ' emphasises the importance of the researcher's subjectivity as analytic resource, and their reflexive engagement with theory, data and interpretation ' (Braun &amp; Clarke,  2021,  p.  330).  Coding  is  therefore  not treated as a mechanical process of identifying pre-existing themes, but as an open, organic, and iterative process of meaning-making (Braun &amp; Clarke, 2021). Researcher subjectivity and situated, reflexive interpretation are central to this active analytic process. As Ely et al. (1997) note, ' if themes 'reside ' anywhere, they reside in our heads from our thinking about our data and creating links as we understand them ' (p. 205 -206).

The focus of our data analysis was shaped by the research questions. To address the first research question concerning multiple stakeholders' perceptions of the all-girls workshop format, we focused on analysing individual  interviews  with  girls,  parents,  workshop  facilitators,  and makerspace practitioners, alongside two reflective sessions. ## 4. Findings

## 4.1. All-girls workshop format: multiple perspectives

The interviews (n = 15) with five girls, six parents, one leader, one coordinator, and two mentors/facilitators mainly reflect benefits and concerns associated with the all-girls workshop format. As previously indicated (see Table 2), the perspectives of girls and parents relate only to  the  3D  modelling  workshops,  whereas  practitioners'  views  were drawn from both the 3D modelling and GenAI workshops. Although the technologies used differed, participants' multiple perspectives on the allgirls  format provide insights into the extent to which, and how, this format may  support  girls ' active and  equitable participation in computing, thereby offering implications for the future design of programmes  aimed  at  promoting  greater  gender  equity  in  computing. Related to GenAI, our next section focuses on the key steps taken by practitioners as they continued to adopt the all-girls workshop format, drawing on reflective  lessons from  the  3D  modelling workshops and their ongoing reflections on challenges and further changes.

The  common  benefit  identified  concerns  creating  a ' safe ' and ' relaxed ' space for girls to be more engaged and feel braver to express their ideas and explore what they want. As two mentors indicated:

It has created a safe space for girls [ … ] it inspires them, it creates motivation for them to pursue whatever they want to pursue. And also, I think the safe space is really important because they feel really relaxed and speak their mind (Ana, mentor/facilitator).

I  noticed that the girls talked more than they usually do in other workshops where I have to communicate directly with them to get them engaged [ … ] So I think that ' s the biggest difference, getting them to speak their mind and participate (Mika, mentor/facilitator).

Parents and practitioners felt that girls tended to get scared and then talk less in previous mixed-gender workshops.

But if  there  are  many  more  boys  than  girls,  then  they  [girls]  get scared. Because boys are so much braver and so much louder and so much more active, then girls prefer to stand by and watch (Parent 4).

Because she is afraid that she will not know something or something will be too difficult, and then it seemed to me that if the environment was just girls, [ … ] it might be easier for her to connect (Parent 3).

Girls have a space they can come to and explore and work and join other workshops, and I think because it ' s girls only, they are more likely to sign up and feel not intimidated. (Mika, mentor/facilitator).

These quotes reveal that gendered interpersonal behaviours by boys restricted girls' participation and engagement (Archer et al., 2025). In addition, the parents' comments show gender stereotypes and assumptions that boys normally know more and are braver than girls. This kind of deficit-based view is also reflected in girls' responses. For example, when a YCR asked a girl participant how she felt about the all-girls workshop  compared  to  previous  mixed-gender  workshops,  she  said ' it's not like that again [ … ] Us girls are quieter. ' The external factors, such  as  male-dominated  participation  and  key  adults'  deficit-based views,  appear  to  exert  power  on  the  girls'  identity  construction  ( feel quieter ) and participation ( talking less ).

One practitioner highlighted the influence of male-dominated culture in school STEM and pointed out the role of the all-girls workshops in rekindling and developing her interest in robotics.

She had a very good time here because she is very interested in robotics, but at school there are only boys in a similar course. She is more comfortable here because there are also girls in the activities that  would  normally  be  associated  with  boys  (Vera,  programme coordinator).

Moreover, the two facilitators recognised an inspiring role of the allgirls workshops. For example, Ana shared her own story about entering and working in the technology field:

You know they are interested in my story, and how I got into it, and that ' s an important part of the workshop to inspire.

As a result, the girls indicated their preference for the workshop with all girls:

YCR: And how would you feel if you were in a group where everyone else was just boys?

Amy: Not so good, though. It ' s better if it ' s just girls.

YCR: You can connect more, right?

Amy: Yes.

Cathy: I would much rather be in an all-girls group.

Mika also shared: ' girls expressed excitement about this programme, and they then asked when they can take part in it again. '

The value of female mentors/facilitators in the all-girls workshops was also emphasised. For instance, Mika indicated that ' I am a woman, and I can think of all of the things that relate to girls. I'm not sure how, for  example,  a  man  would  do  that. ' However,  she  also  stressed  the importance of being open-minded to learn about individual girls and make pedagogical practice more relatable to them. As she reflected:

Some of the girls expressed that if it wasn ' t an all-girls workshop, they wouldn ' t have come. I did not relate to that at all. I was very outgoing. I was a very technical person so I fit into that [mixedgender] group, and it was very difficult for me to try and create a workshop where a girl might feel like that. So I asked a lot from them to share their experiences [ … ] So they had to basically tell me some worries and problems that they were facing.

Despite the recognised benefits, two parents preferred mixed-gender groups in workshops. Parent 2, who has a daughter and a son, expressed a  concern  that ' [all-girls  workshops]  can  be  discriminatory  towards some boys who would also want to come to [learn] the same thing. ' While Parent 5 stated ' the mixed groups are the best ' , they recognised the value of the all-girls workshops in encouraging girls ' participation in technology-related activities and in challenging the societal stereotypes that ' girls should be more beautiful and dance. '

On one hand, multiple stakeholders agreed that the all-girls workshop  format  implied  a  girl-friendly  environment  to  encourage  girls ' registration and participation; on the other hand, they identified other factors  that  may  influence  their  sign-up  decision  and  experience, including interest, workshop topic and content, and pedagogical design and approach.

Interest is essential. If she is not interested in attending, the fact that there ' s only girls will not make a difference (Parent 3).

The topic implies that there would likely be boys, and because of that, she may not feel as brave about wanting to be here (Parent 2).

[The topics] They're complicated [ … ] But I'm not very good at it yet and that's why I find it really difficult (Amy).

I was a little disappointed, because at the beginning they said that we would each make our own robot and then take it home, only now I realised that basically everyone would only get one part of the robot to take home (Joy)

If the moderator does not give space to some children who are timid, and girls are usually timid, those children just do not show up a second time (Parent 4).

In particular, Parent 2 and Amy raised concerns about inappropriate topics and complicated learning content. Joy expressed disappointment that, contrary to initial expectations of building a complete robot, girls were instead assigned to make only individual components. This change, seemingly introduced without explicit negotiation with the girls, highlights how pedagogical decisions may limit opportunities for girls ' voices  and  agency  within  the  workshop,  even  in  an  all-girls  setting. Despite being situated in a safer and friendlier environment, the girls were still expected to learn ' cool ' things decided by parents and practitioners to make them better fit into STEM and/or computing fields. For example,  one  parent  highlighted  the  importance  of  their  daughter gaining  access  to  technological  knowledge  typically  associated  with boys, while girls' own knowledge and strengths were less recognised and valued. It  is  worthwhile  to  question  whether  such  external  decisions were made from a deficit perspective, particularly when compared to stereotypical assumptions about boys ' interests and strengths. ## 4.2. GenAI workshops: practice and reflection

To contextualise practitioners' reflections, we first present the key steps they took towards gender equity during the GenAI workshops. This supports  a  deeper  understanding  of  practitioners ' reflections,  highlighting applicable lessons learned, challenges encountered, and future improvements.

## 4.2.1. Key steps taken by practitioners towards gender equity

Our analysis highlights three key steps that the practitioners took: (i) representation; (ii) relationship building; and (iii) a responsive approach. Firstly, to enhance representation of girls and women, their efforts centred on promotional materials and role models at the symbolic level (Harding, 1986). They wrote the advertising and marketing materials  in  a  more  female-centred  way  by  using  female  pronouns  and grammar and foregrounding girls within imagery, fostering equitable access and outreach. As Vera explained:

When we announce the workshops, we make sure that it is written with the female gender in mind, and that we make a particular point of  saying  that  we  want  more  girls  in  our  programme … we will continue to use the female gender in workshop announcements.

Furthermore, the girls not only learned with other girls but also had the opportunity to learn more about and from women role models. These included their workshop mentors/facilitators and successful women in STEM and computing. Practitioners recognised the value of this exposure  in  inspiring  the  girls,  who  did  not  often  hear  from  women  role models in school:

At  the  workshops  we  also  sometimes  introduce  women,  famous women in science. And this aspect I think was very inspiring for them, you know. They don ' t hear it every day in school (Irena, senior leader).

The lead facilitator shared that her intention of bringing women role models was to challenge gender stereotypes:

I try to present to them that maybe the things that they think they can ' t do or shouldn ' t be doing just because they ' re girls. They need to get that out of their mind (Mika, facilitator).

Drawing on Harding's (1986) three-level framework, practitioners' efforts to increase the representation of role models and to change language  and  images  on  their  promotion  materials  can  be  primarily understood as changes at the symbolic level. These changes foreground women and challenge dominant gendered representations of computing in terms of who fits or can belong in this field. In doing this, practitioners hoped  that  the  girls  can ' get  that  [gender  stereotypes]  out  of  their minds ' . This suggests that the change at the symbolic level may support girls'  sense  of  belonging  and  identity  development  at  the  individual level.

Their second step focuses on relationship building at the social level (Sultan et al., 2024). Two facilitators used various strategies to help girls build their relationships with the space , peers , and facilitators . Firstly, the facilitators gave the girls opportunities to ' talk about the space we are in ' to let them feel comfortable, safe, and relaxed. Ana added that they also made the girls understand this was also the ' space for complaining. ' Indicated in our observation notes, ' there were more casual conversations, laughter, and free movement among girls, and facilitators used more body language and softer voice, in comparison to our previous observations of mixed-gender youth programmes. ' Vera also noted in the  reflective  meeting, ' it  was  much  more  relaxed.  I  saw  some  girls dancing in the middle of the session. ' The efforts to build a space that is inclusive and welcoming for girls, and to strengthen girls ' relationship with and ownership of the space, can nurture positive feelings associated with being together and a sense of belonging (Rahm et al., 2022; Sultan et al., 2024).

Although some girls had known each other before the workshops, the facilitators  ran  interactive  activities  to  foster  peer  relationships.  For instance, the GenAI workshops began with the Jenga Tower game as an ice-breaking activity. The questions written by practitioners on every Jenga piece were about girls ' personal experiences. The main goal, according  to  Mika,  was  to  encourage  individual  participants  to  share something about themselves and engage with each other more, building an equitable and supportive relationship.

We wanted to get them to feel like they can share their hobbies. And you  know,  nobody  will  make  fun  of  them.  We  usually  try  to encourage them to tell us even more if we don ' t know about it. And I feel that the ice-breaking activity at the very beginning sort of sets the tone for the rest of the workshop (Mika, mentor/facilitator).

Pedagogically, this activity also offered an opportunity for the facilitators  to  adapt  their  pedagogical  practice  in  relation  to  the  girls ' personal experiences and interests. As a result, the facilitators felt that the girls were more open to sharing ideas. Particularly, Mika noticed that younger girls felt less ' scared ' while being with other older girls.

They opened up quite a bit. I think the younger girls particularly, they were a bit scared because I think everyone was a bit older, but I felt that as soon as they got asked a few questions about themselves, they felt they had to explain a lot about themselves.

Moreover, the facilitators attempted to build a more equal relationship  with  the  girls  during  their  teaching  practice.  For  instance,  they valued the girls ' funds of knowledge (Gonz ´ alez et al., 2005) and lived experience,  drawing  them  into  a  dialogue  that  aimed  to  collectively build knowledge. As Mika explained in their approach to introducing fundamentals of AI:

We always start with the discussion and start by questioning what they think they signed up for, what they think we ' re gonna do in this workshop, what sort of knowledge they had before, what idea they had before we give them any information to really see what they know and how they got to know the things. They teach me a bit more about the programming, for example Scratch for programming.

Break time was also strategically used to transform the traditional student-to-teacher relationship to a collaborative learning partnership, as Ana said:

We discussed everything from fashion to Eurovision. You know, it ' s break time, and they really share a little bit more. I think that the


<!-- PAGE 10 -->


break time is really important. Actually it allows for connection with members of the workshop. So you can really feel that you are at the same level. We were both learning something.

Thirdly, practitioners highlighted a responsive approach to interacting and engaging with girls at the individual level (Harding, 1986), rather than employing a fixed, standardised approach. During the image-generation activity using Playground, practitioners found that the girls  had  different  difficulties  and  preferences.  For  example,  Mika noticed that younger girls were not confident in using English to write appropriate prompts to generate images they envisioned. Responding to this, Mika explained, ' we just gave her a bunch of images to point out what she liked, so that she can then generate based on that and then use her drawing as well. ' Another girl preferred creating digital drawings on a computer; facilitators allowed her to ' choose to work whichever way you want ' and provided individual support when she used another app. These examples illustrate how a responsive, flexible approach attends to girls' diverse needs and preferences during the workshops, aligning with Harding's (1986) individual level.

## 4.2.2. Practitioners ' reflections: challenges and further changes

While taking these key steps, practitioners identified several interconnected challenges and issues concerning workshop topic, pedagogy, and technology. In a post-practice reflection session supported by episodes of workshop video recordings, Mika indicated that girls were not very  engaged  with  the  climate  change  topic.  Despite  the  discussion around climate change at the beginning of the second day, she found that ' they were stuck on the topic. ' During the image-generation process, she noticed:

They were focusing more on actual creating than that on our topic of climate change. What they were doing individually were more universal or popular than the specific things we recognise.

In line with the practitioner's observation, only two out of 44 outputs from the girls reflected the climate change topic. One image described penguins on melting ice, and the other picture illustrated trash in the sea. Facilitators felt the time they gave the girls to think about climate change issues was very limited. For instance, Ana suggested: ' it should be longer. I don't think time is enough for the girls to really understand the  climate  change  topic  and  its  potential  positive  impact  [on  our environment and society]. '

Although the workshop topic has environmental and social values that could facilitate STEM-rich making for social action (Foster, 2019) and long-term computational participation (Kafai, 2016), girls' engagement and their outputs led us to reflect how relevant the workshop was to the girls. From the reflective meetings with practitioners, we learned that the topic primarily reflected the practitioners' interest and values, while girls ' voices seemed to be missing. The topic was chosen by one facilitator who had working experience in AI and satellite imaging. Also, most activities were decided by the facilitator, who assumed what might be of interest to and/or beneficial for girls. As Mika mentioned:

The actual workshop is in the content that we ' re gonna be doing. That was like more [on] Ana ' s side, so I just gave her my feedback on it.  And then she kinda came up with ideas and ways that we can adapt and extend it into the workshop.

Additionally, there was little evidence showing that the girls' assets had affected the workshop design. This suggests that unequal power relations still existed, despite efforts to create a more friendly and supportive space and an equal relationship between girls and practitioners. If the girls' voices are not considered and valued, it is possible they still feel this is 'not for me ' or 'irrelevant to me ' .

In  one  group  reflection  meeting,  one  practitioner  recognised  the inappropriate workshop topic and stressed the need for more hands-on activities:

We could also get some other topics where there are more hands-on [activities], because I think it ' s easier to get them to do so just using your hands and experimenting a little bit (Mika, mentor/facilitator).

They also suggested a co-production approach underscoring girls ' agency and voices in future workshop design:

We are very into like co-production with children or youth [ … ] We want to know what they would do, how they would do it. So this is something that we also want to do with this project you know. Maybe for the next programme that we will be, I don ' t know, applying at the end of the year. We will have discussion with these girls and ask them what they felt about and what they would change and how they would do it (Irena, senior leader).

The  second  challenge  identified  by  practitioners  was  a  lack  of collaboration among girls in the actual practice, although they tried to build peer relationships at the social level. As Mika reflected in the group reflection meeting, ' apart from the game in the beginning, I think most of it was very individual. ' The lack of peer collaboration might reinforce the  stereotype  that  computing  is  a  solitary  activity  (Diekman  et  al., 2011). Mika reiterated this issue in the video-based, one-to-one reflective interview:

The girls didn ' t really interact that much with anyone. It was more of just going through an individual journey through the AI. Because it was a calmer environment for them, I feel.

This aligns with our workshop observation, where the girls worked independently on laptops most of the time. The ' calmer ' environment may indicate limited opportunities for peer discussion and group work, suggesting that collaborative talk and tasks could be more intentionally designed and incorporated into workshop delivery. Drawing on Harding's  (1986) theory suggests that  relationship  building  at  the  social level and girl-friendly environment at the symbolic level are not sufficient;  structural  changes,  such  as  the  structure  and  organisation  of technology teaching (Sultan et al., 2024) and interaction structure, were less attended to and enacted by the practitioners. We also noticed that when the girls met problems, they tended to ask for more help from the two facilitators than peers. Although Mika and Ana tried hard to respond to their individual requests, they pointed out a practical challenge:

We don ' t always have time to help them as mentors … if they don ' t get our attention or the help they need, not just attention but actual help to continue, the workshop discourages them a lot. So we try to balance it out.

Although GenAI technology can offer rich opportunities for personalised learning (Zhang et al., 2024), collaborative hands-on learning is still important for girls. Given that computing can be a social and creative practice, design sharing and collaboration with others are paramount (Kafai, 2016). Literature also shows that peer collaboration can support girls' engagement in computing, alongside developing problem-solving and teamwork skills, expanding social networks, challenging gender stereotypes, and fostering computational perspectives (Barksdale et al., 2022; Pinkard et al., 2017). Computational perspectives refer to ' perspectives designers form about the world around them and  about  themselves ' (Brennan &amp; Resnick,  2012,  p.  3).  Beyond engagement and skills, the evidence suggests that peer collaboration supports girls' self-image and beliefs in relation to computing at Harding's (1986) individual level.

The third challenge, highlighted by the practitioners, was related to GenAI tools and interactions with them. First, selecting a safe and userfriendly tool for girls to use was identified as a challenge.

It ' s something where we first need to go through what is available and also make sure that it ' s safe for such a young age group [ … ] We need to find  models  that  don ' t  include  pornographic  images.  We don ' t  want  to  expose  the  kids  to  anything  like  that.  So  it ' s  very


<!-- PAGE 11 -->


difficult to find models that actually work and the interface is also user-friendly (Mika, mentor/facilitator).

Second, Mika highlighted the barrier accessing to free or affordable GenAI tools:

Over the years, the fact is that a lot of things are now no longer free. So even Playground, for example, we cannot use it anymore because they  removed some of the functions and it became pro. Unfortunately, I don ' t think [the makerspace name] has enough money to pay, as the tool develops.

This reflects that the development of GenAI tools is driven by the need to make profits, possibly excluding  users  from  under-resourced backgrounds.

Third, prompt writing in terms of language barriers and idea expression was identified as another key challenge for the girls. Since the girls ' first language is not English and the GenAI tools used in this workshop only accepted English prompts, this created a structural-level barrier (Harding, 1986), limiting accessibility for these girls. Even though the facilitators  encouraged  the  girls  to  use  Google  translator,  some  still struggled  to  write  clear  text-to-image  prompts.  Mika  elaborated  that crafting a proper prompt capable of producing an image that captured and reflected their initial ideas was a challenge.

Actually, describing in words what you want to see and then also what you have in mind when you say these words and what the AI gives you are completely different things (Mika, mentor/facilitator).

Compared to physical drawing, using Playground requires users to articulate what they really want clearly, which is difficult for individuals who often do not have fully formed ideas at the outset. Writing prompts in English can be understood as a design-embedded structural barrier that  reflects  AI  tool  developers ' positionality  and  the  dominance  of English among GenAI tools. Linguistic proficiency, particularly in English, is thus positioned as a prerequisite for participation.

The  shift  from  direct  visual  expression  to  text-based  expression mediated by Playground places additional cognitive and linguistic demands on the girls. Mika found that the girls were more impressed with their own drawings than those generated by GenAI through just ' a press of a button ' . They also preferred animating the physical or digital images created by themselves.

Girls then use their own drawings instead of what they generated. So here was quite interesting to see that they weren ' t that impressed by, for example, an image that they got through a press of a button or an image on the Internet. It was a lot more interesting for them to see the drawings that they made come to life and be animated.

The quote above reflects tensions around agency, voice, and pedagogy in workshops using GenAI, as well as underlying power relations among girls, practitioners, and technology. Despite practitioners' pedagogical  intention  in  using  Playground  to  generate  images,  the  girls demonstrated stronger attachment and interest in their own drawings. This  raises  a  critical  question  regarding  whose  voice,  agency,  and creativity are valued when integrating the GenAI tools into the activities primarily designed by the practitioners. Girls are positioned as passive users (Fuller et al., 2013) of Playground through its limited technological  functions  (i.e.,  primarily  accepting  English  prompts)  rather  than active creators, reflecting wider symbolic and structural issues, as well as the ways in which activities are shaped by the tool's functionality. Although the pedagogical approach with Playground prioritises technological novelty, the decision to use this particular GenAI tool in this activity  overlooks  its  constraints  that  might  limit  the  girls'  creative expression.  Girls'  creative  expression  mediated  by  English  prompts became less fluent. They also felt their ideas were inadequately reflected by the GenAI outputs. This might be because the tool's databases and algorithms are primarily shaped by men and underrepresent girls' and women's  perspectives.  In  comparison  to  girls'  own  creations,  the

AI-generated images may limit girls' sense of ownership. In this case, Playground seems to dominate the decisions on key features of images, such as style and colour scheme.

Despite the constraints of Playground, what matters more is pedagogy -why GenAI is used and how it is integrated -rather than the tool alone. Had practitioners provided more prompt-writing scaffolds and engaged the girls in a collective inquiry into how to better use Playground to support creativity, the tool might have become a dialogic partner  (Liu,  2024)  that  could  support  girls  in  developing  ideas  and exploring  multiple  potentials  in  a  GenAI-supported  dialogic  space (Wegerif &amp; Major, 2019). In contrast, the other GenAI tool, Deforum, was perceived as more useful. According to Mika, the girls were more engaged in using Deforum to animate their own drawings and were more excited by the resulting animations. This may be because Deforum respected girls' original work while offering a new representation mode -transforming static images into dynamic animations. Hence, the girls' different  experiences  highlight  the  importance  of  integrating  GenAI pedagogically  to  centre  girls,  rather  than  replace  or  dominate,  girls ' voices and self-expression.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Beyond girlification: centring girls in computing

Regarding the all-girls workshop format, our findings have shown varied perspectives among participating girls, their parents, and makerspace  practitioners  in  terms  of  perceived  benefits  and  concerns. Consistent with prior all-girls initiatives in computing (e.g., Lang et al., 2020; Sultan et al., 2024), the findings confirm that the all-girls format, implying a more relaxed and girl-friendly environment, could make girls feel more welcomed and included. Using the all-girls format reflects the practitioners'  attempt  to  make  the  workshop  more  feminine  through girlification (Sultan et al., 2024). However, girlification in the workshops primarily operates at the symbolic level (Harding, 1986) and can be superficial and performative, thus remaining insufficient to support girls' meaningful and sustained participation in computing. Drawing on Harding's (1986) multi-level framework, our analysis of the three key steps revealed limited attention to structure-level changes, such as activity design, interaction structure, and tool selection, that are critical for girls' active and equitable participation.

It  is  important  to  note  the  distinction  between  girlification  and centring girls. The former in this case focused on girlified adjustments, such  as  grouping  girls  together,  including  women  facilitators,  introducing role models of women, using more feminine language and images in marketing materials, and choosing a workshop theme focusing more on social and environmental impact but less on technical aspects. While these adjustments could reduce some barriers to participation, they may not  challenge  or  transform  the  deeper  structures,  norms,  and  power dynamics that marginalise girls' voices and agency in computing. Our analysis has revealed that, despite the practitioners ' deliberate creation of the girl-friendly ' pinky ' space and steps focusing on representation, relationship building, and the use of a responsive approach, girls still remained marginalised in relation to their voices, agency, and strengths. Practitioners  retained  decision-making  power  regarding  workshop topics,  tools,  and  pedagogical  approaches,  primarily  based  on  their previous personal experiences and assumptions about what would be relevant  to  and  beneficial  for  these  girls.  However,  girls  were  not involved in these decisions.

In particular, the girls' engagement with two GenAI tools revealed the power dynamics among practitioners, GenAI, and girls. Girls' voices, agency,  and  creativity  seemed  to  be  constrained  by  Playground.  In contrast, they were more engaged in using Deforum to animate their drawings, because it leveraged, rather than replaced, the girls' original creations and offered an alternative mode of representing their ideas. These different experiences and outcomes underscore the importance of pedagogically  integrating  GenAI  in  ways  that  amplify,  rather  than


<!-- PAGE 12 -->


overshadow, girls' voices, agency, and creative expressions. This aligns with UNESCO's (2024) call for a human-centred approach to AI. Practitioners are suggested to be cautious about a technology-over-human mindset (Wu &amp; Miller, 2025). For practitioners, equity-oriented pedagogical  considerations  and  approaches  are  therefore  needed;  these include rethinking the purposes of using a certain GenAI tool in relation to  gender  equity  and  exploiting  its  affordances  in  equitable  ways  to amplify girls' voices, support the enactment of agency, and foster creative expression.

Recognising  the  power  relations  between  practitioners  and  girls, centring girls also means giving them greater power in making decisions and participation in decision-making processes, echoing the emphasis on empowerment and valuing voice in feminist pedagogy. As indicated in practitioners' reflections on further changes, co-production would be a promising approach towards being girl-centred. Girls are empowered to  decide  collectively  with  practitioners  whether,  what,  and  how  a particular GenAI can be used to support them. Therefore, centring girls in  computing involves more than girlification. It requires critical examination and deeper reflection on the root causes of underrepresentation  and  inequitable  participation  of  girls  in  computing,  including structures,  power  dynamics,  and  masculinist  cultural  norms  in  the computing field (Archer et al., 2021, 2025), and it needs transformative changes that centre girls ' voices, needs, agency, and strengths across the symbolic,  structural,  social,  and  individual  levels  (Harding,  1986) through an equity lens.

## 5.2. Implications for GenAI use for gender equity

Based on our findings, we propose several theoretical and practical implications.  Building  on  Harding's  (1986) framework  and  feminist pedagogy  (Miner &amp; Robinson-Hill,  2021;  Musil,  2008;  Webb  et  al., 2002),  our  study  addresses  the  aforementioned  research  gaps  by advancing understanding of girls'  experiences with  GenAI in  all-girls makerspace  workshops,  highlighting  the  critical  distinction  between girlification versus centring girls. Theoretically, this study contributes to educational innovation by proposing a girl-centred, multi-level pedagogical framework (see Fig. 1). This theory-informed and research-based framework emphasises centring girls' voices, agency, and assets, and offers a critical tool to interrogate girlified practices that may attract girls  while  failing  to  position  them  as  active  agents  at  the  centre  of practice.  It  also  helps  challenge  technology-over-human  approaches (Wu &amp; Miller,  2025)  along  with  some  underlying  assumptions  in GenAI-related pedagogy, such as prompt-based, individual interaction with  GenAI.  The  four-level  framework  not  only  helps  explain  why gender inequities in computing persist and are slow to change, but also highlights  the  need  for  multi-level  transformation  for  gender  equity. Therefore, it provides both an analytical lens and can also be used as a design tool for equity-oriented GenAI practices and interventions that move beyond single-level or superficial approaches.

Embedding gender equity within learning spaces is not solely the responsibility of particular dedicated individuals; rather, it is ' everyone's business ' (Archer et al., 2025, p. 14). The study also offers practical  implications  for  practitioners  in  informal  computing  education, girls' parents, and AI developers. First, aligned with our key argument, we emphasise the importance of foregrounding girls' needs, voices, assets, and agency throughout practice from planning and implementation through to evaluation. For instance, our findings have highlighted the marginalisation of girls' voices in decision-making processes regarding workshop purposes, topics, tools, and outcomes. Given the importance of trying to attract and retain more girls in computing, it is important to actively empower them and invite them to make decisions collaboratively with practitioners. As previously argued, girlification is not the same as centring girls. To move beyond girlification towards girl-centred practice in computing, we suggest that practitioners interrogate power relations and engage in critical reflections on questions such as ' For whom/whose  practice? ' and ' Based  on  whose  values? ' (Vossoughi, 2017).

Second, as our findings highlight, practitioners and parents need to move beyond deficit-based assumptions (e.g., presuming girls lack interest,  confidence,  relevant  knowledge, and experience with technology) (Fuller et al., 2013) and critically reflect on and challenge the status quo shaped by social structures,  power,  and  masculinist  culture  and norms (Archer et al., 2025). Our study identified three key steps (i.e., representation, relationship building, a responsive approach) taken by practitioners  and  analysed  them  along  with  practitioners ' reflections through  the  lens of  Harding  (1986). Third, critical reflection on pedagogical decisions and approaches around GenAI use is necessary, specifically considering why and how GenAI is integrated into computing education to meaningfully support and benefit girls. Before making a decision, it is important for practitioners to understand and analyse both affordances and constraints of particular GenAI tools through an equity lens. As shown in previous literature and our data, essential factors, such as safety, accessibility, affordability, language barriers, and biases embedded in training data and algorithms (Browne, 2023; Dwivedi et al., 2023), require close and critical examination. The girls' contrasting experiences with two GenAI tools in our study suggest that GenAI should not be treated as a superficial or trendy ' add-on ' ; instead, it should be intentionally integrated as a lever to amplify girls' voices and maximise GenAI's potential to support girls' participation in computing.

Finally, responding to the reported barriers of current GenAI technologies (e.g., subscription fees, English-language dominance, the lack of tools specifically designed for girls), we suggest developing collaborative partnerships among AI technology developers, practitioners, and researchers. Such partnerships could bridge the gap between tool design and actual use in educational practices. These partnerships could also foster knowledge exchange and reflective dialogue, leading to shared equity-embedded practices that support girls ' sustainable engagement in computing.

## 6. Conclusion

Our research contributes new empirical insights into GenAI practice for  gender equity, addressing a significant research gap in AI educational research. Despite predominantly positive responses from practitioners, girls, and parents about the all-girls format, our critical analysis, together  with  practitioners'  reflections,  revealed  underlying  issues associated with power imbalances and deficit-based assumptions. Our findings  suggest  that  girls'  voices,  agency,  and  assets  were  not  sufficiently centred, which limited the transformative potential of the practice. Therefore, we argue for the need to move beyond painting in pink (Heybach &amp; Pickup, 2017) or girlification in practice, and instead to foreground girls ' voices and agency in co-developing equitable practices for them. Drawing on prior research on gender equity in STEM and our reflective  engagement  with  this  case  study,  we  have  proposed  a girl-centred,  multi-level  pedagogical  framework  and  related  implications for practitioners, AI technology developers, and parents to advance more gender-equitable practice.

However,  we  also  acknowledge  several  limitations  in  this  study. Although it incorporated multiple stakeholder perspectives, the limited sample  size  and  specific  context  mean  that  our  findings  cannot  be generalised to wider makerspace settings and should be interpreted with contextual caution. For example, all participating girls are white and lived  in  an  urban  European  city.  Hence,  their  experiences  are  not equivalent  to  those  of  marginalised  girls  facing  more  complex  intersectional challenges. As previously stated, girls' own views were limited to five interviews conducted by YCRs, and the girls' responses were often short  and  closed.  This  may  be  because  of  limited  research  interview experience  of  these  YCRs.  Furthermore,  although  observations  were conducted,  the  girls'  talk  in  a  European  language  was  not  directly accessible to the researchers. While some of their talk was later shared by  practitioners  during  reflective  meetings,  the  translated  and  paraphrased  accounts  may  have  reduced  the  nuances  of  girls ' original meanings.

In line with our argument of centring girls, future research needs to more fully foreground girls' direct voices by collecting data with them to better understand their experiences in using GenAI in computing and the impact of certain interventions. Drawing on the proposed framework, future research could explore and examine how the potential of GenAI

can be maximised to support girls in meaningful and equitable ways, particularly  how  it  may  genuinely  amplify  girls'  voices,  agency,  and creative expression across symbolic, structural, social, and individual levels. This could be supported by knowledge exchange, collective dialogue,  and  iterative  cycles  of  design,  trial,  and  reflection  involving practitioners, technology developers, researchers, and girls themselves, working towards shared equity goals. Mixed-methods research, particularly those incorporating longitudinal data, could add value by examining  both  immediate  outcomes  and  longer-term  impacts  of  GenAIrelated practices/interventions on girls ' participation and identity construction in computing. Developing equity-oriented, girl-centred practice with GenAI may also need targeted professional development, yet such  provision  for  practitioners  and  technology  developers  currently appears limited. Future research into effective models and practices of such professional development would be valuable.
