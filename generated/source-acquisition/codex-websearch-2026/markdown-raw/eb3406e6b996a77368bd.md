## Beyond inclusion: feminist AI for transformative justice in STEM

Tanja Kubes 1

Received: 11 January 2026 / Accepted: 24 April 2026 / Published online: 20 May 2026 © The Author(s) 2026

## Abstract

While human-centred AI (HCAI) has emerged as a commonly deployed response to algorithmic harms, it remains largely reactive and limited in addressing their structural causes. Drawing on feminist STS, intersectionality, critical posthumanism, and more-than-human anthropology, the paper proposes a more comprehensive Feminist AI Framework (FAIF) that expands existing approaches by addressing both immediate biases and the onto-epistemological assumptions sustaining them  and  positions  feminist  analysis  as  integral  to  technical  literacy.  Drawing  on  extensive  classroom  exercises  using text-to-image generators, the paper argues for the systematic integration of feminist perspectives into the development and deployment of AI and in STEM education in general.

Keywords Feminist AI · STEM education · Intersectionality · Algorithmic injustice · Ontological turn · FAIF

## 1  Generative AI and the reproduction of epistemic injustice in STEM

When generative AI systems became publicly available in late 2022, I quickly began incorporating them into my university teaching as a pedagogical tool. At first, I was struck by the speed and apparent ease with which these systems could generate texts and images. That fascination, however, did not last long. While experimenting with GPTs initially felt playful and exciting, it soon became clear how deeply these  technologies  are  entangled  with  social  assumptions about bodies, identities, and knowledge.

Since  2022,  I  have  conducted  a  recurring  classroombased  analysis  of  text-to-image  generators  in  a  total  of seven classes in the physics department of the Freie Universität Berlin. Additionally, I did the same exercise twice in interdisciplinary contexts with participants coming mainly from  the  humanities,  cultural  studies  and  computer  science. Across these iterations, students worked with a range of widely used platforms, including DALL-E, Midjourney, Nightcafé, Chat GPT, Stable Diffusion, and Bing Image Cre -ator. During  the  exercise,  students  first  generated  images using  prompts  such  as  'STEM  professors,'  'a  software engineer,' 'a robotics expert in a lab,' 'a mathematician,' or 'a climate scientist at work.' Even though some of the courses were taught in German, prompts were to be formulated strictly in English to avoid the grammatical gendering inherent in German nouns that would otherwise pre-structure the outputs. Just as I was during my first experiments with GPTs, the students were thrilled by the results of their prompting. And, just as it was for me, that initial excitement did not last long.

When analysing the outputs,  I  asked  students  to  focus on  three  main  dimensions:  representation,  absence,  and intersectionality.  Thus,  students  first  produced  detailed descriptions of the generated images, examining how professional expertise is visually coded through markers such as gender, race, age, clothing, etc. The analysis then turned to absences, asking which groups appear underrepresented or missing altogether. Finally, students examined how overlapping social categories are represented (or erased) when prompts combine multiple identities.

When prompts introduced additional contexts or identities, stereotyping intensified and divisions of labour  became  strongly  gendered.  For  example,  prompts such  as  'STEM  professors  having  a  party'  generated images of middle-aged white male scientists being served pizza  and  drinks  by  feminized  robots. Viewed  from  the  perspective  of  educational  practice, this GPT-induced perpetuation of prejudice and bias has a troubling  implication:  If  generative AI  systems  do  in  fact participate  in  shaping  cultural  imaginaries  of  knowledge and expertise, then the question of who designs, evaluates, and critiques these systems becomes a matter of structural importance. And since STEM education prepares the future architects of AI, incorporating alternative, comprehensive, and inclusive perspectives into the curriculum becomes an essential site for shaping both the technology and its social implications. Feminist approaches already provide a welldeveloped analytical toolkit for identifying how algorithmic systems  encode  intersecting  inequalities,  for  interrogating whose  knowledge  informs  technological  design,  and  for imagining alternative configurations [ 26, 41 ] (e.g. [ 3, 5, 22, 23, 37]). The recurring representational patterns observed in students' analyses also resonate with longstanding insights from feminist STS, which have emphasized that technologies  are  embedded  in,  and  often  reproduce,  existing  relations of power [ 27, 30, 31, 47 ] [ 4, 24, 29, 33, 39, 45, 54]. Since first implementing the GPT exercise in my classroom, I have gradually synthesized these approaches into a Feminist AI Framework (FAIF), which aligns feminist critique with  insights  from  critical  posthumanism  and  more-thanhuman anthropology. FAIF has been successfully integrated into my seminars since 2024, and I am confident that it holds the potential to advance the development of more robust and equitable AI systems.

This paper develops the argument in five steps. It begins with  two  striking  cases  of  algorithmic  discrimination  and outlines  the  general  idea  of  feminist AI.  It  then  critiques prevailing advocacies of human-centred AI as insufficient for addressing the foundations of structural inequality and examines  why  feminist  perspectives  should  become  integral to STEM education [ 41]. The final section reflects on the transformative potential of feminist AI when embedded in education, research, and technological practice.

## 2  Feminist AI: theoretical foundations and epistemological commitments

When, mid-2010s, during her graduate studies at MIT, Joy Buolamwini  attempted  to  finalize  a  programming  project overlaying inspiring images onto users' faces, she encountered a startling problem: after integrating an open-source facial recognition system, the software failed to recognize her  dark-skinned  face,  while  a  white  Halloween  mask  or even a simple drawing of a face on her palm were correctly identified.  Buolamwini  ultimately  ended  up  resorting  to 'coding in whiteface,' donning a white mask to be detected by her own program [ 10].

This episode, even though dating back almost a decade, illustrates a fundamental reality in contemporary technology still relevant today: AI systems are not neutral instruments but  sociotechnical  constructs  that  encode  and  reproduce existing  power  relations.  In Gender  Shades ,  Buolamwini and  Gebru  [ 11]  showed  that  commercial  facial  recognition systems erred in up to 34.7% of cases for dark-skinned women, compared to less than 1% for light-skinned men. Subsequent research confirms that these inequities persist, demonstrating  that  surface-level  interventions  are  insuffi -cient to address the structural roots of algorithmic bias [ 38, 45].

In some cases, such algorithmic discrimination may be merely  inconvenient.  However,  when  it  comes  to  credit approvals, hiring decisions, predictive policing, or sentencing in court proceedings, it can pose a matter of life-anddeath  significance.  Unfortunately,  despite  vast  evidence to  the  contrary,  dominant  public  and  political  narratives continue to portray AI as objective, rational, and free from human emotion and prejudice (e.g. Borana, [ 9]. This persistent myth of technological neutrality also prevails in so called human-centred AI (HCAI) frameworks that explicitly emphasize fairness and inclusion (UN [ 52 ]; EUR-Lex [ 25]), obscuring the manyfold ways in which AI systems shape

social  relations  and  distribute  opportunities  and  harms unequally.  Feminist  critiques  argue  that  such  techno-optimistic approaches all too often constitute a form of 'ethics washing,' creating the appearance of responsibility without addressing the underlying power dynamics [ 7, 42, 53].

Feminist science and technology studies can provide a corrective  to  such  misguided  development. A  crucial  first step  is  to  understand  the  processes  that  give  rise  to  algorithmic  bias  and  discrimination.  Building  on  Kitchin's account of algorithms as embedded in data infrastructures, institutions and practices, AI systems are best understood as complex sociotechnical assemblages rather than standalone technical artifacts [ 37]. Each component of such an assemblage  carries  cultural,  social,  and  political  dimensions: training  data  reproduces  historical  patterns  of  discrimination;  algorithms  embed  assumptions  about  problems  and solutions (see also [ 6 ]; [ 15]); and infrastructures implicate material, environmental, and global justice concerns.

Amazon's  2014  automated  recruiting  software  infamously exemplified the involuntary bias hidden in any set of data: trained on historical hiring data dominated by successful male candidates, the algorithm systematically devalued women applicants, reproducing structural inequalities encoded  in  the  labour  market  rather  than  generating  new discrimination [ 19]. Interestingly, the programmers at Amazon were by no means oblivious to this issue. In fact, to prevent discrimination, they intentionally removed both names and gender information from applications. What they had not anticipated, however, was that the AI would, based on the  data  it  was  fed,  teach  itself  to  favour  male  applicants over female ones [ 50].

Donna Haraway's concept of 'situated knowledges' may clarify why, in fact, AI cannot ever be neutral. Knowledge is always produced from embodied, partial, and locatable perspectives, which are constitutive of robust and accountable understanding rather than a limitation to overcome [ 29]. In and of itself, this is unproblematic. It merely confirms that knowledge is always partial  and  context-dependent.  Consistent with this premise, AI systems inevitably mirror the assumptions,  blind  spots,  and  positionalities  of  their  creators,  embedded in both their development processes and data environments. They exhibit bias on technical as well as social levels, arising not only from distorted training data but also from the values and power structures that shaped those data in the first place [ 33, 45 ]. Consequently, efforts to mitigate bias in AI cannot be limited to technical fixes alone. They must also engage critically with the social, cultural, and epistemic contexts in which these systems are designed and deployed. At this point, however, one must ask: how can this be realized in practical terms?

Discrimination  and  bias  are  usually  easy  to  recognize. Identifying their specific causes proves much more difficult.

Isolated stances of bias in AI systems can certainly be mitigated  within  existing  paradigms,  but  such  interventions remain  limited  and  often  involve  significant  trade-offs. Improvements in data collection, inclusion targets, auditing practices, and dataset documentation can help to reduce sampling bias and broaden representational coverage. Likewise, targeted  adjustments,  such  as  rebalancing  search  results, modifying  word  embeddings,  or  applying  content  filters, may curb the most visible stereotypes, while fairness-aware learning  methods  and  alternative  optimization  strategies can redistribute error rates across demographic groups (provided that the AI does not - as with Amazon's hiring algorithm - teach itself to reproduce bias and discrimination). However, these measures largely address symptoms rather than underlying causes. By taking existing categories, tasks, and institutional contexts for granted, they aim primarily at equalizing outcomes within established systems rather than questioning the legitimacy of those systems themselves [ 2, 22, 33]. Consequently, a 'fairer' predictive-policing model, for instance, may still reinforce the expansion of policing, and an 'unbiased' advertising system can continue to intensify  stereotyping  and  reproduce  gendered  labour  expectations [ 43, 45].

In addition, different categories of discrimination tend to overlap and reinforce one another. Intersectionality, a concept  first  introduced  by  Kimberlé  Crenshaw  [ 17,  18]  and extended by Patricia Hill Collins [ 13, 14 ], offers a critical framework for understanding how AI systems interact with overlapping structures of oppression.

Crenshaw illustrated the concept using the example of a labour discrimination lawsuit against General Motors from the  1970s.  Back  then,  five  Black  women  had  sued  GM, claiming that the company's seniority and hiring practices discriminated against them. General Motors, in its defence, argued that they did not discriminate against Black employees  in  general,  nor  did  they  discriminate  against  women in general. The argument held some truth, but it masked a significant detail: At General Motors, Black men were typi -cally hired for factory or production-line jobs, while white women were placed in clerical or administrative positions. Black women, however, were excluded from both, illustrating the unique, intersectional discrimination that traditional race-  or  gender-based  legal  frameworks  failed  to  address [ 17].

Feminist  epistemology  and  standpoint  theory  further illuminate these dynamics. Marginalized groups often develop critical insights into social and technological systems  precisely  because  they  navigate  both  dominant  and subordinated perspectives [ 13, 28, 32, 46]. Integrating these insights into AI research and design is crucial for producing systems that do not merely optimize technical metrics but address embedded inequities.

Building on these foundations, feminist AI can be conceptualized as an approach that:

- (1) challenges claims of neutrality, treating AI systems as sociotechnical assemblages encoding and enacting power ([ 37 ]; [ 4]),
- (2) centres situated knowledges, recognizing insights from  marginalized  communities  as  essential  and accountable [ 29],
- (3) applies  an  intersectional  lens  to  reveal  how overlapping systems of oppression shape data, algorithms, and infrastructures [ 51, 13, 17],
- (4) commits to structural transformation rather than superficial optimization, addressing inequitable power dynamics in AI systems [ 42],
- (5) prioritizes collective flourishing and social justice over narrow performance metrics or extractive innovation [ 42], and.
- (6) embeds reflexivity and accountability throughout AI design and deployment processes [ 22].

Feminist AI is thus not merely AI developed by women or  systems  avoiding  obvious  gender  bias.  Rather,  it  integrates  feminist  epistemologies  and  ethical  commitments into  the  conceptualization,  design,  and  deployment of AI, moving beyond liberal feminist concerns with representation to fundamentally reimagine the sociotechnical conditions of algorithmic knowledge production.

## 3  Human-centered AI and the persistence of structural bias

Feminist AI is not the only attempt to address bias in artificial intelligence. Over the past decade, human-centred AI (HCAI) has emerged as a prominent framework advocating that AI systems prioritize human wellbeing, dignity, and rights. Initiatives such as the EU AI Act and ongoing UN efforts  to  establish  international  governance  frameworks explicitly invoke human-centred principles (EUR-Lex [ 25 ];  UN  [ 25]).  Indisputably,  HCAI  represents  an  important departure from purely technical approaches, signalling a growing recognition that AI development requires ethical and social considerations.

However, critical feminist scholarship identified several structural  limitations  that  prevent  HCAI  from  adequately addressing  technological  injustice.  These  limitations  concern the symbolic character of many ethical commitments, persistent  cultural  and  epistemic  biases,  the  concentration of power within AI development, the technocratic framing of fairness, and the anthropocentric assumptions underlying the framework.

First,  HCAI  frameworks  often  function  as  instruments of  legitimation  rather  than  structural  change.  Bietti,  [ 7] argues  that  corporate  ethics  initiatives  frequently  create the  appearance  of  responsibility  while  leaving  underlying development  practices  largely  intact.  Empirical  research supports this claim: a survey of over 150 corporate AI ethics statements found that human-centred rhetoric frequently deflected criticism and resisted regulation while substantive governance mechanisms remained largely absent [ 55].

Second, despite invoking universal human values, HCAI as currently practised tends to reflect a narrow conception of the human shaped by Global North perspectives. Cultural assumptions  embedded  in  AI  systems  frequently  reproduce Western  norms,  while  the  worldviews  and  practices of non-Western communities remain marginal or invisible [ 42,  44].  The  aforementioned  UN  report  highlighted  that 118 countries, predominantly from the Global South, have not participated in any of the major international efforts to regulate AI (UN [ 52];  UNRIC 2024). Research priorities, funding  structures,  and  development  processes,  therefore, largely reflect Western interests, even though communities in  the  Global  South  often  bear  disproportionate  environmental,  economic,  and  labour-related  consequences  of AI infrastructures.

These  epistemic  limitations  also  manifest  in  persistent representational biases. Just as I experienced time and again in my seminars, GPTs frequently associate men with competence and leadership while linking women to child rearing or family roles. Image-generation systems reproduce gender stereotypes, and many AI systems remain structured around binary gender categories, rendering non-binary, genderfluid, and transgender individuals invisible or misrepresented [ 15, 36].

Third, HCAI does not sufficiently confront the concentra -tion of power shaping AI development. A small number of large tech companies dominate the field, determining which systems  are  built  and  which  values  become  embedded  in them [ 1]. Although many HCAI initiatives emphasize stakeholder  engagement  and  participatory  design,  participation without genuine power-sharing often simply serves to legitimize pre-determined decisions. Studies of participatory AI projects show that involvement frequently remains limited to  consultation  rather  than  meaningful  co-design  granting communities  decision-making  authority  [ 48].  Tokenistic inclusion  of  marginalized  voices  can  therefore  create  an appearance of engagement while preserving existing hierarchies [ 35].

Fourth,  much  research  on  AI  fairness  treats  bias  as  a technical anomaly amenable to technical fixes. The prolif -eration of fairness metrics and debiasing techniques fosters


<!-- PAGE 5 -->


an impression of progress while leaving underlying social conditions unchallenged. Reviews of fairness interventions demonstrate  that  most  proposed  methods  focus  on  mathematical optimization while treating the social and political contexts that generate bias as fixed constraints rather than sites of transformation [ 8]. In this sense, technical fairness metrics  may  inadvertently  legitimize  harmful  systems  by suggesting  that  bias  can  be  solved  through  optimization while leaving unaddressed the more fundamental question of whether certain applications should exist at all [ 34].

Finally,  HCAI  largely  remains  locked  into  anthropocentric assumptions that prioritize human interests without adequate  consideration  of  environmental  and  ecological consequences. Yet the environmental footprint of AI infrastructures is substantial: training large language models generates  significant  carbon  emissions,  data  centres  consume vast amounts of water and energy, and hardware production depends on extractive supply chains that devastate ecosystems and exploit workers in the Global South [ 16, 49].

Taken  together,  these  limitations  suggest  that  while HCAI marks an important shift toward ethically informed AI development, it remains insufficient for addressing the deeper  structural  conditions  that  produce  technological injustice.

## 4  The feminist AI framework: a comprehensive methodology

Building on this critique of HCAI approaches, Kubes [ 42] proposes the Feminist AI Framework (FAIF), which conceptualizes interventions in AI development on two analytically distinct levels. The framework distinguishes between, first, addressing the visible manifestations of bias within AI systems and, second, interrogating the deeper epistemological and ontological assumptions that structure contemporary AI research and design.

The first level concerns what, borrowing David Chalm -ers'  [ 12]  well-known  terminology,  might  be  described  as the  'easy  problems' of AI. These include reducing Eurocentrism, racism, sexism, and other forms of bias in datasets and models, increasing diversity in development teams, addressing exploitative labour practices and global inequalities within AI supply chains, and mitigating the concentration  of  economic  and  technological  power  within  a  small number of corporations and countries. Although these challenges are politically  and  institutionally  complex  (and  far from being resolved easily), they can largely be addressed within  the  existing  technoscientific  paradigm  through  a combination of regulatory measures, institutional reforms, and  improved  technical  practices  such  as  auditing,  documentation, and participatory design processes.

The second level addresses what FAIF conceptualizes as the 'hard problems' of AI. These do not primarily concern the  correction  of  specific  biases  but  the  conceptual  foun -dations that shape how AI systems are imagined and built in  the  first  place.  Among  these  foundations  are  assump -tions  such  as  human  exceptionalism,  anthropocentrism, dualistic  separations  between  mind  and  body,  culture  and nature, as well as the presumed universality of Western scientific epistemology. Particularly relevant here is what the French anthropologist Philippe Descola [ 20, 21] describes as the 'naturalist ontology' that has historically structured Western scientific thought. In naturalism, humans and nonhumans are understood as fundamentally distinct kinds of beings:  while  humans  are  assumed  to  possess  interiority (mind,  intention,  consciousness),  non-human  entities  are primarily conceived as material beings governed by universal natural laws. Such an ontological framework encourages the interpretation of technologies, including AI systems, as dispassionate instruments acting upon a passive natural and social environment.

FAIF mobilizes Descola's work in order to question the universality  of  this  ontological  configuration.  Anthropo -logical research has shown that naturalism represents only one  historically  specific  (and  rather  newly  invented)  way of  organizing  relations  between  humans  and  other-thanhuman entities. In Descola's comparative framework, other ontological modes (he calls them animism, totemism, and analogism)  attribute  agency,  intentionality,  or  relational continuity  across  the  boundaries  that  naturalism  draws between humans, animals, environments, and artefacts [ 20]. By foregrounding this ontological plurality, FAIF highlights that dominant assumptions about agency, intelligence, and the  status  of  technological  artefacts  are  not  inevitable  but culturally and historically situated.

Taking this implicit ontological pluralism seriously has implications for how AI systems can be conceptualized. If humans, technologies, and environments are understood not as clearly separated ontological categories but as mutually constitutive  participants  in  relational  worlds,  AI  systems can no longer be interpreted merely as an extension of the human in the form of a tool. Rather, they must be analysed as co-constitutive elements within sociotechnical assemblages that shape and redistribute forms of agency, responsibility, and power [ 40]. From this perspective, the ethical evaluation of AI cannot be limited to correcting biased outputs; it must also examine how particular ontological assumptions inform  system  architectures,  problem  definitions,  and  the framing of technological solutions.

To articulate these shifts, FAIF integrates insights from several  theoretical  traditions.  Intersectionality  and  gender  studies  provide  tools  for  analysing  how  technological systems  reproduce  existing  power  relations  and  patterns

of social exclusion. Critical posthumanism  challenges assumptions of human exceptionalism by emphasizing the entanglement  of  human  and  technological  agencies.  New materialism foregrounds the agency of material infrastructures  and  highlights  how  digital  technologies  are  embedded  in  extensive  material  networks.  Phenomenological approaches  to  embodiment  question  the  assumption  that intelligence  can  be  fully  abstracted  from  bodily  experience and situated contexts of perception and action. Finally, more-than-human anthropology and Indigenous ontologies broaden  the  analytical  frame  by  conceptualizing  ecosystems,  species,  and  landscapes  as  participants  in  relational networks rather than passive environments [ 20, 42, 56].

Taken together, these perspectives expand the scope of AI ethics  beyond  questions  of  fairness  and  bias  toward  a more fundamental interrogation of how knowledge, agency, and responsibility are distributed within sociotechnical systems. They also draw attention to the material and ecological infrastructures that sustain contemporary AI, including hardware production, mineral extraction, energy consumption, cooling systems, and electronic waste streams, thereby foregrounding  the  environmental  and  geopolitical  consequences of large-scale computational infrastructures [ 16].

These  theoretical  commitments  translate  into  several practical implications for research, governance, and education. In research and development, FAIF calls for critically examining the ontological and epistemological assumptions embedded in problem formulation, incorporating intersectional impact assessments, and accounting for material and ecological consequences from the outset of system design. In  governance,  FAIF  proposes  moving  beyond  transparency-centred regulatory approaches toward frameworks that also question the desirability of particular AI applications, establish redistributive mechanisms, enable meaningful participation of affected communities in decision-making pro -cesses, and treat ecological impacts as central governance concerns. And in education, it advocates integrating critical social  theory  into  technical  curricula,  evaluating  students' capacity to anticipate social and ecological harms alongside their  technical  competencies,  and  restructuring  academic incentives  to  reward  interdisciplinary  and  communityengaged scholarship [ 41, 42].

## 5  From critique to creation: the transformative potential of feminist AI

Feminist  AI  as  proposed  in  FAIF  enables  a  shift  from descriptive critique to reconstructive analysis. Rather than merely  identifying  biased  representations,  the  framework provides conceptual tools for explaining why such patterns persist and how systems might be designed differently. The analytical  and  transformative  potential  of  this  approach becomes paradigmatically visible in the text-to-image generator exercise in my teaching, described above.

Through FAIF's intersectional lens, students learn to recognize  that AI-generated  representations  encode  multiple, interlocking systems of domination whose effects intensify at  their  intersections.  These  dynamics  become  especially visible  when  prompts  challenge  several  dominant  norms simultaneously, revealing patterns that single-axis analyses often obscure.

Building on the concept of situated knowledges, students learn to approach training data not as impartial reflections of reality but as historically situated artifacts that materialize past  exclusions  and  ongoing  structural  inequalities.  From this perspective, experiential knowledge from communities systematically misrepresented by AI systems (women scientists, BIPOC researchers, disabled scholars, etc.) emerges as a crucial form of technical expertise. Rather than being reduced to the status of anecdotal evidence, these perspectives become indispensable resources for diagnosing system failures and informing alternative design strategies.

The perspective also enables students to interrogate foundational design assumptions. Instead of accepting existing architectures  as  inevitable,  they  ask  whether  a  software architecture  should  reproduce  prevailing  representational regimes at all. In exploring alternatives, students consider datasets  curated  collaboratively  with  diverse  STEM  communities,  systems  designed  to  expose  rather  than  amplify stereotypes, and governance arrangements granting affected communities  meaningful  authority  over  how  they  are represented.

As mentioned above, I have taught the unit on AI image generators successfully in a total of nine courses. Student evaluations  have  encouraged  me  to  continue  pursuing and finetuning the approach. As readers may already have guessed,  in  the  physics  departments,  many  students  were initially  unconvinced  as  for  the  relevance  of  the  topic. However,  post-course  evaluations  consistently  supported the demand that content like feminist AI should be a core part  of  the  curriculum.  Especially  students  who  initially approached AI systems as neutral tools often arrived at a dramatic re-evaluation of their previous stance. Such shifts do not merely sharpen their ability to identify injustice; they also expand their capacity to imagine alternative configura -tions of technological systems.

The pedagogical implications, thus, are significant. When feminist analysis is embedded within technical curricula rather than confined to optional ethics modules, stu -dents are provided the chance to understand social analysis as integral to responsible technological development. They learn to treat marginalized perspectives as sources of technical knowledge, apply intersectional analysis to complex


<!-- PAGE 7 -->


systems,  and  critically  examine  the  purposes  and  consequences of AI applications rather than focusing solely on performance optimization.

More  broadly,  the  persistence  of  stereotypical  outputs despite  extensive  debiasing  efforts  highlights  the  limita -tions of approaches that frame discrimination primarily as a technical anomaly. FAIF instead demonstrates that just AI requires  structural  change  across  data  practices,  development processes, governance arrangements, and the political economy of AI production. In the context of image generation, this includes questioning engagement-driven platform logics, challenging intellectual property regimes that enable large-scale data extraction without consent, and exploring alternative  ownership  and  governance  models  in  which affected communities exercise meaningful decision-making power.

## 6  Reimagining STEM education: a call for foundational change

This paper has argued that achieving (more) just AI requires a fundamental transformation in how AI systems are conceptualized,  developed,  governed,  and  deployed.  Rather than  treating  ethical  reflection  as  an  optional  add-on  to technical practice, I propose for feminist perspectives to be integrated  as  constitutive  elements  of  technical  expertise throughout all stages of AI development and deployment. Because AI systems operate as sociotechnical assemblages that  embody social and political relations, the capacity to analyse these dynamics should be recognized as an indispensable dimension of technical competence. Building on this premise, the article suggests that feminist perspectives should become integral to STEM education.

It further examined how dominant approaches to AI governance like human-centred AI remain limited in their ability to address structural forms of technological injustice. In response, the paper introduced the Feminist AI Framework as  an  approach capable of addressing both the immediate manifestations of bias in AI systems and the deeper epistemological and ontological assumptions that produce them.

The stakes of this intervention are considerable. As AI systems  become  increasingly  embedded  in  social,  economic,  and  political  infrastructures,  their  design  choices risk stabilizing existing inequalities at unprecedented scale. FAIF  offers  an  alternative  orientation  by  foregrounding marginalized  perspectives,  questioning  taken-for-granted assumptions about knowledge and expertise, and opening space for sociotechnical futures oriented toward collective flourishing rather than extraction and exclusion.

The trajectory of AI development is not predetermined. As of today, AI systems reflect particular configurations of institutional priorities, economic incentives, and epistemic assumptions.  These  configurations,  however,  can  always be organized differently. Feminist AI provides both analyti -cal and practical resources for making alternative choices: asking more adequate questions, recognizing complexities overlooked  by  narrow  technical  approaches,  and  centring the experiences of those most affected by algorithmic sys -tems.  Its  promise  lies  not  in  offering  definitive  solutions, but  in  enabling  more  reflexive,  accountable,  and  justiceoriented forms of AI development.

Realizing this orientation requires sustained interventions across  multiple  domains.  In  education,  feminist  analysis should become a core component of STEM curricula rather than an optional ethical supplement. In research and development,  it  calls  for  community-centred  design  processes, redistributive  funding  structures,  and  governance  mechanisms  that  ensure  meaningful  participation  from  affected communities. In policy and regulation, it requires moving beyond the limits of human-centred AI toward frameworks that explicitly address structural inequality. And in activism and  collective  organizing,  it  involves  supporting  communities  resisting  harmful AI  deployments  while  developing alternatives such as data commons and cooperative technological infrastructures.

Finally, feminist AI must itself remain open to revision. As Haraway reminds us, all analytical frameworks are partial,  located,  and  accountable  to  those  they  affect.  FAIF should therefore be understood not as a definitive solution but  as  a  starting  point  for  ongoing  critical  engagementparticularly from communities most directly impacted by AI systems. The central question is therefore not whether we can afford to pursue such a transformation, but whether we can afford not to.
