Several  empirical  studies  have  documented  an  increasing  familiarity  with  and  use of AI-based applications among university students, particularly since the popularization  of  tools  such  as  ChatGPT.  Black  and  Tomlinson  (2025)  describe  how  students use  this  technology  for  academic  writing  and  information  research,  often  without specific  training  or clear  institutional  guidance  from universities or teaching staff. In exploring  the  benefits  of  AI  in  educational  experiences  (Neeraj  &amp;  Nirmala,  2024) highlight its potential to provide personalized experiences that bridge the gap between theory  and  practice.  However,  one  study  analyzed  papers  generated  by  social  work students  using  AI,  identifying  errors  in  interpretation,  problems  with  citations, plagiarism  and  little  diversity  in  the  sources  used  (Bills,  2025).  These  deficiencies not  only  affect  academic  quality,  but  may  compromise  essential  ethical  principles  of social work, such as integrity and professional competence. The uncritical incorporation  of  AI-generated  content  without  adequate  verification  can  weaken  the  professional  judgment  of  future  graduates,  especially  in  disciplines  based  on  relationship, care  and  ethical  decision  making.  However,  alongside  initial  curiosity  and  interest, there are also concerns and resistance, particularly when AI is perceived as a threat to essential  interpersonal  aspects  of  professional  practice  (Alwadani  et  al.,  2024; Crawford et al.,  2024).

From a theoretical perspective, technology acceptance models have been widely used to understand students' predisposition toward these tools. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT and  its  extended  version,  UTAUT2)  explain  how  people  of  different  ages  adopt  new technologies based on variables such as perceived usefulness, ease of use, social influence, and technological self-efficacy (Obenza et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2024). In a meta-analysis on  the  acceptance  of  artificial  intelligence-based  technologies  in  education,  Ali  et  al. (2024)  demonstrate  strong  associations  between  the  key  variables  of  the  UTAUT  and TAM models and the intention to use, particularly with regard to perceived usefulness and performance expectations. There is accumulating evidence that perceiving academic or professional benefits can foster a positive attitude, whereas anxiety about AI, ethical concerns, and a lack of control over its operation can act as barriers (Budhathoki et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025).

Some recent research has identified distinct profiles among university students based on their attitudes toward AI, incorporating cognitive, emotional, and behavioral factors. Faraon  et  al.  (2025)  found  that  performance  expectancy  was  the  main  predictor  of intention to use ChatGPT, though there were variations between the Nordic countries and  the  United  States.  Tiwari  et  al.  (2023)  and  Surya  Bahadur  et  al.  (2024)  have emphasized  the  importance  of  the  perceived  value  of  learning,  social  influence,  the usefulness of the tool and its legitimacy in shaping the intention to use it.

In the specific case of social work, these issues are particularly relevant because the discipline is based on principles such as empathy, human relationships, ethical care and personalized  intervention.  Several  studies  warn  that  advances  in  AI  technology  may generate  resistance  or  ambivalence  among  those  trained  in  professions  considered 'irreplaceable'  in  human  terms  (Hazaimeh  &amp;  Al-Ansi,  2024;  Saleh  et  al.,  2025).  The potential  for  automated  systems  to  take  over  certain  functions  (e.g.  diagnosis,  case

classification,  referral  and  bureaucratic  follow-up)  may  trigger  feelings  of  threat  or distrust, particularly in the absence of critical reflection.

Similarly,  the  use  of  AI  in  educational  settings  is  not  solely  for  the  purpose  of increasing efficiency. Al-Kamzari and Alias (2025) highlight its potential to personalize learning and encourage participation in secondary physics teaching through tools such as virtual labs and intelligent tutors. However, they also address the challenges associated with teacher preparation and data privacy, emphasizing the importance of balancing AI usage  with  fostering  ethical  judgment  and  critical  thinking  skills.  In  a  similar  vein, Palmquist  et  al.  (2025)  propose  an  integrated  framework  combining  AI  literacy  with social and emotional competencies such as self-regulation and empathy. They conclude that this approach can enhance personalized learning, foster collaboration, and address ethical challenges. This is particularly relevant for social intervention-oriented degrees, where technological and relational competencies must be developed together.

From this perspective, understanding students' attitudes toward artificial intelligence becomes a priority. The present study aims to explore the instrumental beliefs, as well as the emotional, ethical, and professional positions, of social work students in the face of AI advancement,  identifying  the  factors  that  shape  their  acceptance,  rejection,  or ambivalence.

## Material and method

## Participants

The  survey  sample  consisted  of  42  undergraduate  social  work  students  at  a  Spanish public  university.  Participation  was  voluntary  and  anonymous.  No  individual  sociodemographic  data,  such  as  age  or  gender,  were  collected.  The  survey  was  distributed and completed electronically. As the study involved an anonymous, non-intrusive survey and no personal or sensitive data were collected, it complied with the ethical regulations of  the  institution,  and  a  full  review  by  the  institutional  ethics/IRB  committee  was  not required.

## Instruments

## Quantitative questionnaire

An adaptation of the General Attitudes Towards Artificial Intelligence Scale (GAAIS) was  applied  (Schepman  &amp;  Rodway,  2020,  2023).  This  instrument  has  demonstrated robust psychometric properties for assessing general attitudes toward AI, distinguishing between perceived positive and negative aspects-a structure confirmed through confirmatory factor analyses conducted in large simples (Schepman &amp; Rodway, 2020, 2023). Subsequent  studies  have  further  supported  its  cross-cultural  validity,  as  shown  by successful adaptations in Korean, Italian, Chinese, and Turkish contexts (Cicero et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Kaya et al., 2024; Seo &amp; Ahn, 2022), reinforcing its reliability and relevance for use in diverse educational settings.

The scale was adapted by the removal of items 1, 2, 3, 11, 13, 14 and 17 (due to their reference  to  generic  AI  use,  e.g.  'Artificial  intelligence  can  provide  new  economic opportunities for this country') and the subsequent addition of two items referring to

A further three items were concerned with reflections on AI and its potential, namely: 'The development of artificial intelligence has been demonstrated to be of benefit to human beings.'

The questionnaire with 17-items was used to explore students' attitudes toward artificial intelligence (AI) in social work. The 17-item instrument was rated on a five-point Likert scale  (0 = Strongly  disagree;  1 = Disagree;  2 = Neutral;  3 = Agree;  4 = Strongly  agree)  and referred to students' perceptions, beliefs, emotions, and dispositions regarding the future use of AI in social and professional contexts. Negatively worded items were recorded to maintain the directionality of the scale.

## Qualitative open-ended questions

In addition, the questionnaire incorporated four open-ended questions to gain a deeper and  more  nuanced  understanding  of  student  perceptions.  These  questions  aimed  to identify: 1) tasks that are irreplaceable for social workers versus AI, 2) tasks that could be automated or replaced, 3) a comparison of human and artificial communication skills, and 4) the potential contributions of AI to social work.

## Procedure

The questionnaire was distributed digitally as part of the Communication Techniques module in the fourth year of the Bachelor's degree in social work. Students were informed in advance about the formative and analytical objectives of the questionnaire and asked to consent to the anonymous use of their data for educational research purposes.

## Data analysis

The  data  were  analyzed  using  IBM  SPSS  Statistics  software  (version  28).  Descriptive analyses were performed, as well as the calculation of internal reliability using Cronbach's alpha  coefficient.  An  exploratory  factor  analysis  (EFA)  was  also  performed  using  the principal components method and Varimax rotation. The objective was to identify the latent dimensions of the questionnaire and establish attitudinal profiles toward AI.

Following the quantitative analysis, the responses to the open-ended questions were analyzed  using  an  inductive  thematic  coding  procedure,  appropriate  for  exploratory studies aimed at identifying patterns grounded in participants' perspectives rather than predefined theoretical categories (Braun &amp; Clarke, 2006). Based on a systematic reading of the texts, emerging categories were established and organized according to common semantic themes. For each category, representative textual examples and the number of participants  who  mentioned it were  recorded.  This  qualitative  analysis  complemented the interpretation of the quantitative results, providing a contextualized understanding of the meanings attributed to artificial intelligence by the students.

## Results

## Descriptive statistics

The descriptive analysis of the 17 items of the questionnaire on attitudes toward artificial intelligence revealed a pattern of responses centered on intermediate values of the Likert scale (0-4), with some tendency toward neutrality or moderate agreement. The highest scores were observed for items such as 'I believe that social work professionals will soon be replaced by artificial intelligence' ( M = 3.31, SD = 0.95), 'I am impressed with all that artificial intelligence can do' ( M = 2.98, SD = 0.95) and 'People like me will be replaced by artificial  intelligence'  ( M = 3.31,  SD = 0.84).  In  contrast,  particularly  low  means  were recorded  for  items  such  as  'I  would  like  to  use  artificial  intelligence  systems  in  all activities  of  my  daily  life'  ( M = 0.52,  SD = 0.67),  'Artificial  intelligence  works  better than  people'  ( M = 0.71,  SD = 0.77)  or  'I  would  like  to  use  artificial  intelligence  in  all activities of my job in social work' ( M = 0.74, SD = 0.79).

The overall  attitude  score  toward  artificial  intelligence,  calculated  as  the  mean  of the 17 items (after recoding those formulated in a negative sense), presented a mean of M = 1.94  (SD = 0.37),  with  a  symmetrical  distribution  (skewness = 0.124)  and  no significant  kurtosis  (0.103),  suggesting  an  approximately  normal  distribution.

## Scale reliability

Cronbach's alpha coefficient for all 17 items was α = 0.661, indicating acceptable internal consistency in exploratory studies, although there is room for improvement. This value suggests  that  the  items  share  a  moderate  common  variance  in  measuring  the  general construct of attitude toward artificial intelligence, but could also be capturing differentiated dimensions, which motivated the exploratory factor analysis.

## Exploratory factor analysis

An exploratory factor analysis was conducted to identify underlying patterns in students' responses and to examine how different items clustered together into meaningful dimensions  of  attitudes  toward  artificial  intelligence.  The  analysis  was  performed  using  the principal  components  method  with  orthogonal  (Varimax)  rotation,  which  facilitates clearer interpretation by maximizing the distinction between factors. The suitability of the data for this analysis was assessed using standard diagnostic tests. The Kaiser-Meyer -Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy yielded a value of .550, indicating a low but acceptable level for exploratory purposes in small samples. Bartlett's test of sphericity was  statistically  significant  (χ 2 (136) = 308.991, p &lt; .001),  confirming  that  correlations among  items  were  sufficient  to  justify  factor  extraction.  The  analysis  resulted  in  six components with eigenvalues greater than one, together accounting for 74.12% of the total variance. This indicates that a substantial proportion of students' response variability  can  be  explained  by  a  limited  number  of  underlying  attitudinal  dimensions. Communalities  after  extraction  ranged  from  .477  to  .877,  suggesting  that  most  items were well represented by the factor solution. The rotation converged after eight iterations and produced a conceptually interpretable structure, with no problematic cross-loadings. ## Qualitative results

The  qualitative  results  provide  further  insight  into  students'  perceptions  of  artificial intelligence  (AI)  in  social  work.  In  response  to  the  first  question,  the  majority  argued that  certain  aspects  of  professional  work  cannot  be  replaced  by  AI,  emphasizing  the importance of empathy and human skills in particular. Direct  contact  and  the  human  dimension  were  considered irreplaceable,  as  was  the  ability  to  adapt  to  unique  situations.  One  student  explains: 'What the AI does is copy previous examples,' reflecting a lack of trust in its flexibility.

However,  there  is  still  a  clear  perception  of  a  limit  to  direct  intervention.  While recognizing  the  operational  utility  of  AI,  it  is  insisted  that  'a  person  is  better.'  Some responses are skeptical or ambiguous, such as 'I don't think there is something that could clearly be replaced' or 'I don't know.' These statements reveal an openness to AI acting as a  supportive tool, provided it does not invade the relational or ethical spaces of social work.

In reference to the third question, the vast majority of students disagreed that AI could possess greater communication skills than social work professionals. Responses stated that human communication 'cannot be replaced by artificial intelligence' and that AI is unable to pick up 'non-verbal language or certain changes in tone.' One student explained that, without a camera, AI would not be able to observe people's non-verbal language.

The importance of authentic empathy and ethical judgment was also emphasized, with one  participant  noting  that  'social  workers  are  irreplaceable  in  terms  of  authentic empathy, interpretation of non-verbal cues and complex ethical judgement.' Although it was acknowledged that AI could have advantages in terms of the volume of information, this was considered insufficient for establishing meaningful communication. 'Yes in quantity, but not in quality.'

Some answers acknowledge possible technical advantages, such as 'multilingualism and availability,' or even compare with professionals in training: 'an AI may have more skills than certain professionals who are still learning.' However, it is insisted that the human ability to adapt to unpredictable reactions, interpret ironies or generate authentic listening cannot be emulated. The lack of humanity in automated interactions is reflected in statements such as, 'a machine is programmed to respond . . . but it lacks that bit of humanity.'

In  the  fourth  and  final  question,  students  recognized  the  usefulness  of  AI  as a complementary tool in social work. They highlighted its potential to 'facilitate and advance bureaucratic processes,' 'speed up procedures' or 'prepare reports.' These assessments are associated with a practical vision of AI as a support to free up time in direct intervention.

Benefits  were  also  identified  in  resource  management  and  data  analysis,  especially with vulnerable groups: 'creating applications to learn more about society, helping with aspects  such  as  unwanted  loneliness,  detecting  bullying  or  suicidal  ideas.'  Its  role  in 'classifying,  organizing  and  referring  cases'  was  valued,  especially  in  contexts  of  high professional overload.

At  an  organizational  level,  it  was  suggested  that  AI  could  improve  institutional networking and facilitate users' access to relevant information. For example, one participant  suggested  that  it  could  help  with  mass  awareness  campaigns  on  certain  issues. Other  interventions  point  to  possible  methodological  contributions,  always  under  the principle of not replacing the human component: 'Develop methodologies that benefit the community without replacing people.'

Finally, some participants expressed skepticism or a lack of knowledge: 'It is probable, but since I have not used it much, I have no idea.' Some placed its use in exceptional contexts: 'To respond to emergency situations when there are no professionals available.' Overall,  a  nuanced  and  pragmatic  perception  emerges.  AI  can  increase  operational efficiency,  but  it  should  not  compromise  relational  ethics  or  replace  the  need  for a professional presence.

This  qualitative  analysis  reveals  a  tension  between  technological  openness  and  the defense  of  a  human  approach  to  social  work.  Students  believe  that  AI  can  improve processes  as  long  as  it  does  not  replace  core  functions  requiring  ethical  judgment, empathy, and personal engagement.

## Discussion

The results of this exploratory study offer valuable insights into the attitudes of social work students toward AI. These attitudes are complex and nuanced, as they are articulated  around  affective,  cognitive,  and  professional  components,  moving  away  from a dichotomy of acceptance or rejection. This multifactorial structure is consistent with the  findings  of  Maphosa  and  Maphosa  (2023),  who  argue  that  attitudes  toward  AI  in higher education cannot be explained by a single factor, but rather manifest as a set of multidimensional beliefs where functionality, emotions, and ethics coexist in an often non-linear way.

The integration of quantitative and qualitative results reveals both convergence and complementarity.  On  the  one  hand,  the  overall  score  of  the  questionnaire,  which gravitates  around  intermediate  values,  mirrors  the  ambivalence  observed  in  students' open responses-where technological utility is recognized but ethical concerns prevail. The identification of six distinct components in the exploratory factor analysis, including dimensions such as professional threat, admiration, and dystopian anxiety, aligns closely with the thematic patterns that emerged in the narrative data. For example, the factor reflecting  perceived  threat  corresponds  with  qualitative  expressions  of  fear  regarding dehumanization and the erosion of empathy in social work. Conversely, the qualitative findings  added  contextual  depth  and  ethical  nuance,  such  as  the  symbolic  weight attributed to the notion of 'humanity' or the cautious optimism about AI's administrative role-elements not fully captured in the quantitative structure. Rather than contradiction,  the  two  strands  of  data  jointly  portray  a  reflective  student  body  navigating a complex technological landscape. This coherence reinforces the validity of the mixedmethods design and enriches the interpretative potential of the findings.

## Threat

The identification of a component focused on professional threat represents one of the most significant contributions of this study. This perception of human role displacement in social work is indicative of the tension between the ideals of automation and the reality of the social professions. Recent research in South Asian university settings indicates that, although perceived performance promotes AI implementation, concerns linked to professional  identity  and  job  security  may  act  as  a  hindrance  (Dahri  et  al.,  2024). Furthermore, Strzelecki (2024) emphasizes that the adoption of tools such as ChatGPT in the university setting is strongly determined by the disciplinary environment and the impact on the autonomy of human judgment. This fear is complemented by a dimension termed 'dystopian anxiety.'

It has been expressed by students that they are fearful of a future in which artificial intelligence  is  autonomous,  dehumanized  and  difficult  to  control.  This  emotional response  has  been  documented  in  studies  such  as  those  by  Budhathoki  et  al.  (2024),

(2025) emphasize that the impact of technological self-efficacy is diminished when students' perceptions are predominantly influenced by negative emotions.

A further noteworthy finding is the ambivalence exhibited by some students. While there  is  recognition  of  the  practical  utility  or  instrumental  value  of  AI  in  the  work environment,  there  persists  a  concomitant  fear,  manifesting  as  affective  resistance  in the joint saturation of positive and negative items. This ambivalence was identified in Ali et  al. ## Ambivalence (mixed feelings)

A further salient finding is the ambivalence that characterizes a segment of the student body. While there is recognition of the advantages of AI in terms of practical utility or instrumental value in the work environment, there persists a fear, manifesting as affective resistance, characterized by a simultaneous positive and negative response. This ambivalence has been identified in the meta-analysis of Ali et al. (2024), where the connections between ease of use, perceived usefulness and attitude are robust, but not linear, and are modulated by confidence in the technology and clarity of purpose.

## Fascination

Finally, although less representative, the components related to technological fascination and admiration  reveal  a  'technophile'  attitudinal  profile  among  a  proportion  of  students.  As Kanont et al. (2024) have demonstrated, this fascination can act as a driver of action in the acceptance of AI as an educational assistant, highlighting the role of enthusiasm. Nevertheless, it must be noted that this approach also has the potential to engender an uncritical perspective that serves to obscure the technology's social and ethical ramifications.

The  results  of  the  qualitative  analysis  provide  a  rich  source  of  data  that  can  be interpreted in various ways, thus facilitating access to the underlying value frameworks. Students  attribute  instrumental  value  to  AI  in  repetitive,  analytical,  or  administrative tasks  such  as  case  triage,  document  organization,  and  identification  of  situations  of loneliness. Nevertheless, a symbolic boundary is maintained: social intervention, active listening and empathy are considered non-delegable spheres.

This  finding  aligns  with  the  perspective  articulated  by  Palmquist  et  al.  (2025),  who underscore the imperative for the integration of AI in educational settings to be accompanied by  the  cultivation  of  emotional,  ethical,  and  relational  competencies,  particularly  within professional intervention degree programmes. In this sense, the results of the present study serve  to  reinforce  the  importance  of  ethical  judgment  in  defining  the  limits  of  AI  use. Concerns expressed by students regarding technological dehumanisation and loss of agency cannot  be  considered  a  simple  conservative  rejection;  rather,  they  should  be  regarded  as a demand for contextualisation and control. The student body does not deny the value of AI; rather,  it  demands  that  its  implementation  be  guided  by  professional  and  pedagogical principles.  This  standpoint  is  consistent  with  the  propositions  set  forth  by  Luo  et  al.


<!-- PAGE 12 -->


(2025),  who  argue  for  an  expanded  conception  of  AI  literacy  that  encompasses  not  only technical  proficiencies,  but  also  ethical  literacy  and  the  capacity  for  critical  deliberation. A hallmark of qualitative discourse is the coexistence of positions that appear to be contradictory. This ambivalence should not be interpreted as an absence of judgment, but rather as an indication of a reflective attitude and an ongoing process.

While acknowledging the potential of AI, the student body emphasizes the importance of maintaining professional judgment and the quality of human relationships, even as it recognizes the functional applicability of AI. This narrative tension is consistent with the 'technological double consciousness' model, which views innovation not as a replacement for  ethical  judgment, but as a call to critically redefine its applications (Palmquist et al., 2025). A further salient finding of the qualitative analysis is the pivotal symbolic function of 'humanity.' It is evident that students do not delineate the profession exclusively in terms of technical competencies, but also in terms of affective, ethical, and situated dimensions. The use of terms such as empathy, active listening, presence, bonding, closeness and feelings indicates  a  preference  for  AI  as  a  supportive  instrument  rather  than  as  an  independent agent.  Therefore,  the  repeated  allusion  to  humanity  as  an  irreplaceable  feature  in  social work practice suggests that students are concerned not only with technical functionality, but also with preserving the profession's sense of identity. This finding serves to reinforce the  notion that affective-relational  dimensions are not regarded as 'additions' to professional  performance,  but  rather  as  intrinsic  components  of  it.  The  results  also  reveal a  significant  minority  of  students  who  claim  never  to  have  used  applications  such  as ChatGPT, citing distrust or a lack of knowledge as the reason for avoiding them.

The findings indicate that a considerable proportion of students have never utilized applications such as ChatGPT, either due to a lack of trust or due to an absence of the necessary knowledge. The findings of this study indicate the presence of a digital literacy gap that exerts an influence on attitudes toward AI, irrespective of academic aptitude. These findings  suggest  that  a  reevaluation  of  training  processes  is  necessary,  with  the integration of not only access to technology, but also the development of a critical, safe, and meaningful relationship with it. These findings encourage a reconsideration of the manner in which AI is introduced in university curricula. Rather than encouraging solely technical  or  utilitarian  use,  a  pedagogical  approach  is  required  that  promotes  ethical reflection, an understanding of algorithms, an analysis of biases and deliberation on their impact  on  human  relationships.  Digital  literacy  must  be  accompanied  by  emotional literacy,  particularly  in  subjects  related  to  care,  education  and  social  intervention.  As Tiwari et al. (2023) suggest, the meaningful adoption of these technologies is contingent on learners recognizing their value in specific contexts and perceiving that they respond to authentic needs, rather than those imposed on them.

This study contributes to the growing empirical literature on the integration of AI in higher  education  and,  more  specifically,  in  social  work  training.  While  much  of  the existing  research  has  focused  on  technological  adoption  from  a  general  perspective (Budhathoki  et  al.,  2024;  Strzelecki,  2024),  our  findings  provide  evidence  from a  contextually  grounded,  discipline-specific  lens.  The  nuanced  attitudes  identifiedparticularly  the  tension  between  perceived  utility  and  ethical  boundaries-align  with and  expand  on  prior  studies  that  highlight  the  importance  of  professional  identity, emotional  engagement,  and  ethical  deliberation  in  the  reception  of  AI  technologies (Luo et al., 2025; Palmquist et al., 2025). Moreover, the results reinforce previous claims

about the role of disciplinary culture in shaping perceptions of AI (Dahri et al., 2024), offering empirical support from the under-researched field of social work education in Spain. In doing so, this study enriches the international dialogue on AI literacy and its pedagogical implications, emphasizing the need for critical, value-based approaches to digital integration in the human services professions.

## Strengths and limitations of the study

From a methodological perspective, it is important to note that combining exploratory factor  analysis  with  qualitative  thematic  analysis  has  enabled  the  capture  of  both  the latent structure of attitudes and their explicit meanings. This triangulation approach has been demonstrated to facilitate a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon than would be possible with a single approach (Fetters et al., 2013). Nevertheless, the study is not without its limitations. Firstly, the size of the sample and its disciplinary composition  restrict  the  generalizability  of  the  results.  It  is  recommended  that  future studies  include  larger  comparative  samples  across  degree  levels  in  order  to  explore possible differential patterns. Secondly, although the scale used has been preliminarily validated,  it  could  benefit  from  further  refinement  to  include  items  on  agency,  selfregulation, and concrete usage practices. Furthermore, longitudinal studies exploring the evolution  of  attitudes  toward  AI  in  training  and  work  environments,  as  AI  becomes increasingly prevalent, would be valuable.

The present study has shown that social work students' attitudes toward AI are not polarized  but  shaped  by  ethical  concerns,  emotional  ambivalence,  and  professional identity. These findings underscore the urgent need for social work educators to move beyond  purely  technical  training  and  address  the  ethical,  relational,  and  contextual dimensions of AI in the classroom. Integrating critical AI literacy into curricula-covering algorithmic bias, data ethics, and the irreplaceable value of human judgement-can better prepare students for future professional scenarios. Educators should foster spaces for reflection and debate, enabling students to develop not only digital competence, but also the ethical sensitivity and critical thinking necessary to assess when and how to use AI  in  practice.  The  integration  of  AI  into  social  work  education  must  therefore  be intentional,  value-driven,  and  oriented  toward  preserving  the  core  principles  of  care, dignity, and human connection that define the profession. This transformative approach can help ensure that AI becomes a meaningful and responsible ally-rather than a threat -within the field of social intervention.

## Implications for social work education

These  findings  underscore  the  need  for  social  work  education  to  integrate  artificial intelligence  not  merely  as  a  technical  skill  but  as  a  domain  requiring  critical,  ethical, and relational competencies. Educators should promote AI literacy that includes understanding  algorithms,  bias,  and  ethical  dilemmas,  while  simultaneously  reinforcing  the humanistic core of the profession-empathy, presence, and ethical judgment-as irreplaceable elements. Pedagogical strategies such as case-based analysis, reflective assignments  using  AI  tools  (e.g.  ChatGPT),  and  guided  debates  can  help  students  develop informed, context-sensitive perspectives. Given the digital literacy gaps and emotional

Rather  than  viewing  AI  as a  replacement or threat, its introduction should serve to enhance professional identity and equip future practitioners with both technological fluency and critical awareness.
