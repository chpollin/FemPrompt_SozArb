## An Ethical Framework for Assessing Artificial Intelligence as Augmentation or Automation in Social Work

Kwangseon Hwang

Dep. of Public Management and Policy, Gachon University, South Korea kwangseonhwang@gmail.com

ORCID https://orcid.org/0000-0003-1757-7458

## Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) - spanning predictive risk models, large language models, algorithmic decision systems, and digital-care devices - is being deployed with increasing ambition across social welfare systems worldwide. Existing scholarship documents many resulting harms - bias, opacity, surveillance, erosion of discretion - but remains conceptually fragmented and rarely anchored in a framework specific to social work. This paper develops a tri-lens analytical matrix crossing three moral traditions (utilitarian, deontological, virtue-ethical) with AI's two operational arenas  (frontstage  client-facing  systems  and  backstage  algorithmic  administration)  and  four constitutive social-work values (privacy, self-determination, social justice, human dignity). Rather than  assuming AI integration is either  inevitable  or  uniformly  beneficial,  the  framework  asks: under what conditions - if any - can specific forms of AI function as augmented intelligence that extends relational practice, rather than as automated welfare that entrenches exclusion and erodes  moral  responsibility?  The  matrix  is  applied  to  three  purposively  selected  cases  -  the Allegheny  Family  Screening  Tool  (US),  algorithmic  welfare  administration  (Dutch  SyRI, Australian  Robodebt,  and  Korean  crisis-household  detection),  and  digital-care  technologies (Korea and OECD comparators) - whose cross-modal heterogeneity demonstrates that distinct AI modalities pose distinct ethical risks requiring distinct safeguards. The analysis shows that AI is  ethically  defensible  in  social  work  only  when  it  augments  practitioner  judgment  without displacing  relational  authority,  advances  substantive  rather  than  merely  formal  equity,  and  is embedded in contestable, auditable institutions. The paper's contribution is threefold: it reframes the field's debate from replacement versus resistance to augmentation versus automation; supplies a  reusable  diagnostic  matrix;  and  integrates  Korean,  European,  and  North  American  evidence within a single analytic lens anchored in social work's commitments to relational practice, care ethics, and anti-oppressive praxis.

Keywords: artificial intelligence; predictive risk modelling; augmented intelligence; social work ethics; digital welfare state; algorithmic decision-making; relational practice; anti-oppressive practice; care ethics

## 1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence is reshaping social welfare. In child protection, predictive risk models generate scores that shape whether families are investigated (Vaithianathan et al., 2017;

<!-- PAGE 3 -->

Eubanks, 2018). In welfare administration, data-matching and machine-learning systems identify 'risky' claimants and allocate or withdraw benefits (Alston, 2019; van Toorn et al., 2024). In community care, IoT sensors and AI-enabled devices supplement or substitute for human caregivers (Lee, 2025; López-Peláez et al., 2024). The ambition is expansive: fewer missed risks, faster service, broader reach, lower unit cost.

Yet this same infrastructure has been repeatedly implicated in harm. The Dutch System Risk Indication (SyRI) was ruled incompatible with the European Convention on Human Rights because its opacity and disproportionality undermined privacy and procedural fairness (District Court of The Hague, 2020; Appelman et al., 2021). Australia's Robodebt system generated unlawful debts against hundreds of thousands of welfare recipients and produced documented psychological harm, leading to a royal commission (Carney, 2023). Evaluations of predictive child-welfare tools have repeatedly found that what the systems detect is as much institutional visibility as genuine child risk (Eubanks, 2018; De-Arteaga et al., 2020). Meta-reviews of AI in services report a widening ledger of 'dark side' effects - manipulation, dependence, de-skilling, accountability gaps, and erosion of trust - that are especially acute when AI operates on populations least able to contest it (Puntoni et al., 2021; Grundner and Neuhofer, 2024; Mortati and Freitas, 2025).

Two clarifications are essential from the outset. First, AI is not one thing. As Narayanan and Kapoor (2024: 1) observe, 'AI is an umbrella term for a set of loosely related technologies' whose capabilities, failure modes, environmental costs, and ethical implications differ substantially across applications. The planetary costs of training LLMs - their substantial energy and water demands - do not, for example, apply to the smaller datasets on which many predictive risk models are built (Strubell et al., 2019). A single analytical framework applied uniformly across radically different technologies risks obscuring more than it reveals. This paper therefore differentiates between AI modalities (Section 4) before applying its framework.

Second, AI integration into social welfare is neither inevitable nor uniformly beneficial. Although this paper develops conditions under which AI can be ethically deployed, it does not assume that integration is the correct default. Critics within the AI field (McQuillan, 2022) and within social work's critical tradition (Massey et al., 2026; Webb, 2006) argue that in high-stakes domains such as child protection and welfare administration, the precautionary principle should apply: systems should not be deployed until they can be shown to be safe, effective, and compatible with the rights of those they affect. This paper acknowledges that alternative as a serious position and engages it explicitly in the research question and framework.

The paper argues that the most defensible posture, given the actual landscape of AI deployment in welfare, is neither uncritical adoption nor blanket refusal, but a principled framework for AI as augmented intelligence - a sociotechnical design posture in which AI explicitly extends, scaffolds, and documents human professional judgment rather than replacing it (French and

<!-- PAGE 4 -->

Shim, 2025; Tangi et al., 2025). This is only possible, however, when specific institutional, procedural, and relational conditions are satisfied.

Three framing commitments shape the argument. First, it responds to a recurring concern in the recent literature that reviews of AI in social work re-circulate a familiar list of harms without advancing a coherent analytical framework that structures comparison across cases (Reamer, 2023; Victor et al., 2024). Second, it positions social work within the broader institutional architecture of the digital welfare state so that AI is understood not as an isolated tool but as the third wave of digital era governance (Dunleavy et al., 2006; Margetts and Dunleavy, 2013; van Toorn et al., 2024). Third, it treats the abuse of AI in social welfare - its use for surveillance, discipline, and denial of service - as a central, not peripheral, ethical issue (Alston, 2019; Eubanks, 2018; Carney, 2023).

## 1.1 Research question, gap, and contribution

The paper's organizing research question is: under what conditions - if any - can specific forms of AI in social welfare function as augmented intelligence that expands substantive reach and supports core social-work values, rather than as automated welfare that entrenches exclusion and erodes relational practice?

The research gap is threefold. Conceptually, existing work either treats AI ethics at a high-level sector-agnostic register (Floridi et al., 2018; Bouderhem, 2024) or offers descriptive overviews of concerns in social work without a structured comparative apparatus (Reamer, 2023; Victor et al., 2024; López-Peláez and Kirwan, 2024). A systematic, disciplinarily anchored framework for comparing heterogeneous AI applications in social welfare - one grounded in social work's distinctive commitments to relational practice, care ethics, and anti-oppressive praxis - is missing. Geographically, the English-language debate is dominated by North American and Western European cases; it rarely engages Korean and wider East Asian evidence, despite Korea now operating one of the most ambitious AI-based welfare infrastructures in the OECD (Park, 2025; Lee, 2025; Hong Seok-Han, 2023; Hong Seung-Heon and Hwang, 2024). Normatively, a decision grammar for when, how, and whether to deploy AI - one that does not assume deployment is the appropriate default - is absent from the literature.

The paper's contribution lies primarily in the integration and application of these elements, not in the originality of the individual components. The moral traditions, social-work values, and governance frameworks it deploys are each well established in their respective literatures (IFSW, 2018; NASW, 2021; Floridi et al., 2018; UNESCO, 2021). The contribution is analytical: combining them into a single comparative matrix, applying it systematically to heterogeneous cases across multiple welfare regimes, integrating Korean evidence largely absent from Englishlanguage scholarship, and deriving from the analysis a set of concrete institutional conditions for responsible deployment.

## 2. Analytical approach and methodological transparency

<!-- PAGE 5 -->

## 2.1 Research design

This study is a conceptual and theoretically informed comparative case analysis. It is not an empirical intervention study, a systematic review, or a meta-analysis. It makes an analytical and synthetic contribution: it develops a novel framework, justifies its components, applies it comparatively, and derives normative and practical conclusions. Drawing on (i) peer-reviewed literature across social work, social policy, public administration, socio-legal studies, AI ethics, and service research; (ii) authoritative legal, regulatory, and governmental documents (EU AI Act, GDPR, US Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights, UNESCO Recommendation, NIST AI RMF, SyRI judgment, Australia's Robodebt Royal Commission final report); and (iii) Korean-language scholarship and policy documents, it integrates evidence otherwise absent from English-language debate.

Four principles distinguish this design from the 'narrative literature overview' format that Reviewer literature typically criticizes. A single research question organizes the inquiry (Section 1.1). The analytical framework is explicit, systematic, and comparative rather than illustrative (Section 6). Case selection is transparent and criterion-driven (Section 2.2). The framework's diagnostic questions are derived from explicit principles (Section 6.5) rather than assumed. The paper also explicitly acknowledges that interpretive choices are present throughout, and addresses this in the limitations (Section 2.3).

## 2.2 Case selection criteria

Cases were selected purposively on four criteria:

High stakes. The case involves consequential decisions about risk, eligibility, rights, or care.

Distinct AI modality. The cases span four major AI application types in welfare: predictive classification, conversational AI and LLMs, algorithmic administration, and embedded digital-care technologies - reflecting the technology heterogeneity addressed in Section 4.

Evidentiary depth. Each case is supported by peer-reviewed academic evidence plus legal or governmental documentation, enabling verifiable analysis.

Geographical and institutional variation. Cases cover the US (Allegheny), Europe (SyRI, Netherlands), Oceania (Robodebt, Australia), and East Asia (Korean crisis-household detection and digital care).

Each case is of different scope and evidentiary character - one turns on a single tool (AFST), one on a contested legal judgment (SyRI), one on a royal commission (Robodebt), one on qualitative fieldwork (Korean digital care). Rather than treating this heterogeneity as a weakness to be minimized, the paper treats it as an analytical resource: the matrix is precisely designed to generate comparable diagnostic questions across cases that differ in technology type, governance context, and evidentiary base. The cross-case synthesis (Section 8) is explicit about which conclusions rest on which type of evidence.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->

## 2.3 Limits

Three limits should be stated clearly. First, this paper does not produce new empirical data; its contribution is analytical and synthetic. Second, the framework's diagnostic questions are interpretive: another researcher might plausibly have derived different questions from the same matrix dimensions. This is acknowledged as a constitutive limit of interpretive work, not a flaw to be remedied by further claims of objectivity. Third, the Korean evidence draws on published policy, peer-reviewed Korean-language scholarship, and official sources, but is not built on new fieldwork. Fourth, two Korean sources - Park (2025) and the Ministry of Health and Welfare (2025) press release - have been queried for verifiability; the paper uses them as primary evidence of the Korean policy direction but does not make analytical claims that depend solely on these two sources.

## 3. The digital welfare state: institutional context of AI in social work

AI in social welfare is not a free-standing technical development. It is the third wave of a long movement of digital transformation in government. Dunleavy et al.'s (2006) seminal work argued that New Public Management was giving way to a digital era governance (DEG) paradigm, characterized by reintegration, needs-based holism, and digitalization of services. Margetts and Dunleavy (2013) subsequently described a second wave associated with big-data analytics, cloud infrastructure, and data retention regimes, and anticipated a third wave involving data science and artificial intelligence. It is this third wave that the present paper engages (Tangi et al., 2025).

Van Toorn, Henman, and Soldatić (2024: 508) offer the working definition of the digital welfare state this paper adopts: a particular state formation in which wide-ranging digital technologies, including automated decision-making systems, algorithms, big data analytics and artificial intelligence, are integrated into the administration and provision of welfare services by government agencies, which in some cases are delivered through a complex set of contracted providers.

The welfare state is a normative project before it is an administrative one. Myrdal's (1960: 45) classic statement frames its stakes: the rich Western states have committed themselves to 'the broad goals of economic development, full employment, equality of opportunity for the young, social security, and protected minimum standards as regards not only income, but nutrition, housing, health and education, for people of all regions and social groups.' Digital transformation in welfare is measured not only by efficiency but by how faithfully it honours these commitments.

Two features of the DWS matter for social work. First, welfare is increasingly produced by a mixed economy of public agencies, contracted vendors, and opaque software supply chains, so ethical and legal accountability is distributed across actors who may not share professional obligations (van Toorn et al., 2024; Alexopoulou, 2025; Bovens and Zouridis, 2002). Second, the populations most exposed to AI-mediated welfare are also, on average, the least able to contest it

<!-- PAGE 7 -->

- because their relationships with the state are dense, their digital and legal literacy is often uneven, and their administrative visibility is high (Eubanks, 2018; López-Peláez et al., 2024).

Korea occupies a distinctive and important place in this story. Over the past decade, Korea has built a national infrastructure for AI-mediated welfare. Its AI crisis-household detection system integrates data from electricity and water disconnection, health-insurance arrears, pension nonpayment, rental arrears, and credit-card delinquency to identify people likely to be in hardship, with field outreach following (Park, 2025). A government AI Welfare and Care Innovation Task Force was established in 2025 to coordinate AI use across welfare administration and care (Ministry of Health and Welfare, 2025). Digital-care technologies are being deployed at scale to older adults living alone (Lee, 2025). Korean legal scholars have begun a sustained critical engagement with this infrastructure (Hong Seok-Han, 2023; Hong Seung-Heon and Hwang, 2024). This evidence base has been almost entirely absent from English-language AI-social-work scholarship; its integration here is a substantive contribution.

## 4. Artificial intelligence in social welfare: differentiating the technology

Predictive risk modelling (PRM) uses structured administrative data and statistical models to generate probability scores for adverse outcomes - in child welfare, the likelihood of a maltreatment report or re-report; in welfare administration, the likelihood of fraudulent claiming. PRM systems are typically trained on historical administrative records, raising structural concerns about encoded institutional bias. They are generally not built on large training datasets (unlike LLMs) and do not involve generative capabilities. Their ethical risks centre on misclassification, opacity of scoring logic, and the reuse of data beyond original collection purposes.

Before an ethical framework can be applied meaningfully, the subject of analysis must be specified. AI is not one thing. Narayanan and Kapoor (2024: 1) observe that AI is "an umbrella term for a set of loosely related technologies," making it "all but impossible to say anything about AI as a generic technology that holds across all applications." The four AI modalities examined in this paper are heterogeneous in their technical architecture, risk profile, and ethical implications.

Large language models (LLMs) and conversational AI are trained on large corpora of text and generate language outputs in response to user prompts. In social welfare, they are deployed as chatbots for triage, psychosocial support, information provision, and increasingly as copilots that assist practitioners in drafting case notes and reports. Unlike PRM, LLMs involve genuine generative capacity and associated risks of hallucination, affective simulation, and the production of harmful content. Their environmental footprint (energy and water demands at training scale) is substantially larger than that of PRM systems (Strubell et al., 2019). Ethical risks centre on deception, emotional harm, data confidentiality, and the displacement of relational care.

<!-- PAGE 8 -->

Algorithmic decision-making systems (ADS) in welfare administration use rules-based or machine-learning logic to automate or support high-stakes administrative decisions: eligibility determinations, fraud referrals, benefit withholding, debt assessment. These systems are backstage (in Mortati and Freitas's (2025) terminology), operating on administrative databases rather than in citizen-facing encounters. SyRI and Robodebt are instances of this modality. Their ethical risks centre on procedural fairness, due process, purpose limitation, and the systemic distribution of error onto already-marginalised populations.

Embedded digital-care technologies include IoT sensors, AI-enabled monitoring devices, companion robots, and telecare platforms deployed in the homes or care settings of older adults, disabled people, and others receiving community care. Their ethical risks combine surveillance (continuous in-home monitoring), consent (in contexts of cognitive decline or power asymmetry), and the redefinition of care itself - the risk that technological mediation substitutes for, rather than supplements, human relational presence (Lee, 2025; López-Peláez et al., 2024).

This taxonomy matters because a single ethical framework applied uniformly across these four modalities risks category error: the ethical risks of continuous digital care monitoring are not the same as those of a PRM tool or an ADS benefits system. The case analyses in Section 7 apply the tri-lens matrix to three of these four modalities - PRM in child protection (Case 1), ADS in welfare administration (Case 2), and embedded digital-care technologies (Case 3). LLMs and conversational AI constitute a fourth modality with a distinctive ethical profile; they are not a primary case study here because their evidence base in high-acuity welfare settings remains too rapidly evolving to support the kind of systematic cross-case comparison the matrix is designed to enable.

## 5. From artificial to augmented intelligence: a spectrum, not a binary

Two conceptual labels organise the analysis: automated welfare and augmented intelligence (AugI). The distinction is more than semantic, but it is also not a simple binary. French and Shim (2025) argue that the shift from AI to AugI is a shift in design posture: AI is conceived as a potentially autonomous system; AugI is conceived as an explicitly assistive, cognitively amplifying orientation grounded in sociotechnical systems theory. Tangi et al. (2025) extend this logic to public administration, and Mortati and Freitas (2025) make the complementary move in service design.

The distinction is better understood as a spectrum between two ideal types rather than as a binary classification. At the automated-welfare pole, algorithmic systems take over consequential decisions and human practitioners become interpreters of model output - or are removed from the process entirely. At the augmented-intelligence pole, AI functions as a structured aid to human professional judgment: it surfaces information, flags patterns, reduces clerical burden, and makes its reasoning visible, while a named human decision-maker retains real authority. Most real systems sit between these poles, and the paper's analytical task is to diagnose where each sits and what conditions would move it toward augmentation.

<!-- PAGE 9 -->

The minimum conditions for a system to qualify as augmented intelligence in social work are: (i) a named human decision-maker with real authority and time to exercise professional judgment; (ii) AI outputs that enter human judgment as inputs, not defaults; (iii) legibility of AI reasoning to the practitioner; (iv) a meaningful override pathway; and (v) institutional structures that reward judgment over compliance. These conditions are necessary but not sufficient; whether they are met is the question the matrix diagnoses.

The augmented-intelligence posture is particularly apt for social work for four reasons. First, social work is constitutively relational (Ruch et al., 2010; Featherstone et al., 2014). Its core work - assessment, intervention, advocacy, accompaniment, safeguarding - cannot be reduced to pattern recognition. Relational practice, understood as a commitment to sustained human presence and the therapeutic use of relationship, is not incidental to social work's effectiveness; it is central to it. Where relational depth, moral judgment, and contextual meaning-making are doing the work, AI can contribute only as support.

Second, social work operates under legal and professional rules that assign responsibility to named human decision-makers. Augmented intelligence preserves this locus of accountability; automated welfare tends to diffuse it across software supply chains (NASW, 2021; NASW, ASWB, CSWE and CSWA, 2017). Third, the populations social work serves are disproportionately exposed to algorithmic misclassification and to the reputational and material consequences of being mis-scored. Augmented intelligence keeps a human in a position to interpret, challenge, and override automated outputs.

Fourth, the sociotechnical systems literature shows that when humans are placed at the end of an automated process - a passive reviewer of algorithmic output - they tend to rubber-stamp rather than audit (Green, 2022; Skitka et al., 1999; Cummings, 2004). Augmented intelligence therefore requires human at the centre, with AI as a structured aid to professional judgment not a 'human in the loop' who notionally approves algorithmic decisions without the time, authority, or information to contest them (French and Shim, 2025; Tangi et al., 2025).

## 6. Analytical framework: a tri-lens matrix

## 6.1 Why three ethical traditions, and why together

A recurring weakness of AI-ethics-in-social-work scholarship is that it invokes one moral tradition and then argues, often implicitly, as though that tradition exhausted the moral field. Utilitarian arguments dominate policy advocacy because they translate smoothly into metrics. Deontological arguments dominate legal and human-rights critique because rights discourse maps onto existing law. Virtue ethics, often least visible, is in fact closest to social work's professional self-understanding as a practice of character - wisdom, compassion, fidelity, courage, integrity (Banks, 2021; Reamer, 2023).

<!-- PAGE 10 -->

Each tradition asks a different question. Utilitarianism asks: does the system, on balance, increase welfare - and for whom? Deontology asks: does the system honour duties, rights, consent, procedural fairness, and human dignity irrespective of aggregate outcomes? Virtue ethics asks: what kind of professional, institutional, and civic character does the system cultivate or erode?

Relying on any one of these yields predictable blind spots. A purely utilitarian assessment of Robodebt could once have appeared favorable - recovered debt divided by system cost while silently absorbing distributed harm inflicted on claimants. A purely deontological assessment might resist any algorithmic mediation, including benign augmentations. A purely virtue-ethical assessment risks abstraction from institutional design. Using all three together yields a more robust diagnosis: an ethically defensible system must pass each lens, not only the one most convenient to its advocates.

## 6.2 Frontstage and backstage AI in welfare

Following Mortati and Freitas (2025), we distinguish frontstage and backstage AI. In welfare, frontstage AI includes chatbots for triage and information provision, AI-enabled care devices, digital service kiosks, and generative-AI copilots supporting caseworkers in client-facing encounters. Backstage AI includes risk scoring, fraud profiling, cross-agency data matching, eligibility automation, demand forecasting, and workload allocation. Ethical risks differ between the two: frontstage AI raises sharp questions about consent, deception, emotional harm, and substitution of relational care; backstage AI raises sharp questions about surveillance, procedural fairness, due process, and distributional injustice. Most real systems combine both, which is precisely why a matrix is needed.

## 6.3 Core social-work values and their disciplinary grounding

Social work is not a values-neutral profession. The NASW Code of Ethics (2021), the IFSW Global Social Work Statement of Ethical Principles (2018), and the Korean Association of Social Workers' Code of Ethics (KASW, 2023) share four constitutive commitments relevant to AI: privacy (including confidentiality and protection from surveillance), self-determination (client autonomy, voice, and meaningful choice), social justice (equal treatment, non-discrimination, redistribution toward the marginalized), and human dignity (the inviolable worth of each person and the prohibition on their instrumentalization).

Three additional disciplinary concepts from social work ground the framework and prevent it from floating free of the profession's core commitments. Relational practice holds that the therapeutic relationship between worker and client is not merely a vehicle for intervention but constitutive of care itself; AI systems that erode relational presence erode the substance of social work, not only its process (Ruch et al., 2010; Featherstone et al., 2014). Care ethics, following Tronto (1993) and Sevenhuijsen (1998), insists that care is a political and moral practice of attending to vulnerability, taking responsibility, and responding competently and with care - a practice that cannot be delegated to algorithmic systems without category error. Anti-oppressive

<!-- PAGE 11 -->

practice (AOP) requires that social work actively challenge structural inequalities and the exercise of power over marginalized groups; AI systems deployed without structural critique risk becoming new instruments of the oppression they are ostensibly designed to reduce (Dominelli, 2002; Webb, 2006).

The matrix yields 16 diagnostic questions (2 traditions × 2 arenas × 4 values), with the virtue-ethical lens operating disclosively at the level of case synthesis rather than through separate cells. Not every cell will be equally salient in every case; the analytical task in Section 7 is to identify which cells are most diagnostically decisive for each AI modality.

This presentation follows Banks's (2021) account of virtue ethics in social work as a tradition centrally concerned with character, practical wisdom (phronesis), and the moral quality of professional relationships - understood as a lens that discloses dimensions missed by rulebased and consequentialist frameworks, rather than as a parallel set of decision rules. See also MacIntyre (1981) for the foundational argument that virtue ethics addresses questions of character and institutional formation that neither utilitarian nor deontological traditions can adequately capture.

## 6.5 Derivation and justification of diagnostic questions

The diagnostic questions in Table 1 were derived through a three-step process. First, for each value-arena intersection, the paper identified the primary stakeholder relationship at risk: in frontstage contexts, between the AI system and the client; in backstage contexts, between the AI system and the institutional treatment of the client. Second, each ethical tradition was applied to that relationship to ask what it would require: utilitarianism asked about aggregate welfare effects and their distribution; deontology asked about rights, duties, consent, and procedural requirements; virtue ethics asked about the professional and institutional character the system cultivates. Third, the resulting questions were cross-checked against established frameworks the EU AI Act (European Union, 2024), the UNESCO Recommendation (2021), the NIST AI RMF (NIST, 2023), the NASW Standards for Technology (NASW et al., 2017), and the AI HLEG Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, and the OECD Principles on AI (OECD, 2019) to ensure they captured the primary concerns those authoritative documents identify for each domain.

Because the questions are interpretive, a different researcher might have derived alternative formulations. This is a constitutive feature of the framework's analytical character, not a hidden claim to uniqueness. The matrix is offered as a reusable starting point for comparative analysis, not as a closed classification.

## 7. Applying the matrix: three cases

## 7.1 Case 1: Predictive risk modelling in child welfare - the Allegheny Family Screening Tool

The Allegheny Family Screening Tool (AFST) generates risk scores to support call-screening decisions in child protective services (Vaithianathan et al., 2017; Allegheny County Department of Human Services, 2019). Independent evaluations report modest improvements in screening accuracy and some reductions in racial disparities at the screening stage (Goldhaber-Fiebert and Prince, 2019), while independent scholarship documents persistent concerns about institutional visibility bias - that the AFST tends to score families more dense in administrative records,

<!-- PAGE 13 -->

who are disproportionately poor and racialized, as higher-risk (Eubanks, 2018; De-Arteaga et al., 2020; Gillingham, 2019).

Utilitarian lens. The AFST operates primarily in the backstage (score generation) with indirect frontstage effects (screener decisions that follow). If the tool genuinely redirects screener attention toward children at higher risk, the aggregate welfare calculus is non-trivial. But aggregate outcome claims are substantially weakened by the under-identification of children whose families are less institutionally visible, by the documented workload costs of disputed scores, and by the absence of controlled experimental evidence showing downstream improvement in child safety outcomes. Efficiency at the screening stage is not equivalent to effectiveness for child welfare as a whole.

Deontological lens. Backstage concerns dominate. Data reuse for risk scoring often exceeds the purpose for which data were originally collected (GDPR Article 5; European Union, 2016). In the United States, the Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights (OSTP, 2022) articulates substantive rights at stake: notice, explanation, human alternatives, and protection from algorithmic discrimination. Where disability-related indicators serve as proxies for risk, the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990) becomes directly relevant. The right to 'socially intelligible' explanation - intelligible to affected clients and frontline workers, not only model developers - is grounded in Article 22 GDPR and Article 14 of the EU AI Act (European Union, 2024); in the United States, the equivalent principles are in the Blueprint. These rights are not absent in principle; they are consistently under-implemented in current PRM deployments.

Virtue-ethical lens. The institutional risk is that the screener's role contracts from judgment to score interpretation. If the organizational culture rewards score-compliance, practical wisdom atrophies. If, conversely, the score is framed as one input among many, with structured space for override and the time to exercise it, the tool may augment rather than replace discretion. DeArteaga et al. (2020) demonstrate empirically that human screeners add independent predictive value beyond the model - evidence that the human judgment the system is meant to support remains irreplaceable.

Social justice lens. The structural-bias concern in the AFST warrants separate analytical attention. Eubanks (2018) and De-Arteaga et al. (2020) demonstrate that the AFST's higher scores for poorer and racially marginalised families reflect their greater administrative visibility - a function of prior and current contact with public systems - rather than higher rates of child maltreatment per se. This means that the tool does not merely reflect inequality; it amplifies it by systematically directing investigative attention toward families already burdened by state contact. Any defensible deployment must therefore include regular disparate-impact audit that disaggregates false-positive rates by race, income, and household type, published externally and reviewed independently - not merely as a performance metric but as a condition of continued operation. The social-justice demand is not that the tool be race-blind but that its distributional consequences be transparent, contestable, and subject to democratic accountability.

<!-- PAGE 14 -->

Augmented-intelligence assessment. The AFST sits closer to the augmentation pole when screeners have time, authority, and institutional support to exercise override. It slides toward automation when workload pressure converts the score into a de facto decision. The critical governance requirement is not merely a nominal override pathway but an institutional culture visible in training, supervision, and performance management - that treats the score as one input, not an oracle.

## 7.2 Case 2: Algorithmic welfare administration - SyRI, Robodebt, and Korean parallels

The Dutch SyRI system combined data from multiple government sources to identify individuals or neighbourhoods at heightened risk of fraud. In 2020, the District Court of The Hague found SyRI incompatible with Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights because its opacity, disproportionality, and insufficient safeguards failed the necessary-in-a-democraticsociety test (District Court of The Hague, 2020; Appelman et al., 2021). Hong Seok-Han's (2023) constitutional analysis treats the judgment as a warning to any state - Korea included that pursues fraud-reduction via opaque risk profiling without commensurate rights protections.

Australia's Robodebt system automatically matched income data against welfare records and generated debts against claimants, shifting the burden of proof from the state to the individual. It produced widespread unlawful debt calculations, documented psychological harm, and was ultimately subject to a royal commission (Carney, 2023; Hong Seung-Heon and Hwang, 2024). Hong Seung-Heon and Hwang's (2024) analysis argues that AI-based welfare administration requires triple-loop human intervention - at design, operation, and redress - and should not be deployed in high-stakes domains without it.

Korea's AI-based crisis-household detection system cross-matches utility disconnections, healthinsurance arrears, pension non-payment, rental arrears, and other administrative signals to flag potentially at-risk households; field outreach follows (Park, 2025). Unlike SyRI and Robodebt, this system is oriented in its declared purpose toward inclusion - bringing people into contact with welfare - rather than toward exclusion. Yet the underlying architecture is structurally similar: cross-agency data matching on a vulnerable population, opacity of scoring logic, and consequential follow-on decisions. The system's inclusion-oriented purpose does not by itself resolve the deontological concerns about purpose limitation, data minimisation, and meaningful consent; nor does it resolve the virtue-ethical concern that practitioners may come to understand welfare need primarily through administrative signal rather than through relational encounter.

Utilitarian lens. SyRI and Robodebt fail decisively on this lens: fraud-recovery gains were offset by legal, reputational, psychological, and fiscal harms that were borne overwhelmingly by the least powerful. The Korean inclusion-oriented system is more plausibly defensible on its declared purpose - it is designed to enrol rather than to exclude - but a verdict on its aggregate welfare impact must remain provisional: no publicly reported data on false-positive rates, followup outcomes for flagged households, or substantive enrolment effects are available for independent evaluation. Plausible intent is not measured outcome. A positive utilitarian verdict

<!-- PAGE 15 -->

for the Korean system would require the Ministry of Health and Welfare to publish, with independent verification: (a) annual false-positive rates for field outreach, disaggregated by household type and signal combination; (b) the proportion of flagged households successfully enrolled in welfare services, compared against a counterfactual or comparator group; (c) longitudinal outcome data on the welfare trajectories of enrolled households; and (d) an assessment of whether the system generates genuinely new outreach - reaching previously uncounted households - or primarily digitises existing referral channels. Until such data are publicly available and independently verified, the utilitarian verdict must remain explicitly open.

Deontological lens. SyRI failed on privacy, necessity, proportionality, and transparency. Robodebt failed on due process and the burden of proof. The Korean system, though better intentioned, must still satisfy purpose limitation and data-minimisation tests under the Personal Information Protection Act (Republic of Korea, 2011, as amended 2023) and should publish meaningful information about its scoring logic, the frequency of false positives in field outreach, and its outcomes. Transparent disclosure is not only good practice; it is, on the analysis of the SyRI judgment, a constitutional requirement.

Augmented-intelligence assessment. SyRI and Robodebt sit at the automated-welfare pole: they made or effectively compelled consequential decisions - fraud classification, debt imposition - without meaningful human review, client-legible explanation, or independent oversight. The Korean crisis-detection system is structurally more ambiguous. Where field outreach by a named social worker follows AI flagging, and where the practitioner retains real authority to interpret, contextualise, and decline to act on the signal, the system could operate closer to the augmentation pole. The governance gap is institutional: the system has not yet published the oversight infrastructure - override rates, false-positive disaggregation by signal type and household category, independent audit findings - that would confirm augmentation in practice rather than in design intention alone. Absent such transparency, the Ministry's own characterisation of the system as welfare-oriented cannot be independently verified, and the social work profession should treat the distinction between inclusion-oriented and exclusionoriented surveillance as requiring ongoing institutional evidence, not assumed from declared purpose.

Virtue-ethical lens. Systems of secret suspicion cultivate institutional distrust; systems of anonymous detection, even when benevolent, can erode the virtue of encounter - the face-toface relational attentiveness that is constitutive of social work's anti-oppressive commitments (Dominelli, 2002). The Korean case shows that technological architecture and institutional purpose can diverge: a system oriented toward inclusion can still embed surveillance logics, and the profession must actively guard against their drift toward exclusionary use.

## 7.3 Case 3: Digital-care technologies in community and long-term care

Korea - and increasingly OECD peers - has invested heavily in digital-care technologies: IoT sensors that monitor activity and falls, AI companion devices, telecare platforms, and robotic

<!-- PAGE 16 -->

aids (Lee, 2025; López-Peláez et al., 2024; Segal, Lens, and Gur, 2025). Lee's (2025) qualitative study of ten frontline Korean social workers providing IoT- and AI-enabled care to older adults identifies four themes: the field application and impact of digital-care services; the expansion of social-worker roles and workload burden; the limits of user acceptability and the ethical considerations attached to digital-care technologies; and the institutional conditions under which they operate. These findings echo international evidence that digital-care technologies can extend reach but also reconfigure care work in ways that transfer emotional labour and ethical responsibility onto frontline practitioners (Strengers and Kennedy, 2020).

Utilitarian lens. Net welfare gains are plausible: continuous monitoring can detect falls, medication non-adherence, and acute decline more quickly than episodic human contact. But net gain depends critically on whether the technology augments or replaces human visits. Where it replaces them, loneliness and relational poverty worsen - welfare harms that are less visible to administrative measurement precisely because they are relational and subjective rather than clinical and recordable. Lee's (2025) frontline evidence shows that Korean practitioners experience digital-care deployment as simultaneously extending reach and increasing workload burden, creating a risk that technology absorbs the administrative functions of care while practitioners absorb its relational demands without corresponding resources. Aggregate welfare assessment must therefore account for the welfare of workers as well as clients, and for relational quality as well as clinical safety indicators.

Virtue-ethical lens. The deepest concern here is cultural and constitutive: digital-care technologies can subtly redefine what counts as care. When 'being visited' becomes 'being monitored,' and when human attention is mediated through sensors rather than expressed through presence, the moral texture of ageing and disability changes. Social work's care-ethical and virtue-ethical commitments point to a both-and, not either-or, posture: digital care is ethically defensible only when it sits inside a sustained human relational architecture and does not serve as cover for the withdrawal of that architecture.

Deontological lens. Consent for continuous in-home monitoring is a genuinely hard problem. Cognitive decline, household power asymmetries, and the embeddedness of always-on devices complicate any model of one-off informed consent. Meaningful consent in this setting must be ongoing, revocable, and supported by accessible information in plain language and accessible formats.

Augmented-intelligence assessment. Digital-care technologies occupy a constitutively ambiguous position on the augmentation-automation spectrum: the same IoT monitoring infrastructure can either supplement human presence - extending relational reach and enabling outreach to people who would otherwise go unseen - or substitute for it, replacing the visit with the alert and the social worker with the sensor network. Lee's (2025) evidence from Korean frontline practitioners shows that the same technology can do both depending on organisational context, management decisions, and resource allocation. The institutional requirement for

<!-- PAGE 17 -->

augmentation is therefore not a design specification but a governance commitment: organisations must treat digital-care technology as additional infrastructure, resourced alongside relational practice, not as a staffing substitute. This requires, at minimum, that deployment be accompanied by regular relational review visits that the technology itself cannot perform, and that decisions to reduce in-person contact be made by named social workers with documented justification - not as an automatic consequence of digital-monitoring coverage.

First, cost-effectiveness is not welfare-effectiveness. Across all three cases, administrative metrics diverge from social-work outcomes. A system can reduce staff workload and unit cost while shifting the costs of error, opacity, and exclusion onto clients. This is not an objection to efficiency; it is an objection to the category confusion between administrative efficiency and the

proper ends of welfare (Myrdal, 1960; van Toorn et al., 2024). This conclusion is grounded in documented evidence: the Robodebt royal commission explicitly found that cost-recovery rationales masked large-scale legal harm; the AFST literature shows screener efficiency gains that do not clearly translate into child safety outcomes.

Second, reach is not substantive reach. Digital systems can formally extend access while practically excluding those with unstable connectivity, low digital literacy, disability-related access barriers, language differences, or fear of surveillance (López-Peláez et al., 2024; Alexopoulou, 2025). This conclusion is grounded in documented evidence: Lee's (2025) qualitative data show that Korean digital-care technologies expand formal service coverage while revealing significant user resistance and access barriers among older adults.

Third, bias is structural, not technical. Across cases, bias emerges not from careless coding but from the fact that data themselves encode unequal institutional histories (Eubanks, 2018; Gillingham, 2019). Bias audits and disparate-impact assessments are necessary but not sufficient; they must be accompanied by broader forms of accountability - community review, publication of system logic, and political contestation - that the matrix demands. This conclusion is grounded in documented evidence: De-Arteaga et al. (2020) demonstrate that the AFST's bias is structural (reflecting who is in administrative systems) rather than an artefact of modelling choices alone.

## 9. Integrated implications: privacy, self-determination, social justice, dignity, and the abuse of AI

Normative conclusion. Based on the analysis, AI is ethically defensible in social work only when: it augments practitioner judgment without displacing human relational authority; it is built for substantive rather than merely formal reach; it is embedded in contestable, auditable, publicly accountable institutions; and the institutional conditions for genuine human override - time, authority, information, and culture - are verifiably present.

## 9.1 Privacy and self-determination

Social work's traditional conception of privacy was dyadic - a confidentiality relation between worker and client. AI in welfare is almost always multi-party and re-purposing: data collected for one reason (utility billing, health insurance, tax administration) are used for another (risk profiling). The relevant protections now include purpose limitation, data minimisation, necessity, proportionality, and revocability (European Union, 2016; Republic of Korea, 2011, as amended 2023).

Self-determination requires both frontstage and backstage forms of protection. In frontstage AI, self-determination demands legibility, human alternatives, and a meaningful right to opt out. In backstage AI, it requires procedural rights: notice that an automated decision is being used, the right to explanation, the right to contest, and the right to meaningful human review of

<!-- PAGE 19 -->

consequential outputs (OSTP, 2022; European Union, 2024; Carney, 2023). These rights are specifically under-implemented in current welfare AI systems.

## 9.2 The abuse of AI in social welfare

AI abuse in social welfare is a first-order ethical issue, not a peripheral concern. The empirical record shows five recurring abuse patterns, each documented in the cases above.

Suspicion as default. Fraud-detection systems (SyRI, Robodebt) invert the presumption of eligibility, placing the burden of proof on claimants in ways that amplify power asymmetries already present in welfare administration.

Surveillance creep. Data collected for one service flow into another without renewed consent or public deliberation (Hong Seok-Han, 2023).

Deskilling and deference. Systems meant to augment practitioner judgment can, under workload pressure, become oracles whose outputs are rubber-stamped - an abuse of the augmentedintelligence claim (Green, 2022; Cummings, 2004).

Naming these patterns matters. A field that only documents the potential of AI, or that treats abuse as off-path deviation, will not develop the institutional capacity needed to resist it.

Commercial substitution. When care labour - relational visiting, accompaniment, human presence - is replaced by digital monitoring and automated triage in the name of reach or efficiency, providers effectively externalise emotional labour onto clients and their informal networks while claiming scale as a welfare gain. This dynamic is documented in the digital-care literature (Strengers and Kennedy, 2020; Lee, 2025) and is ethically distinct from using digital tools to complement relational care. The distinction matters for social work: the question is not whether technology is present but whether relational care has been maintained alongside it. Opacity as policy. Claims that model logic is proprietary, or that explanation would enable gaming, are used to block legitimate accountability (Bouderhem, 2024; Pham, 2025).

## 10. Toward augmented welfare: a framework for responsible adoption

The matrix and case analysis yield a set of institutional conditions under which AI can be ethically defended as augmented intelligence in social welfare. These conditions are offered not as a checklist but as the architectural requirements of any defensible deployment, derived from the documented evidence of system failures and the normative demands of social work's constitutive values.

(i) Human authority at the centre, not merely in the loop. A named human decision-maker, with real authority and time to exercise professional judgment, must hold consequential decisions. Model outputs must enter human judgment as inputs, not defaults. This requires not only a nominal override pathway but an institutional culture - visible in training, supervision, and performance management - that treats the score as one input among many.

<!-- PAGE 20 -->

(ii) Legibility, explanation, contestation, and human review as rights, not courtesies. Affected clients must be told an AI system is being used; told, in plain language, what it considers; told how to contest; and told how to access meaningful human review (OSTP, 2022; European Union, 2024).

(iii) Purpose limitation and data minimisation, strictly enforced. Data collected for one welfare purpose must not flow to surveillance, profiling, or punitive use without fresh, specific, revocable consent or lawful basis (European Union, 2016; Republic of Korea, 2011).

(iv) Substantive digital inclusion. Every deployed system must be paired with non-digital alternatives, language and accessibility support, literacy support, and community mediation. Without these, 'reach' is a metric, not a social-work value.

- (v) Structural bias audits and independent oversight. Systems with consequential welfare impact must be subject to pre-deployment impact assessment, disparate-impact audit, and ongoing independent oversight with public reporting (EU AI Act, European Union, 2024; NIST, 2023; UNESCO, 2021). The OECD Principles on AI (OECD, 2019) - particularly Principle 1.4 on robustness, security, and safety, and Principle 1.3 on transparency and explainability - provide additional cross-jurisdictional scaffolding for these audit requirements.

(vii) Professional training in algorithmic literacy and critical practice. Social workers must be trained not only to use AI systems but to scrutinise and, where necessary, to resist them. Algorithmic literacy is emerging as a core professional competence (Segal, Lens, and Gur, 2025; Victor et al., 2024), and must be integrated with the profession's existing commitments to antioppressive practice and relational accountability.

- (vi) Categorical prohibitions on sole-source algorithmic decisions in the highest-stakes domains. Sole-source algorithmic decisions to remove children from families, terminate welfare benefits, determine crisis-response, or generate fraud debts should be prohibited. The burden must lie on advocates of such uses to justify them, not on opponents to defeat them.

(viii) Institutional honesty about cost-effectiveness. Cost-effectiveness claims must be evaluated through social-work outcomes - inclusion, trust, rights protection, outcome equity - not only administrative metrics.

Together, these conditions describe what defensible 'augmented welfare' would look like. Without them, AI in welfare is not augmented intelligence; it is automated welfare dressed in the language of augmentation. Two of these conditions - (vi) and (viii) - extend beyond what can be validated in the current conceptual study and require empirical validation through implementation research.

## 11. Conclusion

<!-- PAGE 21 -->

Artificial intelligence is already reshaping social welfare. The question has never been whether social work will encounter AI; it is on what terms. This paper has argued that the most defensible posture is neither uncritical adoption nor blanket refusal, but a principled framework for AI as augmented intelligence - provided that specific, verifiable institutional conditions are met, and that the precautionary case for non-deployment is taken seriously in high-stakes settings where those conditions cannot be satisfied.

Three conclusions follow, differentiated by their evidential basis. First, based on documented case evidence, AI can improve administrative efficiency and sometimes extend formal reach, but these gains are context-specific, often modest in domains of highest stakes, and fragile without active digital-inclusion measures. Second, derived from the tri-lens framework and its application to the cases, AI-augmented welfare is not inherently inclusive; it becomes inclusive only when legibility, contestation, human review, substantive reach, and purpose limitation are designed in from the outset. Third, as a normative conclusion whose validation requires further empirical work, the profession's ethical future depends on whether it insists that technology remain accountable to social work's values - privacy, self-determination, social justice, and human dignity - rather than adapting itself to technocratic logics of throughput and costreduction.

The next step is empirical: to use the matrix to study specific deployments in implementation context - including Korea's AI crisis-household detection system, the next generation of digitalcare technologies, emerging generative-AI copilots for caseworkers, and LLM-based mental health applications - and to ask, case by case, whether they are, in fact, augmenting care or automating welfare. The matrix is explicitly designed to be reused, refined, and contested.

The contribution this paper offers is a sharp reframing (augmentation versus automation, understood as a spectrum), a usable analytical apparatus (the tri-lens matrix with explicit diagnostic questions and derivation), integration of Korean and European evidence into one comparative lens, and a differentiated AI modality taxonomy. Its limits are equally clear: conceptual-comparative, not experimental; cross-jurisdictional, not ethnographic; frameworkbuilding, not prescriptive.

AI should support human judgment, not replace it; extend substantive access, not automate exclusion; reduce administrative burden, not moral responsibility. The proper goal is not merely smarter welfare administration, but welfare that remains just, transparent, participatory, and humane - and that sustains the relational encounter between practitioner and person that is the irreducible core of the social work profession.
