## 8.1  What Is Prompt Engineering?

A prompt refers to an 'input' or 'directive' that a user presents to an AI model, especially a large language model (LLM), to achieve the desired result (OpenAI API, 2025). Prompts are conditional inputs that induce LLMs to perform specific tasks or generate texts, codes, images, and videos. Prompts serve as a key medium for controlling the output of a model.

Think of it as an 'order' or 'request' given to an AI. For example, if the order, an  'iced Americano,  please'  at  a  café  is  a  prompt,  then  the Americano  made  by the  barista  (AI)  is  the  output.  In  addition  to  containing  only  questions,  prompts also include persona, context, desired formats, and specific instructions to help the AI  create  better  answers.  If  you  say  'You  are  a  child  counseling  expert  in  social work.  Please  explain  'self-   esteem'  to  an  8-   year-  old.'  The  prompt  corresponds  to the assignment of roles and instructions, and 'Summarize the following case history, but please write a summary with a focus on the positive changes in the client.' This kind of prompt provides context or desired perspectives (An, 2023).

Prompt  engineering is  the  art  and  science  of  designing  inputs  (questions  or instructions)  for  Generative  AI  models  to  produce  optimal  outputs.  It  refers  to the  process  of  systematically  and  strategically  designing  and  improving  prompts (instructions)  to  obtain  the  optimal  results  intended  by  the  user  from  the AI  ( An, 2023). This is not a modification of the AI model's code itself, but a utilization technology that brings out the full potential of AI by improving the way we com -municate to AI. It is a field that studies 'how best' to communicate with AI to get the most desired output (OpenAI API, 2025).

Prompt engineering is where the 'rubber meets the road,' because it transforms the  social  worker  from  a  passive  observer  of  technology  into  an  active,  skilled pilot. AI  is  a  tool  of  infinite potential,  but  it  requires  human  direction  to  align with  the  dignity  and  worth  of  the  person.  Therefore,  we  compare  prompting  to 'supervision.' When you consult a supervisor, the quality of their advice depends on how clearly you present the case, the context, and your specific question. As discussed earlier, AI systems can be prone to algorithmic bias . Skillful prompting is a frontline intervention where social workers can instruct the system to check its own biases and focus on equity.

## 8.2  Client-  Centered Prompt Design

Suppose  that  you  work  with  a  14year-  old  boy  in  foster  care,  telling  you  about persistent  depression,  sleep  disruption,  and  reduced  motivation  following  family separation.  He  continues  attending  school  and  has  some  friends.  Social  worker A prompts to summarize mental health risks. Social worker B prompts to provide a  summary  of  resilience  and  protective  factors.  Despite  that  it  is  the  same  case, LLMs  will  provide  completely  different  assessments.  Which  of  these  interpret -ations would be more valid?

Client-  tailored  prompt  design adapts  LLMs'  responses  to  the  unique  needs, identities,  and  contexts  of  individuals.  This  approach  reflects core  social  work values: meeting  clients where  they are, honoring  their cultural and emotional realities, and tailoring interventions to their lived experiences. When prompts are individualized,  AI-  generated  responses  become  more  accurate,  ethically  aligned, and therapeutically meaningful.

## 8.2.1  Prompts Tailored to Client Demographics

We can use contextual factors (e.g., age, education, literacy level, or socioeconomic status)  to  shape  how  an  LLM  communicates.  Adjusting  a  prompt  in  these  ways helps  ensure  the  content  is  developmentally  appropriate,  accessible,  and  relevant to each client's circumstances.

To illustrate, prompts can incorporate age-  specific developmental theory: 'Explain the idea of emotional boundaries as if speaking to a 10year-  old in the Concrete Operational Stage.' Similarly, for younger children, using stories or concrete examples (e.g., 'Tell a short fairy tale style story that illustrates emo -tional  boundaries  using  two  clear  behaviors')  can  strengthen  comprehension  and support behavioral change.

Prompts can also reduce information barriers by replacing jargon with plain lan -guage, structuring information into short lists or tables, and minimizing complex sentence  structures.  For  older  adults  or  clients  with  lower  literacy,  prompts  may instruct  AI  to  simplify  text  or  create  visually  clear  summaries.  These  strategies improve readability and ensure vulnerable groups can access essential information (Djeffal, 2025).

When  recommending  services  for  clients  with  limited  financial  resources, prompts  might  specify:  'Provide  only  free  or  lowcost  community  counseling resources.'  This  ensures  that  recommendations  remain  practical  and  actionable within the client's financial constraints.

## 8.2.2  Privacy-  Conscious  Prompting

Privacy-  conscious  prompting  explicitly  instructs  AI  to  follow  confidentiality standards  (such  as  Health  Insurance  Portability  and Accountability Act  [HIPAA] and  core  social  work  ethics)  to  protect  sensitive  client  information.  This  helps reduce risks related to reidentification or disclosure of personally identifiable information (PII) (Djeffal, 2025).

For instance, prompts can direct the  AI to anonymize case material: 'Summarize the  following  case  notes,  replacing  all  names  and  contact  information  with  neu -tral  placeholders  such  as  'Mr.  A'  or  'anonymous.'  Prompts  can  also  require  the model to generalize sensitive health or identity information to prevent accidental disclosure. Reducing data granularity in this way strengthens client confidentiality and aligns AI-  supported work with professional ethical standards.

## 8.2.3  Prompt Strategies to Minimize Bias

Because LLMs  may  reproduce  social biases embedded  in their training prompt  strategies  that  minimize  bias  are  essential  for  equitable  practice.  These prompts help ensure AI avoids stereotyping or discriminatory assumptions based on gender, race, disability, or socioeconomic status (An, 2023; Khan, 2024).

## 8.3  Ethical Guardrails in Prompting for Evidence-  Based and Effective Practice

LLMs can extract key information (e.g., presenting problems, goals, and progress) from large case files and convert it into structured summaries. This reduces the time social workers must spend preparing reports or supervision materials, allowing for more client-  focused work (Djeffal, 2025).

Prompts  can  also  specify  frameworks  to  maintain  consistency  across  practitioners (e.g.,  'Summarize  using  a  short-   term  intervention  model.').  Social  workers  may also prompt AI to gather up-  to-  date research or policy information (e.g., summar izing evidence on Opioid Use Disorder) and present it in a presentationready form. This  expands  the  practitioners'  access  to  knowledge  and  strengthens  evidencebased intervention planning (An, 2023).

Incorporating explicit instructions on anonymity, confidentiality, and cultural humility creates an 'ethical fence' within the prompt  itself.  This ensures  AIgenerated  content  respects  client  rights,  reduces  risk  of  harm,  and  supports  cul -turally competent, anti-  oppressive practice. While the technical skills required for strong prompting are important, the ethical application of these skills is paramount.

Khan (2024) identifies three major domains where AI can enhance social work practice: (1) streamlining documentation and workflows, (2) supporting evidencebased  decision-   making,  and  (3)  promoting  ethical  AI  use  by  minimizing  bias. Let's  revisit  the  risks  we  identified in  Chapter  5,  specifically bias,  privacy,  and the  erosion  of  judgment,  and  explore  how  to  manage  them  actively  through  our prompting strategies.

First and foremost is the No PII Rule . When using public Generative AI tools, we  must  maintain  absolute  strictness  regarding  data  privacy.  We  should  never enter PII, such as client names, dates of birth, social security numbers, or specific addresses, into these systems. Instead, we use pseudonyms or generic descriptors, such as referring to a 'Client A' or 'a 14-   year-  old student living in a rural county.' This ensures we can leverage the tool's reasoning capabilities without comprom -ising the client confidentiality that is the bedrock of our profession.

Second,  we  must  be  vigilant  against Hallucinations .  In  the  context  of  AI,  a hallucination occurs when a model generates incorrect or nonsensical information but presents it with high eloquence and confidence (AI21  Labs,  2025).  Because AI predicts the next likely word rather than retrieving verified facts, it can convincingly invent laws, academic citations, or community resources that do not exist. Therefore, our strategy must always include rigorous fact-  checking. It is the social worker's  responsibility  to  verify  every  statute,  agency  information,  or  reference provided by the AI before using it in practice.

Finally,  we  can  use  prompting  as  a  tool  for Bias  Mitigation .  AI  models  are trained using vast amounts  of internet data, which  inevitably contain societal biases. However, we can proactively instruct the AI to counteract these tendencies. By explicitly  commanding  the AI  to  apply  an  equity  lens,  we  can  generate  more inclusive content. Rather than simply asking for a policy review, a strong prompt would ask the AI to 'Review this policy draft and identify any language that might inadvertently  stigmatize  individuals  with  substance  use  disorders.'  By  doing  so, we turn the AI into a 'partner' in our pursuit of anti-  oppressive practice.

## 8.4  The Anatomy of a Power Prompt: The P.I.C.O. Framework

If you have ever stared at a blinking cursor in a text box, unsure of how to begin, you are not alone. Many social workers feel pressure to ask the 'perfect' question.

However, interacting with Generative AI is not about memorizing computer code; it is about the user communicating with their intent.

We claimed social workers are not passive users, but co-  creators of technology. To co-  create effectively, we must move beyond simple questions or prompts (e.g., 'Write a discharge summary') and toward engineered prompts .

Think  of  a  LLM  as  a  highly  capable,  encyclopedic,  but  literalminded  intern. If  you  give  vague  instructions,  you  may  or  may  not  receive  the  correct  output, depending on the intern's interpretation of the instructions. If you also -vide  context,  constraints,  and  clear  goals,  you  are  much  more  likely  to  receive a  product that accurately supports your practice (Harvard University Information Technology, 2023).

To help you structure your prompts effectively, we introduce the P.I.C.O. framework. This acronym ensures you provide the necessary ingredients, or details, for the AI to understand not just what you want, but how you need it delivered to serve the common good.

## 8.4.1  P: Persona (Who Is the AI?)

First,  tell  the  AI  who  it  is  supposed  to  be.  AI  models  are  trained  on  the  entire internet,  from  medical  journals  to  Reddit  threads.  By  assigning  a Persona ,  you narrow the AI's focus to a specific professional lens (MIT Teaching and Learning Technologies, 2025).

If  you  ask  for  'housing  advice,'  the AI  might  give  you  real  estate  investment tips.  If  you  assign  the  persona,  'Act  as  a  housing  case  manager  for  lowincome families,'  the  AI  shifts  its  tone  to  be  empathetic,  resourceoriented,  and  focused on stability.

Try this : 'Act as a Clinical Social Worker,' 'Act as a Grant Writer,' or 'Act as a Policy Analyst focusing on racial equity.'

## 8.4.2  I: Instruction (What Do You Need?)

This is the specific task you want the AI to perform. Use active, clear verbs. Avoid politeness  markers  (like  'please'  and  'thank  you')  as  they  clutter  the  instruc -tion: the AI does not have feelings, and clarity is king.

Use action verbs such as: 'Draft, Summarize, Translate, Brainstorm, Critique, Simplify.' For complex requests, instruct the  AI to 'think stepby-  step.'  This  forces the  model  to  break  down  its  reasoning,  often  reducing  errors  or  'hallucinations' (Wang et al., 2024).

## 8.4.3  C: Context and Evidence (What Does the AI Need to Know?)

This  is  the  most  critical  section  for  ethical  practice.  You  must  provide  the  back -ground information necessary for the AI to generate a relevant response. However, this  is  also  where data  privacy risks  are  highest.  Never  input  PII.  Do  not  enter client names, dates of birth, social security numbers, or specific addresses.

## 8.5  Iterative Refinement: The 'Chain of Thought'

In  AI, gradient descent is  a  mathematical method that helps a model learn by grad -ually correcting its mistakes (Coursera Staff, 2026). Although social workers don't need  the  calculus  behind  it,  understanding  the  concept  can  dramatically  improve how we craft prompts for practice, assessment, and documentation.

Think of gradient descent as a process of iterative improvement, a careful, stepby-  step  adjustment  toward  a  more  accurate  or  effective  result.  In  many  ways,  it resembles  how  social  workers  approach reflective practice :  we  act,  evaluate  the outcome, make small adjustments, and try again until we reach our goal.

AI models do not magically know the right answer. They learn by: (1) making an initial guess, (2) measuring how far off that guess was, (3) adjusting slightly to reduce  the  error,  and  (4)  repeating  this  process  thousands,  or  millions,  of  times. Gradient descent is the process of learning through repeated small corrections. If the model makes a mistake, it doesn't dramatically overhaul everything: it makes a tiny adjustment in the right direction. Over time, these micro-  corrections produce powerful learning.

The key takeaway is that effective prompting is rarely a 'one-  and-  done' event. It  is  a  'conversation.'  Just  as  you  would  not  expect  a  client  to  fully  articulate their  deepest  needs  at  the  beginning  of  an  intake  session,  you  should  not  expect

To understand this, we must first distinguish between Zero-  Shot and Few-  Shot prompting (Codecademy Team, 2024). Imagine asking an MSW intern to 'Write a case note' without giving them any instructions or templates. This is Zero-  Shot prompting :  asking  the  system  to  perform  a  task  with  no  examples.  While  AI  is capable of this, the results are often generic.

In contrast, Few-  Shot  prompting involves providing the AI with examples of what  good  work  looks  like  before  making  your  request.  By  showing  the  model a  sample  of  a  wellwritten,  strengths-  based  assessment,  you  guide  it  toward  the specific tone  and  structure  you  require.  Once  you  have  established  your  initial prompt, you enter the Iterative Cycle . Think of this as a 'loop' consisting of four stages: Draft, Assess, Refine, and Finalize (Bakst, 2025).

The  cycle  begins  with  the Draft ,  where  you  submit  your  initial  inquiry  using the P.I.C.O. framework. Once the AI generates a response, you move to the Assess phase. Here, you must review the output with a critical, social work lens. Does the text sound robotic or overly clinical? Is the language culturally competent, or does it rely on stereotypes?

Based on this assessment, you move to the Refine stage. This  is  where  magic happens.  You  do  not  need  to  rewrite  the  text  yourself;  instead,  you  can  instruct the AI to fix its own mistakes. You might say, 'Critique your previous answer for potential racial bias and rewrite it,' or 'Expand on the third point and make it more actionable.'  This  technique  often  triggers  a  ' Chain  of  Thought ,'  where  the  AI breaks down its reasoning, leading to more logical and nuanced answers.

Finally,  we  reach  the Finalize stage.  This  is  where  the  human  social  worker makes  the  final edit.  This  step  mirrors  the  principles  of Human-  Centered AI , ensuring that technology serves only to augment our capabilities, never to replace our professional judgment.

## 8.6.1  Scenario 1: The Privacy Breach

You are a hospital social worker needing to summarize a patient's situation for a transfer to a rehabilitation facility.

- Weak: 'I have a patient named Robert Smith, DOB 04/  12/  1955, who had a stroke. He lives at 445 Elm St. His daughter, Sarah, is worried he can't walk. Write a transfer summary for him.'
- Problems : Major Privacy Violation! This prompt includes PII (Name, DOB, Address, Relative's Name). Inputting this into a public AI model violates HIPAA and client confidentiality.

## 8.6.2  Correction

Persona : Act as a Medical Social Worker.

Instruction : Draft a discharge transfer summary.

- Context: The  patient  is  a  69year-  old  male  recovering  from  a  stroke  (CV A).  He  has mobility  limitations  (difficulty  walking).  The  family  support  system  (daughter) expresses  anxiety  regarding  his  mobility  safety  at  home.  No  names  or  addresses  used.

Output: Use professional medical terminology but keep the tone collaborative.

## 8.6.3 Scenario  2:  The  'DeficitBased'  Trap

You are a school social worker writing a proposal for a new mentorship program for students who are frequently suspended.

- Weak: 'Write a proposal for a mentorship program for at-  risk troubled teens in the inner city who have behavioral problems and come from broken homes. Explain why they are likely to fail without us.'
- Problems: Bias  and  Stigmatizing Language. This prompt relies on deficit-  based language ('troubled,' 'broken homes,' 'likely to fail'). It invites AI to generate stereotypes rather than evidence-  based insights. It lacks equity lenses.

## 8.6.4  Correction

Persona: Act as a Youth Development Specialist and Grant Writer.

Instruction: Draft a program proposal for a high school mentorship initiative.

- Context: The  program  serves  students  in  an  urban  setting  who  have  experienced exclusionary  discipline  (suspensions).  Focus  on  systemic  barriers  they  face. Highlight their resilience and potential for leadership.
- Output: Use  strengths-   based  language.  Focus  on  'opportunity  gaps'  rather  than 'at-  risk'  behaviors.

## 8.6.5  Scenario 3: The Hallucination Hazard

You are researching local housing laws to help a tenant who faces eviction.

- Weak: 'List  the  exact  sections  of  the  Tenant  Rights  Code  for  my  state  that  prevent eviction for late fees and provide the phone number for the local legal aid office.'
- Problem: High  Risk  of  Hallucination.  AI  models  act  as  prediction  engines,  not search engines. They often invent legal codes and fake phone numbers that look real. Relying on this for legal advocacy is dangerous.

## 8.6.6  Correction

Persona: Act as a Housing Advocate.

- Instruction: Summarize general concepts regarding tenant protections against eviction due to minor lease violations.
- Context: Do not invent legal codes. Explicitly state if you are unsure of specific local statutes. Suggest keywords I should use to search the official state court website.

Output: Bullet points with a disclaimer that this is information, not legal advice.

## 8.6.7  Scenario 4: Abdicating Professional Judgment

A  client  has  just  disclosed  something  ambiguous  that  might  require  a  report  to Child Protective Services (CPS), but you are unsure.

- Weak: 'My  client  said  she  left  her  5year-  old  alone  for  20  minutes  to  run  to  the store. Is this child abuse? Should I call CPS? Tell me what to do.'
- Problem: Abdicating Responsibility. AI cannot make ethical or legal determinations for you. It does not know your specific jurisdiction's laws or the nuance of the situ ation.  Asking it 'what to do' replaces your professional license with an algorithm.

## 8.6.8  Correction

Persona: Act as a Clinical Supervisor simulating a consultation.

Introduction : Help  me  explore  the  decision-   making  criteria  for  mandated  reporting.

Context: A 5-  year-  old was left unsupervised for 20 minutes. No injuries occurred.

- Output: List  the  factors  I  should  consider  (e.g.,  state  laws  on  age  of  supervision, maturity of child, immediate danger). Do not give a final verdict. End with a reminder to consult my live supervisor.

## 8.7  Prompt Engineering in Social Work Research

Research  is  the  compass  that  guides  our  practice.  However,  many  social  work students find the research process, specifically the volume of reading and the complexity of data analysis, daunting. Let's explore how to apply Prompt Engineering to the research processes.

Think  of  AI  not  as  an  'author'  that  writes  your  paper,  but  as  your  tireless Research Assistant (RA). This RA can summarize 50 articles in minutes, suggest coding categories for qualitative interviews, or help you translate dense statistical jargon into plain English. To do this effectively, let's adapt our P.I.C.O. framework.

## 8.7.1  The Literature Review (Synthesizing Knowledge)

One of the most powerful applications of AI is its ability to digest vast amounts of text.  However, we must be careful. If you ask for a 'list of sources,' the AI may hallucinate non-  existent papers. Instead, use AI to analyze text you already have or to summarize themes.

Weak Prompt: 'Find me quotes about housing first.'

Strong P.I.C.O. Prompt:

Persona: Act as a Social Work Researcher specializing in homelessness policy.

- Instruction: Summarize  the  key  arguments for and against the  'Housing  First' model found in the text below.
- Context: [Paste  the  abstract  and  conclusion  of  six  academic  articles  you  have found].
- Output: Create a comparison table with columns for 'Authors,' 'Key Argument,' and 'Implications for Policy.'

## 8.7.2 Qualitative  Data  Analysis  (The  Coding  Assistant)

Qualitative research involves reading transcripts to find patterns (themes). AI can be  a  'second  coder'  to  help  check  your  bias  or  suggest  themes  you  might  have missed.

- Ethical Checkpoint: You typically cannot upload full client transcripts to a public AI model due to confidentiality (i.e., HIPAA). However, you can use it to generate 'codes' based on de-  identified excerpts or to define your codebook.

The 'Codebook Generator' Prompt:

- Persona: Act as a Qualitative Researcher using Grounded Theory.
- Instruction: Review these three anonymous interview excerpts and suggest five initial coding categories.
- Context: [Paste three de-  identified paragraphs about caregiver burnout].
- Output: Present the codes with a definition for each and a specific quote from the text that supports it.

In  research,  integrity  is  everything.  AI  tools  are  language  models,  not  truth models. Never copy-  paste a Literature Review from AI: it will likely miss seminal, classic works or invent citations. Always verify specific data points (e.g., '66% of youth...') with the original source. ## 8.8  The 'Human in the Loop' Is You

As you can see from these exercises, the difference between a harmful output and a helpful one often lies entirely in the prompt. Technology is not neutral: it mirrors the intent and the clarity of the user. As social workers, our duty is to ensure that when we use these powerful tools, we do so with the same intention, care, and eth ical rigor that we bring to a therapy session or a policy meeting.

## 8.11  Empowerment-  Focused Activities: The Prompt Engineering Lab

Now that we have explored the theory and ethics of prompt engineering, it is time to  experiment.  Reading  about  swimming  is  helpful,  but  eventually,  you  must  get in the water. Think of this exercise as a safe 'sandbox' where you can test the cap abilities of AI without any risk to actual clients.

Your assignment is to select one of the following three tracks, Micro, Mezzo, or Macro, and apply the P.I.C.O. framework (Persona, Instruction, Context, Output) to solve a specific social work challenge. This is your opportunity to be creative and see how the AI responds to your unique leadership style. Step 1: Choose Your Scenario

## Option A: Micro Practice Track (Clinical Role-  Play)

In  this  scenario,  you  want  to  practice  your  engagement  skills  before  meeting  a  client who is hesitant about therapy. Your goal is to use AI to simulate client interaction. You will ask the AI to play the role of a client named 'Sam,' who is mandated to attend counseling but does not believe it will help.

Your Challenge : Write a prompt that instructs the AI to adopt this persona realistic ally including hesitation and defensive language, so you can practice your opening rapport-  building  statements.

## Option  B:  Mezzo  Practice  Track  (Community  Grant  Proposal)

Imagine  you  are  a  community  organizer  in  a  neighborhood  with  limited  access  to fresh produce ('food apartheid'). You have identified a vacant lot that would be per fect for a community garden. You need to draft a grant proposal to secure funding.

Your Challenge :  Write  a  prompt  that  asks  the AI  to  draft  a  'Statement  of  Need.' You must provide the AI with specific demographic context (e.g., the percentage of residents without cars or the distance to the nearest grocery store) and ask it to connect these facts to the benefits of a community garden.

## Option C: Macro Practice Track (Legislative Analysis)

Policy  work  often  involves  digesting  massive  amounts  of  dense  legal  text.  For this scenario, imagine you are advocating for changes in foster care funding. You have a long, complex legislative bill that you need to understand quickly to brief your team.

Your Challenge :  Find  a  recent  bill  summary  or  a  long  article  about  child  welfare policy (or paste a generic long text). Write a prompt asking the  AI to summarize the text specifically for a social work audience, highlighting how the changes would impact funding for aging-  out youth.

## Step 2: Draft and Refine (The P .I.C.O. Framework)

Once you have selected your track, open your preferred AI tool. Do not just ask a simple question. Instead, carefully construct your prompt using the P.I.C.O. method we discussed. Define the Persona (who the AI should be), state your Instruction clearly,  provide  the  necessary  Context  (remembering  to  invent  data  rather  than using real PII), and specify the Output format you desire.

After you submit your first prompt, look closely at the result. Is it useful? Is the tone  correct?  If  the  'client'  in  Option A  sounds  too  robotic,  or  if  the  'grant  pro -posal' in Option B sounds too generic, do not settle. Enter the 'Refine' phase. Ask the AI to adjust its tone, include more specific details, or simplify the language. Continue this dialogue until the output meets your professional standards.

## Step 3: The Reflection

Finally, write a brief reflection (approximately 200-  300 words) on your experience. Consider the following questions:

- How did assigning a specific 'Persona' impact the quality of the AI's response?
- Did  you  catch  the AI  'hallucinating'  or  making  assumptions  that  you  did  not provide?
- How did you feel acting  as  the  'director'  of  the  technology  rather  than  just  the  user?

Remember,  the  goal  of  this  assignment  is  not  to  get  a  perfect  answer  from  the machine, but to practice the skill of guiding it ethically and effectively. Have fun exploring!
