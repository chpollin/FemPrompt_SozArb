## ChatGPT in Social Work? Insights from MSW Students

Shih-Ying Cheng and Jieun Lee

## ABSTRACT

The integration of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) technologies, such as  ChatGPT,  has  drawn  significant  attention  in  higher  education.  While college  instructors  are  exploring  whether  and  how  to  adopt  these  tools,  it remains unclear what specific strengths and limitations instructors and students  may  experience  when  ChatGPT  is  used  in  the  classroom.  This  study examines  the  use  of  ChatGPT  in  a  case  consultation  assignment  involving first-year Master of Social Work (MSW) students ( N =42) who participated in both  peer  and  GenAI  consultations.  Analysis  of  students'  assignments revealed  a  range  of  views,  with  some  expressing  cautious  optimism  and others raising  concerns  about  ethics.  While  students  acknowledged ChatGPT's  strengths,  they  also  recognized  its  limitations.  They  identified specific  situations  where  this  technology  could  be  helpful  and  ethical,  as well as situations where it would be inappropriate. The findings suggest that MSW students can utilize this  technology  responsibly when provided  with adequate  guidance.  The  study  also  uncovered  a  sense  of  anxiety  among students about the rise of GenAI. Some questioned the value of their educational efforts due to ChatGPT's textbook-like responses. Although this doubt was  alleviated  after  the  exercise,  the  finding  suggests  a  need  for  further examination. Implications for teaching and research are discussed.

## ARTICLE HISTORY

Accepted: December 2024

The application of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) technologies in large language models, such as  OpenAI's  ChatGPT,  Microsoft  Edge's  Bing  Chat,  and  Google's  Bard,  has  attracted  considerable attention  in  higher  education.  Currently,  educators  are  divided  on  whether  ChatGPT  should  be prohibited or used in classrooms (Rudolph et al., 2023; Yu, 2023). An overarching recommendation for higher education instructors is to embrace the benefits of this tool while setting ethical guidelines for students' use (Espartinez, 2024; Mollick &amp; Mollick, 2023; Rudolph et al., 2023; Singer et al., 2023; Yu, 2023).  However,  it  is  unclear  what  specific  strengths  instructors  and  students  can  expect  and  what limitations they may experience when ChatGPT is used in the classroom. This study explores the use of ChatGPT in a case consultation assignment in the Human Behavior and the Social Environment (HBSE) course, where first-year Master of Social Work (MSW) students ( N =42) engaged in both peer and GenAI consultations. The study presents students' responses on the utility of ChatGPT in social work.

## ChatGPT in higher education?

GenAI  uses  advanced  machine-learning  models  and  natural  language  processing  to  understand human conversations and respond with natural, human-like languages (Wu et al., 2023). One of the most notable examples of GenAI is ChatGPT; it generates coherent text in response to users' prompts (Wu et al., 2023). Research has examined both the potential positive and negative effects of ChatGPT on  higher  education.  With  regard  to  negative  effects,  some  worry  that  the  application  of  this technology will hinder students' independent thinking abilities, particularly if students overrely on this  technology (Haque &amp; Li, 2024). Given that ChatGPT can generate outputs that mimic human

creativity and expression, this technology raises concerns about plagiarism and violations of academic integrity  (Haque  &amp;  Li,  2024;  Rudolph  et  al.,  2023;  Wu  et  al.,  2023;  Yu,  2023).  Another  concern  is inaccuracy. Due to errors and noises in the training data, ChatGPT can generate responses that have factual errors (often called 'hallucination results'; see Wu et al., 2023, p. 1129). Furthermore, because ChatGPT is trained with a large corpus of nonverifiable text from the internet, it likely offers Euroand White-centric content, which is prone to perpetuating bias and discrimination (Patton et al., 2023; Singer et al., 2023; Stokel-Walker &amp; Van Noorden, 2023; Van Dis et al., 2023).

On the other hand, some point out the benefits of GenAI tools in students' learning. One example is intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) that have been used in medical and physics college education, such as Virtual Interactive Practice Environment Resource (an ITS for medical imaging) and Why2-Atlas (an ITS that teaches qualitative physics; see Chassignol et al., 2018). These systems offer students realtime feedback based on their responses to the questions or scenarios the systems pose, resulting in better learning outcomes (Chassignol et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2020). While ITS is not widely used in social work, the modern use of GenAI presents opportunities. For example, given ChatGPT's access to a wide range of information relevant to social work, it may assist students in understanding complex concepts and fostering creativity by presenting different ideas (Haque &amp; Li, 2024; Rudolph et al., 2023; Singer et al., 2023).

## Diverse perspectives on integrating GenAI

Reactions to ChatGPT in higher education have ranged across a continuum, from outright bans on its use to the active integration of AI technology into curricula (Rudolph et al., 2023; Yu, 2023). Available research indicates a diverse range of perceptions among educators and students. According to a 2023 survey of 100 educators and 1,000 students over 18 years, it is estimated that 89% of college students have used ChatGPT to help with homework assignments, yet 72% of college students endorsed the statement that ChatGPT should be banned from their college's network (Study.com, n.d.). It is unclear why students have endorsed this statement while use is prevalent. Another study conducted with 27 participants  (15  college  students  and  12  professors)  revealed  diverse  perceptions  regarding  the integration  of  ChatGPT  (Espartinez,  2024).  These  perceptions  ranged  from  enthusiastic  adoption, where participants believed in ChatGPT's potential to enhance their educational experience, to ethical scrutiny, where participants expressed concerns about its potential misuse and advocated for developing ethical frameworks to guide its application in educational settings. Others approached the tool with  cautious  integration,  emphasizing  the  value  of  ChatGPT  while  avoiding  overreliance  and balancing its use with traditional pedagogy. While this study contributes to understanding student perceptions  of  using  ChatGPT  in  higher  education,  it  is  unclear  what  may  have  influenced  their perceptions. Profession-specific perspectives and more nuanced discussions on ethics are also needed to guide teaching practice.

As detailed guidance is still developing in higher education, an overarching recommendation on the use of ChatGPT is against a policing approach; instead of focusing on discovering and penalizing academic misconduct, it is recommended to favor an approach that embraces the benefits of this tool -at the same time, providing guidelines for responsible use (Espartinez, 2024; Patton et al., 2023; Rudolph et al., 2023; Singer et al., 2023; Van Dis et al., 2023; Yu, 2023). For example, in an article that presents varied strategies to integrate AI in college classrooms (e.g., asking AI to provide feedback, having AI accomplish tasks), Mollick and Mollick (2023) emphasized the importance of complementing these activities with students' active review of AI's outputs.

## Current study

AI technology (not specific to ChatGPT) has been applied to social work. In education, AI-based virtual simulations have been used to engage students in practicing interview and assessment skills (Asakura et al., 2020). In practice, AI chatbots have been applied to assist risk assessments and safety planning in

<!-- image -->

intimate partner abuse (Glass et al., 2022); support lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and other sexual and gender minorities youth in a mental health crisis (The Trevor Project, n.d.); and manage symptoms of depression and anxiety (Ahmed et al., 2021). Given the increasing significance of AI in the technology landscape, some social work scholars have called for the addition of a competency to the Council on Social Work Education's Educational Policy and Accreditation Standards that focuses on the knowledge and skills to responsibly and effectively use GenAI tools (Rodriguez et al., 2024). While these advancements highlight the importance of training social work students to use AI technologies ethically and effectively,  there  has  been  limited  research  on  social  work  students'  perceptions  of  GenAI.  This study, therefore, examined the extent to which ChatGPT can be useful and how social work students perceive its use in social work education and practice.

The purpose of this study is multilayered. First, we aimed to examine the utility of ChatGPT and its application in social work. Second, we sought to learn with our students and assist them in exploring this tool within an environment where there are instructions for the ethical use of this technology. We anticipate that higher education students are already using or will soon begin using this technology (Patton et al., 2023; Study.com, n.d.). Rather than leaving students to practice independently, we hope they can explore this tool in a controlled setting where guidelines for responsible use are provided. This study presents the teaching experience of a case consultation assignment in the HBSE course. It explores students' perceptions of the strengths and limitations of ChatGPT and their insights on how this technology can be used in alignment with social work ethics. Because social workers often address complex,  real-world  problems  involving  sensitive  issues  that  raise  ethical  concerns  about  GenAI, exploring this topic with social work students is especially valuable.

## Methods

Data were collected from students' case consultation assignments ( N =42) and teaching observation notes during the first year of an in-person MSW program at a large public university in an urban area of a Midwestern U.S. state in fall of 2023.

## Case consultation assignment

The HBSE course introduces various theoretical frameworks and concepts for understanding human behaviors and the effect of social contexts on individuals. The case consultation assignment, due right after the semester midpoint, is a small project with a light writing component and accounts for 15% of the  final  course  grade.  Applying  case-based  learning,  a  pedagogical  approach  that  uses  real-life  or hypothetical scenarios to engage students in critical thinking (Thistlethwaite et al., 2012), this assignment  asks  students  to  (a)  assess  a  'case'  using  the  biopsychosocial  approach  and  an  ecological perspective  (e.g.,  assessing  the  case's  challenges  and  strengths  in  the  microsystem,  mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem); (b) apply concepts and theories to their 'case';  and  (c)  present  the assessment to a small group of peer students with two questions with which they want to consult. The 'case' can be a fictional character in a novel, movie, or documentary; a person they know; or a client with whom they worked at their social work practicum or workplace. To complete this assignment, students must present their case assessment to a small group of peer students (e.g., four to five) and submit a write-up, including their reflections on the consultation process. In previous years, students reported positive experiences with this assignment; they valued the opportunity to apply theories and concepts learned in class and appreciated the feedback from their peers.

For this  exploration,  students  were  asked  to  do  an  'AI  consultation'  following  the  regular  peer consultation. They were instructed to present a condensed version of the case portrait after carefully removing any identifiable information and to consult with ChatGPT using the same questions they had posed to their peers. In their write-up, they discussed how feedback from their group differed from that of the AI chatbot and shared their thoughts on how this technology can be used in social work. This assignment was selected for this exploration for several reasons. First, to present the case to

<!-- image -->

their peers, students are asked to remove and twist any identifying and sensitive information, which is a prerequisite to using this technology. Second, the consultation preparation comes with two questions the presenter wishes to explore, making an excellent opportunity to compare human and AI responses. Third, the timing of this assignment at the midpoint of the semester made pre- and postconsultation discussions possible. This is important because this project aimed to provide students with guidance and discussion opportunities regarding ChatGPT. Last, case consultation is a regular practice in social work, given the social work profession's highlighting of interdisciplinary collaboration and diverse perspectives. Considering the wide range of information AI can access, we figured that AI might be useful in providing perspectives different from those of their peers.

## Pre- and postconsultation discussions

Two discussion sessions were scheduled: one before and one after the consultation. For the preconsultation  discussion,  step-by-step  instructions,  example  illustrations,  and  practice  exercises  were provided  to  students  to  help  them  familiarize  themselves  with  interacting  with  ChatGPT.  Table  1 presents  the  example  case  description  and  consultation  questions,  which  were  adapted  by  the  first author (SC) from her former student, with their permission. During the in-class exercise, students were  instructed  to  input  the  case  description  and  questions  into  ChatGPT,  review  the  chatbot's responses,  and  experiment  with  generating  follow-up  questions.  To  facilitate  a  comparison  of responses from AI and human consultations, Table 1 also includes a summary of peer feedback, as reported by the former student, alongside ChatGPT's responses generated by us. Caveats regarding interacting with ChatGPT were presented to students, including:

- The privacy policy of OpenAI (2023) was reviewed in class to help students understand that AI companies may collect and analyze information users upload.
- The operating mechanism of ChatGPT was discussed to help students understand that ChatGPT generates responses using available data patterns, which can contain errors and biases (Singer et al., 2023).
- The requisites for using ChatGPT were introduced, including a prohibition on the use of any identifying or protected medical information and the necessity of fact-checking (Singer et al., 2023).

In addition, because the assignment asked students to reflect on how AI aligns with social work ethics, this preconsultation discussion also included an overview of the National Association of Social Workers' ethical principles (NASW, n.d.-a) and the standards for technology in social work practice, such as confidentiality  and the  obligation  to  protect  client  privacy  (NASW,  n.d.-b).  Two  of  the  42 students engaged in this assignment did not attend this session due to sickness. The first author (SC) facilitated an individual Zoom session with them to ensure their understanding of ethical guidance.

For the postconsultation discussion, students were asked to reflect on their consultation experiences and share their thoughts about consulting with an AI chatbot and their peers. They were also asked to reflect on how they envision using AI in social work and the extent to which it aligns with social  work  ethics.  Although  students  addressed  these  questions  in  their  written  assignments,  the opportunity for open discussion may elicit new perspectives that did not emerge during individual reflection. This is also an opportunity for us to triangulate the main findings with the students. The first author (SC) shared with students the takeaways from grading students' assignments and invited students to provide further comments and reflections.

To strengthen the designs of this study and assignment, the assignment and study designs were presented to the Center for the Advancement of Teaching Excellence, the HBSE curriculum committee, and the associate dean of the Jane Addams College of Social Work at the University of Illinois Chicago. ## Data collection and analysis

Data for this study were drawn from students' written assignments and the teaching observation notes. All students enrolled in the two sections of HBSE taught by the first author (SC) were presented with this research project (e.g., purposes, data collection) and invited to participate in this study by allowing us to analyze their assignments. Their rights to not participate were emphasized. A Google Form was used for informed consent, in which students were asked to indicate their willingness to participate in the  study  and  provide  information  on  their  sociodemographic  characteristics  (e.g.,  race/ethnicity, gender) for those who opted to participate in the study. All of the 42 students who submitted this assignment provided consent.

Qualitative thematic analysis was conducted to generate themes and codes following the phases of thematic analysis proposed by Braun and Clarke (2006). The first author (SC) generated initial codes

and  collated  codes  into  potential  themes.  Themes  were  identified  primarily  through  theoretical thematic  analysis,  where  the  data  were  categorized  to  link  to  the  questions  to  which  students responded (e.g., perceived strengths and limitations, alignment with ethics) and complemented with inductive thematic analysis to identify additional themes (Braun &amp; Clarke, 2006). The second author (JL) reviewed the codes and themes and contrasted them with the literature. Both authors refined the codes and themes by developing definitions and names of each theme.

## Findings

## Sample characteristics

The sample for this study comprised 42 first-year MSW students enrolled in the HBSE course. Table 2 presents sample characteristics. Among the participants, more than half identified as female/women (63%), followed by male/men (29%) and genderqueer (7%), with an average age of 26.1 years ( SD =6.2). Slightly more than half of the participating students (55%) identified as White, followed by Hispanic or Latino/a/x (17%), Black or African American (12%), Asian (7%), and biracial or multiracial (7%). The majority (98%) were domestic students and did not hold a Bachelor of Social Work (BSW) degree (88%). The students' interests in social work settings varied, with the highest interests noted in mental health and clinical social work (74%), followed by school social work (38%) and child welfare (24%).

## Initial reactions and overall reflections

First,  some  students  expressed  surprise  at  the  effectiveness  and  relevance  of  ChatGPT  in  case consultation as their initial reaction. One student noted that they were initially skeptical due to the complexity  of  their  case  but  were  'pleasantly  surprised.'  Another  was  impressed  that  ChatGPT's suggestions were 'directly applicable and useful.' A few students even preferred ChatGPT's responses over those of their peers. One remarked that ChatGPT was better able to provide developmentally appropriate suggestions for their 'case,' a client living with a developmental disability-an area where their peers lacked expertise. Another appreciated ChatGPT's ability to identify specific resources that their peers could not provide, as illustrated by the student: 'Surprisingly enough, the AI chatbot had an advantage  over  the  feedback  from  my  group  members  because  it  listed  out  more  . . .  specific resources.' Students  also  highlighted  limitations  in  their  own  knowledge,  particularly  as  they  were still in the process of learning social work concepts:

I  am pleasantly surprised with how these consultations went. . . . I think myself and my group members were a  little  biased  when  discussing  strategies  because  lately  we  have  been  specifically  learning  about  motivational interviewing, CBT [cognitive behavioral therapy], and strengths-based approach . . ., so the AI was beneficial in suggesting things we may have not otherwise thought of.

On  the  other  hand,  with  a  deeper  analysis  and  comparison,  most  students  pointed  out  nuanced differences  and  limitations  of  AI  consultation,  including  ChatGPT's  lack  of  contextualized  understanding and emotional intelligence. For example, a student shared, 'There were some themes that were consistent between the AI and my classmates in answering my questions, but overall I felt that the AI did not have a good understanding of the nuances of my role.' Similarly, another student wrote,

My peers gave  insightful  consultation  . . .  because  when  I  presented  to  them,  I  could  give  more  context  and elaboration, whereas the chatbot only got my written case. The chatbot also spent time stating obvious points that my peers could filter through better. The peer consultation was more collaborative.

In the following, we present the identified strengths and limitations of ChatGPT.

## Perceived strengths

Students noted several advantages of ChatGPT when it was used in case consultation. Some found that ChatGPT's structured, step-by-step approach was helpful to them, and some appreciated its ability to provide comprehensive, solution-focused answers. For example, a student shared,

The  question  pertaining  to  . . .  treatment  was  best  answered  by  AI.  The  chatbot  listed  many  concepts  like developing healthy communication skills, coping strategies and emotional regulation, social skills, relationship building,  identity  support,  and  safety  and  boundary  awareness.  Along  with  these  concepts,  the  chatbot  listed specific ways in which I can implement these strategies into their lives. . . . [I]t is a huge advantage that AI is able to provide feedback within seconds.

Many  commented  on  ChatGPT's  knowledge  base  and  how  this  helped  broaden  its  responses  to theories,  intervention  strategies,  and  evidence-based  practices.  For  instance,  a  student  said  that ChatGPT provided a list of theories applicable to their case, including those they had not previously considered.  Another  student  said  that  one  main  advantage  of  ChatGPT  was  'pulling  from  a  vast wealth of knowledge,' including its ability to identify evidence-based interventions and assessments.

The quick and round-the-clock availability of ChatGPT was another acknowledged strength. One student mentioned how ChatGPT's immediate responses might help them brainstorm ideas, particularly when supervision and discussion are unavailable-an often-occurring situation in their practicum. A few students noted the chatbot's objectivity, which was seen as an advantage when human biases might affect the advice given. For example, a student shared, 'In many ways, the AI chatbot offered more complete answers than the group and was understandably more on task. Some of the group's  answers  were  emotionally  charged.'  Relatedly,  another  student  wrote,  'Sometimes  human biases can impact how cases are viewed or handled. In those instances, straightforward, non-emotional answers/advice may be more advantageous.'

## Perceived limitations

As  to  the  limitations  of  ChatGPT,  a  significant  drawback  discussed  was  its  inability  to  engage  in a back-and-forth discussion and ask clarifying questions, which is essential in facilitating a meaningful conversation, as noted by a student:

AI chatbots . . . do not really start a conversation. . . . AI chatbots will not ask questions back to me. Humans will. In asking questions back to me, the questions I ask are now deeper, have more context, and have a better focus. Through this process, I am able to develop and assess more ideas, looking for answers to questions I had not yet thought to ask.

Relatedly,  ChatGPT  might  give  general  advice  at  the  surface  level  rather  than  nuanced,  actionable advice with situational subtleties, given that more information and specifics are usually added through conversation. A student illustrated this idea well:

With the AI chatbot, it was not as convincing as talking with people in person, as they could give their reasoning for certain solutions over others. . . . For example, when one person brought up an action item as a possible step forward, I could tell them that I had already tried that or a reason why that might not be the best next move. I could give them more information about the case, and they could adjust their response from there.

While some students experimented with asking ChatGPT follow-up questions for specifics, others were  reluctant  to  converse  with  a  chatbot.  For  example,  a  student  shared,  'I  know  I  could  have probably responded with critiques or add[ed] specifics for the AI chatbot, but since it wasn't a genuine interaction, I wasn't as inclined to engage with it.'

Students also noted ChatGPT's lack of empathy and personal connection, which was considered essential, particularly for those who sought to be inspired or validated in the consultation process. For instance, a student wrote,

The  chatbot  presented  the  advice  without  feeling,  so  I  could  not  receive  it  with  feeling.  Consulting  with  the chatbot was uninspiring, while consulting with my peers inspired me to put more work and thought into the client's case. . . . I found that discussing my case [with peers] was validating, because they acknowledged that the major decisions in the case are out of my control, and they came up with questions/options I can bring to my supervisor.

Concerns about the trustworthiness and biases of ChatGPT were also raised. A student shared their doubts  about  AI  chatbots  because  their  responses  are  based  on  data  patterns  but  not  real-life experiences:

The function of the AI is to string together words that make sense in response to input. With humans, on the other hand, they are bringing lived experiences and research into the mix. AI chatbots . . . are based in patterns and not in fact.

Another student wrote, 'It is difficult to verify where Chat GPT is sourcing its answers from, and so it may produce non-evidence-based recommendations.'

## Alignment with social work ethics

Confidentiality emerged as a paramount concern among students when discussing how GenAI can be used in ways that align with social work ethics. Some shared that GenAI should never be used in social work practice because it can violate confidentiality and privacy. Others emphasized the necessity of maintaining confidentiality when interacting with ChatGPT. For example, a student who used a movie character as their case made up a pseudonym when interacting with AI. Another student twisted their case's  information:  'Instead  of  typing  'facial  tumor,'  I  typed  'medical  condition'  in  order  to  avoid sharing potentially uniquely identifying information.'

Participants also highlighted the importance of using AI with a critical mindset in tandem with human  oversight.  This  includes  fact-checking  AI  outputs,  filtering  AI  responses  with  social  work values, and seeking human input simultaneously. For example, a student wrote,

<!-- image -->

To ensure ethical usage of the tool . . . any information given by the AI must be verified by credible sources. I  would  suggest  that  the  AI  be  used  as  a  tool  in  combination  with  peer  reviewed  research  as  well  as  peer consultation.

## Another student shared,

AI chatbots . . . should only be used as a jumping-off point. We cannot take a response from AI and put it into practice verbatim. But we can take a response from a chatbot and assess it for ethics and fit, modify it as needed, and then try to put it into practice.

Moreover, students identified circumstances in which they saw the utility of using ChatGPT in social work-including listing considerations for social work assessments, brainstorming options for interventions or treatments, generating worksheets and activity plans, sourcing materials, and outlining theories and concepts. Students unanimously stressed that GenAI should only be used under human guidance,  as  illustrated  by  a  student:  'The  process  would  need  oversight  from  human  beings.  . . . ChatGPT should expand on what the social worker identifies and not the other way around.'

A few students noted that AI is an inevitable trend and that social workers should stay current with technological advances for competent practice. For example, a student wrote,

These technological advancements present a unique opportunity for social workers. . . . The alternative is that the profession  will  resist  inevitable  social  change  resulting  from  technological  advancement  and  not  only  render social workers inept and archaic, but it also creates a vacuum of unregulated, unmitigated human interaction with technology.

Similarly, another student wrote, 'Ultimately, the goals should be to integrate AI into social work to enhance the field's overall mission.'

## An emphasis on critical thinking

Among the participating students, the overall reflections on the use of GenAI in social work were mixed, with some expressing cautious optimism and others stressing concerns about ethics and its limitations. ChatGPT was considered an efficient tool for providing information and can be a starting point  for  deeper  exploration.  Most  students  were  cautiously  aware  of  ChatGPT's  limitations  and potential  harms,  including  violating  privacy  and  confidentiality,  lacking  emotional  and  contextual understanding,  and  responding  with  nonfactual  information.  Students  were  able  to  discern  the strengths  and  limitations  of  human  and  AI  consultations  and  used  the  inputs  to  maximize  their understanding of the 'case.' For example, a student wrote,

I think that both methods can be highly effective in expanding one's thinking and helpful in looking at the case from other perspectives. But both options should be taken with a grain of salt, as the most knowledgeable on the case will still be the client themselves and then the clinician.

Another student emphasized that, while AI could be beneficial, it could also be dangerous if practitioners overrely on it when considering social work interventions:

Using AI in social work can be both beneficial and dangerous depending on how it is used. . . . [T]he AI bot recommended social work interventions that I had considered, but also suggested others that I had not thought of. This can be advantageous if I was struggling with weighing out treatment or interventions options to engage in with a client. On the contrary, if AI bots are taken too seriously, it can be very dangerous for those seeking out AI information to use professionally without taking time to investigate the information given.

Students underscored the importance of critical thinking: 'People just have to use critical thinking and their human intuition to take what's useful from what the AI says, and remember that it might not be an exhaustive answer.'

## Experiencing a gain in professional confidence

An unintended consequence of this experience was the students' reaction to ChatGPT's capabilities and its effect on their thinking about education. During the preconsultation discussion, when students practiced  interacting  with  ChatGPT,  some  expressed  frustration  with  its  ability  to  generate  highquality responses so quickly. For example, one student mentioned feeling disheartened after seeing ChatGPT produce, in seconds, what it would take them hours to write. Another student expressed discomfort, stating, 'I didn't necessarily like . . . AI chatbot being able to provide me with answers in seconds  while  I  am  working  hard  to  obtain  a  degree  to  do  this  for  a  living.'  This  sentiment  was replaced  by  a  sense  of  affirmation  in  the  postconsultation  discussion,  where  students  reached a consensus that AI can never replace human social workers given its lack of emotional connection and  contextual  understanding,  as  illustrated  by  a  student:  'The  complexity  of  the  human  brain requires humans for the tailored support, empathy, flexibility, and ethical thinking that social workers provide-aspects that are irreplaceable by technology.'

## Discussion

Like many higher education instructors, we shared concerns about how GenAI might affect teaching and learning (Haque &amp; Li, 2024; Rudolph et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Yu, 2023). We heard of issues such as instances of plagiarism, including submitting assignments created by ChatGPT. We were also aware of diverse teaching practices regarding students' use of GenAI, ranging from strictly forbidding it  to  actively  incorporating  ChatGPT  into  teaching  and  learning  (Rudolph  et  al.,  2023;  Yu,  2023). Using  a  case  consultation  assignment  in  the  HBSE  course,  this  study  explored  the  strengths  and limitations  of  GenAI  and  its  application  in  social  work.  It  shares  our  teaching  experiences  with integrating ChatGPT, contributing to the limited literature on students' perceptions of GenAI and their experiences using this tool in social work education.

This  study  found  diverse  perceptions  of  GenAI  in  social  work.  While  some  students  expressed cautious optimism, others highlighted ethical concerns and limitations. The strengths of ChatGPT, such as availability and expansive knowledge, were acknowledged, but students also noted its limitations, including a lack of contextual understanding and emotional connection. These findings align with prior studies reporting varied perceptions of ChatGPT in higher education (Espartinez, 2024; Rudolph et al., 2023; Yu, 2023). In this exercise, while most students affirmed the use of ChatGPT in education, a few believed that GenAI should never be used in social work practice due to potential privacy violations. Conversely, some viewed GenAI as an inevitable trend and emphasized that social workers have an obligation to stay current with technological advances for competent practice. These varied perceptions highlight the need for further research on social work students' views of GenAI. Given that perceptions and experiences vary across courses and expectations of social work education, future  research  could  collaborate  with  instructors  of  different  courses  to  explore  the  alignment  of teaching experiences. This finding also suggests that the profession would benefit from more discussion and training on this topic, including updating ethical guidelines for technology use in social work. This should include an in-depth examination of the role and effect of GenAI in social work practice (NASW, n.d.-b).

Students in this study offered insightful discussions about the appropriateness of using ChatGPT in social  work.  Students  generally  considered  ChatGPT  suitable  as  a  starting  point  or  supplementary resource for information on theories, concepts, and intervention strategies. Other appropriate uses included identifying assessment options and generating activity plans. However, students concluded that  ChatGPT  should  not  be  a  stand-alone  resource.  Students  also  emphasized  the  importance  of strictly adhering to principles of confidentiality. These discussions have several implications. First, the findings  suggest  that,  with  proper  guidance,  MSW  students  can  use  this  technology  responsibly, supporting recommendations to embrace the benefits of ChatGPT in higher education (Espartinez, 2024; Patton et al., 2023; Rodriguez et al., 2024; Rudolph et al., 2023; Singer et al., 2023; Van Dis et al.,

<!-- image -->

2023; Yu, 2023). The specific strengths and limitations of ChatGPT that were identified can inform future exercise designs for instructors incorporating this tool into their classrooms. For example, social work students may benefit most from using ChatGPT to explore knowledge-based questions, such as applicable theories, evidence-based practices, assessment options, considerations of the influences of one's ecological systems on interventions, and lists of available resources.

Students emphasized the importance of practicing critical thinking when interacting with GenAI tools. Like other tools, ChatGPT is helpful only when used correctly. Students stressed that, ultimately, humans are the ones who consume information and make decisions; practitioners should fact-check, filter using social work values, and apply their knowledge and practical wisdom when using ChatGPT. This teaching experience underscored the need to provide guidance on using ChatGPT. Providing step-by-step instructions, hands-on practice opportunities, and detailed caveats about ChatGPT's use ensures ethical and responsible engagement. Students should be guided to develop awareness of the tool's  limitations  and  potential  harms,  and  they  should  be  asked  to  adhere  to  confidentiality  and practice  critical  thinking-including  critically  assessing  ChatGPT's  outputs,  cross-referencing  them with evidence-based practices, and reflecting on the ethical implications.Our experience suggests that future teaching practices could benefit from incorporating these strategies.

The  finding  on  the  change  in  students'  perceptions  of  ChatGPT's  capabilities  and  their  own education  is  novel.  During  the  preconsultation  exercise,  some  students  expressed  frustration  with the chatbot's textbook-like responses, leading them to question the value of their educational efforts. This sentiment mirrors broader concerns about job security in the age of AI, with fears that AI could replace human roles (Vorobeva et al., 2022; Yu, 2023). However, in our teaching experience, this doubt and frustration were alleviated after the exercise. In their written assignments and postconsultation discussions, students reached a conclusion that social workers are unlikely to be replaced by AI, citing the importance of emotional connection and nuanced understanding-qualities that AI lacks. When designing  this  assignment,  we  did  not  anticipate  that  social  work  students  would  experience  such anxiety, nor did we expect it to become a process of building professional confidence. This finding suggests the need to explore students' perceptions of GenAI, particularly regarding how it influences their views and expectations of social work education.

Last, it is worth noting that this teaching experience informs revisions to the design of this case consultation  assignment  for  future  use.  First,  given  that  some  students  expressed  concerns  (e.g., potential confidentiality violations) about GenAI, along with the growing discussions on the environmental effect of GenAI services (Berthelot et al., 2024), we believe it would be beneficial to make AI consultation optional. Those who feel uncomfortable engaging with AI consultation can opt out and instead provide a written discussion explaining their reasons. Offering this option could achieve the same goal of facilitating a discussion on this technology without requiring participation from those who  feel  hesitant  about  using  this  tool.  Second,  students  may  derive  greater  benefits  from  AI consultation  if  they  are  informed  about  what  types  of  experiences  are  more  and  less  helpful. Instructors can guide students by explaining that ChatGPT's responses have been found to be more helpful for prompts related to theories, evidence-based practice options, and assessment tools to help students create more effective prompts.

## Limitations

There are several  limitations  to  consider.  First,  this  study  was  conducted  with  first-year  MSW students  enrolled  in  two  sections  of  HBSE  courses  at  a  single  university,  so  the  findings  may not  be  generalizable  to  BSW  students  or  MSW  students  at  other  institutions.  The  teaching experience on the application of ChatGPT using different exercise designs in other classes may also  differ  significantly  from  this  experience.  Second,  the  findings  were  drawn  from  students' written  assignments  and  teaching  notes  from  students'  in-class  discussions.  Although  students were  encouraged  to  express  their  thoughts  freely,  they  might  feel  reluctant  to  report  perspectives  that  were  very  different  from  those  of  their  peers,  particularly  in  group  discussions.

<!-- image -->

Third,  this  study  is  limited  to  the  use  of  ChatGPT  in  case  consultations,  with  many  students using  hypothetical  cases,  such  as  movie  characters.  While  a  few  students  were  aware  that  AI chatbots  had  been  used  to  assist  individuals  in  need  by  providing  information  and  resources (e.g.,  Ahmed  et  al.,  2021;  Glass  et  al.,  2022;  The  Trevor  Project,  n.d.),  most  were  not  familiar with this application. Consequently, the discussion remained focused on their use of ChatGPT for  this  case  consultation.

## Conclusions

This study shares insights from integrating ChatGPT into classroom teaching. Through a case consultation assignment, it explored social work students' perceptions of the strengths and limitations of GenAI and its role in social work. The findings reveal diverse views on the use of GenAI, suggesting the need to update ethical technology guidelines within the field. The study presents students' perceived strengths and limitations of ChatGPT. It identifies scenarios where this technology may be beneficial and ethical, as well as situations where its use would be inappropriate. The findings suggest that MSW students can use this technology responsibly when given proper guidance, providing support to the call to embrace the benefits of ChatGPT in higher education (Rodriguez et al., 2024; Rudolph et al., 2023; Singer et al., 2023; Yu, 2023). This study additionally uncovered a sense of doubt among social work students regarding their  educational  efforts  in  light  of  the  rise  of  GenAI.  Future  research  should  investigate  how  the emergence of GenAI influences students' expectations of social work education.
