## Abstract

The rapid growth of Generative Arti /uniFB01 cial Intelligence (GenAI) has led to a new paradigm shift across professions. Social work, which is grounded in relational ethics and judgment, is at a crossroads of technology and professional values. Purpose: The scoping review aims to map the current landscape of GenAI adoption by frontline social workers in active practice. Method: This scoping review was conducted according to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology. Four databases were systematically searched using the PCC (Population, Concept, and Context) framework to identify studies published between 2018 and 2026. A total of 133 papers were examined, of which seven studies met the inclusion criteria. Results: The scoping review identi /uniFB01 ed four main areas of GenAI adoption in social work practices: administrative work /uniFB02 ow and documentation support, assessment and clinical decision support, client engagement and public health outreach, and professional development and supervision. Findings indicate that GenAI can signi /uniFB01 cantly reduce administrative burdens and support structured assessment work /uniFB02 ows, but practitioners across many countries have a healthy resistance to full automation. Key concerns include the empathy gap in client communication, loss of professional discretion, and digital depersonalization. Conclusion: The scoping review concludes that in social work, the adoption of GenAI should emphasize augmentation rather than automation. It should be ethically integrated to apply a domain-speci /uniFB01 c, secure AI based on empathy and human dignity. Future research should focus on longitudinal client outcomes, the evaluation of the AI tools in practice, and professional guidelines for AI-aided decision-making.

## Keywords

Generative Arti /uniFB01 cial Intelligence (GenAI) is an emerging technology built on deep learning models that are trained from massive datasets (Al-Kfairy et al., 2024). Since 2022, various GenAI tools have been created to generate content including text, images, audio, and video. Examples include OpenAI ' s GPT series (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and GPT-4o), Google ' s Gemini (formerly Bard), Meta ' s LLaMA family, and Anthropic ' s Claude. In social services, special applications such as CLOVA CareCall, Microsoft Copilot integration in case management systems, domain-speci /uniFB01 c chatbots are starting to emerge, adapted to the communication patterns and documentation requirements of frontline practice. With great accessibility and convenience, GenAI tools are widely used by professionals in information retrieval, translation, report drafting, coding, and so on (Hodgson et al., 2023; Reddy, 2024).

This interpersonal essence creates a complex relationship with GenAI. Empirical investigations into technology adoption within social work have traditionally focused on electronic health records, telehealth platforms, and decisionsupport systems (Tickner et al., 2023). ## Method

This review employed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2020), as scoping reviews are effective for examining emerging evidence in the frontier research /uniFB01 eld, such as the adoption of GenAI in social work. The presentation of results followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (Tricco et al., 2018). A prede /uniFB01 ned protocol was developed prior to the search to ensure a transparent, systematic, and reproducible process.

To ensure that the review focused on the actual use of Generative AI by frontline social workers, the eligibility criteria were developed using the PCC model (Population, Concept, Context) to map evidence and retain professional relevance. ## Source of Evidence Selection

Following the systematic search, a total of 133 records were initially identi /uniFB01 ed through systematic database searching: Web of Science (n = 31), Scopus (n = 73), ProQuest (n = 22), and ACM Digital Library (n = 7). All 133 results were uploaded to the NoteExpress platform. After removing 42 duplicates, 91 unique records were screened. One record published before 2018 was excluded, leaving 90 reports for retrieval. Of these, one report could not be retrieved, leaving 89 reports for full-text eligibility assessment.

Two independent reviewers screened the 89 reports applying the eligibility criteria. To ensure consistency, they performed calibration screening on 10% of the records, /uniFB01 nding initial agreement over 90% between the two reviewers. Any disagreements would be resolved by discussion or by consulting another reviewer. The reasons for the exclusion can be summarized as follows: (1) studies focusing on theoretical analysis of values and ethical norms in AI applications or narrative reviews without primary data (n = 29); (2) studies measuring only attitudes and perceptions about AI tools or comparing AI-generated and human-generated data without real practice experience (n = 11); (3) studies designing, testing, or validating generative AI tools and frameworks for social services without practical feedback from practitioners (n = 29); and (4) studies applying generative AI in social work education and training without real-world social work practice or frontline service delivery (n = 13). Ultimately, seven of them met all eligibility criteria and were included in the /uniFB01 nal thesis. Selection was conducted in two steps: First, two reviewers screened 90 unique record titles and abstracts against eligibility criteria. The second step was to check consistency; they pre-screened a random 10% sample with initial agreement of 90% between the two authors. ## Domains and Tasks of Generative AI Adoption in Social Work

The /uniFB01 rst domain is administrative work /uniFB02 ow and documentation support, which is the most frequent use of GenAI in all studies. This involves using LLMs and AI tools to summarize cases, write reports, draft correspondence, and complete paperwork. Báez et al. (2025) studied re /uniFB02 exively 21 U.S. clinical social workers. They found that practitioners used LLMs such as ChatGPT to write emails to large groups, organize thoughts for clinical documentation, and write templates for treatment plans. One participant said ChatGPT is ' the super brain that holds everything together ' (Báez et al., 2025, p. 9). Extending this theme from individual practice to organizational intervention, Baum et al. (2024) reported on the deployment of GenAI tools -including automated prior authorization writers and social service matching models -within multidisciplinary community-based teams serving 1,652 Medicaid patients across Washington and Virginia. The integration of these tools reduced administrative intake time by 34.2% (from 38 to 25 min per intake), demonstrating that GenAI-driven work /uniFB02 ow optimization can yield measurable ef /uniFB01 ciency gains in resource-constrained public health settings. Similarly, Tan et al. (2025), through a two-stage participatory design study with 51 social service practitioners in Singapore, found that documentation -described by participants as a task requiring them to document ' anything and everything ' (p. 4) in multiple structured formats (e.g., 5Ps, DIAP, BPSS) -was the most frequently identi /uniFB01 ed pain point amenable to AI assistance. Practitioners in that study expressed willingness to use GenAI for converting point-form notes into formal reports, with many indicating they would ' copy and paste, then amend ' (p. 5) AI-generated outputs. Although these ef /uniFB01 ciency improvements are acknowledged, practitioners are not willing to have their work fully automated. Báez et al. (2025) suggested that clinical social workers fear that handing the task to AI would strip clinical records of human-centered nuance. Tan et al. (2025) reported the same sentiment, arguing that practitioners should have their hands on AI-generated reports. This shows a key difference: GenAI is a useful tool for social work to reduce administrative burden, but professional standards favor human authorship. Cross-study comparison highlights a consistent pattern: while ef /uniFB01 ciency gains from GenAI-assisted documentation were quanti /uniFB01 ed in both U.S. (34.2% reduction in intake time; Baum et al., 2024) and Singaporean contexts (Tan et al., 2025), practitioners across all studies uniformly rejected full automation of clinical records. This transnational convergence suggests that the preference for human authorship in documentation re /uniFB02 ects a core professional value rather than a context-speci /uniFB01 c preference.

The second domain is assessment and clinical decisionsupport, which concerns the use of GenAI to support or augment clinical assessment and decision-making. The included studies offer nuanced, and at times cautionary, perspectives on this application. Bächler et al. (2025) introduced the concept of noise de /uniFB01 ned by Kahneman et al. (2021) as unwanted variability in judgments regarding social work assessment. In a survey experiment with 211 Danish child welfare social workers (115 control, 96 treatment), one group was exposed to an AI-generated assessment (Microsoft Copilot) before evaluating a vignette, while a control group assessed without AI input. Results showed that the mean concern level did not differ between groups, indicating that the AI input did not introduce new statistical bias. However, noise was reduced slightly in the AI-exposed group, with an approximate 26% reduction in the standard deviation of numerical assessments ( p = .006). The AI-generated assessment functionally served as an anchor, reducing undesirable variability while leaving the freedom to decide for practitioners. This study provides the /uniFB01 rst direct experimental evidence that GenAI can support decision-making by reducing judgment variability without undermining professional autonomy -a critical /uniFB01 nding in a /uniFB01 eld where both consistency and contextual responsiveness matter. Lehtiniemi (2023) approached assessment from a qualitatively different angle, examining how Finnish child welfare caseworkers experienced a predictive AI tool designed to forecast severe risks (emergency placement or custody) for children. Drawing on interviews with caseworkers involved in pilot trials, the study found that while the AI tool was technically capable of generating risk predictions, caseworkers ' professional epistemology clashed with the tool ' s underlying logic. Caseworkers viewed anticipation not as the production of decontextualized risk scores but as a professional knowledge-making practice grounded in intimate, situated encounters with clients. The AI ' s ' bundle-of-features ' approach to individual cases was viewed as reductionist -overlooking protective factors, coping capacities, and the person-in-situation framework central to social work. Lehtiniemi ' s concept of ' contextual AI valences ' effectively captures this tension: for AI to matter in social work assessment, it must support -rather than circumvent -practitioners ' own knowledge-production processes. Báez et al. (2025) reinforced this picture from the clinical social work perspective. Practitioners in that study described using LLMs as ' brainstorming partners ' (p. 9) for treatment planning -for example, prompting ChatGPT with ' For a 30-ish year old woman with dysthymic depression, what are some treatment goals to start with? ' (p. 30) -but

simultaneously emphasized that AI-generated suggestions required human /uniFB01 ltering and contextualization. The study identi /uniFB01 ed a clear preference for ' augmentation over automation ' (Báez et al., 2025, p. 18) with practitioners asserting that AI should function as a ' sounding board ' (Báez et al., 2025, p. 13) rather than an autonomous decision-maker. Tan et al. (2025) reported similar patterns in Singapore, where practitioners valued AI-generated assessments for ' expanding [their] perspective ' (p. 5) and identifying factors they might otherwise overlook, but stressed that AI outputs were ' just guidelines ' (p. 6) requiring supervisory review. Notably, Tan et al. also found that AI tools sometimes failed to identify safety and risk concerns that practitioners considered ' nonnegotiables ' (p. 6), such as domestic violence indicators in family cases, which underscore the limitations of generalpurpose LLMs in high-stakes clinical contexts. Comparing assessment applications across studies reveals an important distinction between experimental and naturalistic settings. Bächler et al '' s (2025) controlled survey experiment demonstrated that AI-generated assessment anchors reduced interpractitioner variability by approximately 26% without introducing statistical bias, providing causal evidence for AI ' s decision-support potential. In contrast, Lehtiniem '' s (2023) qualitative /uniFB01 ndings from naturalistic child welfare practice revealed that even technically accurate AI predictions could be epistemologically incompatible with practitioners ' situated knowledge-making processes. Together, these /uniFB01 ndings suggest that the effectiveness of GenAI in assessment depends not merely on technical accuracy but on the degree to which AI outputs respect and augment practitioners ' professional reasoning frameworks.

The third domain is client engagement, communication, and public health outreach. The selected studies reveal both the promise and the profound challenges of deploying conversational AI in relational professions. The two studies by Jo and colleagues (Jo et al., 2023; Jo et al., 2025) offer the most extensive empirical examination of this domain through the case of CLOVA CareCall, an LLM-driven voice chatbot deployed across over 140 municipalities in South Korea to conduct regular check-up phone calls with socially isolated individuals. The 2023 study, drawing on focus groups and interviews with 34 stakeholders (users, teleoperators, and developers), found that CareCall ' s open-ended conversational capabilities provided unexpected emotional bene /uniFB01 ts: users reported that the chatbot helped mitigate loneliness by asking caring questions about their health and engaging in conversations about hobbies and interests beyond narrowly de /uniFB01 ned health topics. Teleoperators valued that the system provided a ' holistic understanding of each individual ' (Jo et al., 2023, p. 2) through rich conversational logs while of /uniFB02 oading the burden of routine check-up calls. However, the study also identi /uniFB01 ed critical limitations: the LLM ' s poor long-term memory meant the chatbot could not retain personal health details across sessions, leaving users feeling as though they were ' talking to a stranger ' (Jo et al., 2023, p. 12) even after weeks of interaction. This empathy gap -the inability of current LLMs to convey genuine emotional attunement -existed as a recurring concern. The 2025 follow-up study by Jo et al. shifted the analytical lens from end-users to the public agency workers (n = 21, across 13 sites) responsible for adopting and operating CareCall. This study revealed a signi /uniFB01 cant implementation gap: while decision-makers in public agencies expected AI chatbots to expand public reach without requiring additional human resources, frontline workers experienced increased burden due to new, unanticipated labor demands -particularly the need to follow up on lapses in user engagement (e.g., individuals not answering AI calls, which triggered mandatory human callbacks). The study documented how CareCall unexpectedly served as a ' window ' (Jo et al., 2025, p. 3) for care recipients to communicate unmet social service and healthcare needs, but this serendipitous bene /uniFB01 t came at the cost of additional coordination work for already-stretched frontline staff. Báez et al. (2025) offer a clinical social work perspective on client-facing AI. Practitioners in their study noted that numerous clients were already turning independently to LLMs for mental health support -such as accessing immediate coping strategies during moments of crisis -and many recognized that these tools could expand service access for clients who face /uniFB01 nancial or systemic barriers to traditional therapy. However, practitioners voiced signi /uniFB01 cant concern over the empathy gap, with one participant asking, ' Do they feel that empathy from ChatGPT? ' (Báez et al., 2025, p. 8) and worrying that clients might forgo human support under the false assumption that AI could replace the relational depth of the therapeutic alliance. Baum et al. (2024) pointed out that GenAI affects the client beyond direct conversation, but also care coordination. In their community-based early intervention model, GenAI helped match patients to appropriate social services such as food assistance and housing, appointment scheduling, and accompaniment. The intervention resulted in a 63.3% patient goal completion rate, suggesting that AI-assisted care coordination integrated into multidisciplinary human teams can signi /uniFB01 cantly improve client engagement without destroying the human relationship.

The fourth domain is professional development, supervision, and ethical preparedness. Tan et al. (2025) provided the most detailed account of GenAI ' s role in supervision. In their participatory design study, both junior and senior practitioners identi /uniFB01 ed AI as a potentially valuable tool for enhancing the quality and ef /uniFB01 ciency of clinical supervision. Junior workers described the AI tool as a ' readily-available supervisor ' (Tan et al., 2025, p. 6) that could help them prepare for sessions by generating intervention ideas and re /uniFB02 ective questions. Supervisors, in turn, valued AI-generated prompts -such as lists of exception questions from Solution-Focused Brief Therapy -as ' really good foundation ' (Tan et al., 2025, p. 6) for discussion with supervisees. The study documented a distinctive interaction paradigm: AI-generated outputs served as shared artifacts around which supervisory

dialogue could be structured, with one supervisor noting, ' I can bring [this list] to supervision to see whether my supervisee has used these questions ' (Tan et al., 2025, p. 6). Báez et al. (2025) corroborated these /uniFB01 ndings within a U.S. sample, noting that practitioners often turned to LLMs as a ' second set of eyes ' (p. 13) when peer consultation was unavailable. Some supervisors also integrated ChatGPT into clinical supervision to prompt critical discussion, weighing the model ' s suggestions against their own professional judgment and using disagreements as teachable moments. Even so, the study raised important cautions: senior clinicians worried that overreliance on AI might spoonfeed less experienced staff, weakening the development of independent clinical reasoning. Tan et al. (2025) echoed this anxiety, with one program director asking directly, ' Will using this AI actually disable our ability to make assessments? ' (Tan et al., 2025, p. 6).

## Perceived Bene /uniFB01 ts and Challenges of GenAI Adoption in Social Work

A central /uniFB01 nding of this review is the ' ef /uniFB01 ciency-discretion paradox ' that pervades the included literature. On one hand, the evidence is unequivocal that GenAI tools can substantially reduce administrative burden. Baum et al. (2024) demonstrated a 34.2% reduction in administrative intake time with GenAI-assisted work /uniFB02 ows; Bächler et al. (2025) showed that AI-generated assessments can serve as effective anchors that reduce unwanted variability in professional judgments; and Báez et al. (2025) documented practitioners ' enthusiastic adoption of LLMs for tasks ranging from email drafting to treatment plan generation. These /uniFB01 ndings collectively suggest that GenAI can liberate practitioners from routine bureaucratic tasks, enabling greater focus on direct client work. On the other hand, the same studies reveal a pervasive resistance to ceding professional discretion to algorithmic systems. Lehtiniemi (2023) captured this tension most explicitly: Finnish child welfare caseworkers did not reject the predictive AI tool because of inaccuracy, but because its mode of knowledge production -abducing future risk from historical data -was epistemologically incompatible with the person-in-situation framework that grounds their professional judgment. As Lehtiniemi argued, ' AI does not feed into automated but human-to-human processes ' (p. 13), and for caseworkers, meaningful anticipation is a process of situated knowledge-making, not a computational outcome. This /uniFB01 nding resonates across contexts: Báez et al. (2025) reported that U.S. clinical social workers treated LLM outputs as ' starting points ' (p. 10) requiring human contextualization, while Tan et al. (2025) found that Singaporean practitioners insisted AI-generated assessments were ' just guidelines ' (p. 6) requiring supervisory review. Bächler et al. (2025) show experimentally that resistance to AI should not be ruled out as practical: When used as a decision support anchor rather than a substitute for human judgment, AI lowers decision noise without compromising professional autonomy. This pattern is the same across studies from several countries (Denmark, 2025; Finland, 2024; Singapore, 2025; USA, 2026): the preference for increasing over automation in social work is based on the professional epistemology of social work, not just context- or culture-speci /uniFB01 c preferences.

The included studies provide convergent evidence for what Báez et al. (2025) termed the ' imitations in converging empathy ' (p. 2) -the inability of current GenAI systems to convey genuine emotional attunement. This limitation was documented across multiple study designs and stakeholder perspectives. Jo et al. (2023) found that while CareCall users valued the chatbot ' s constant accessibility, they often felt disconnected during interactions. The system failed to retain personal user disclosures or sustain ongoing conversational follow-up, leaving participants feeling they were ' talking to a stranger ' (Jo et al., 2023, p. 12). Báez et al. (2025) captured this core professional apprehension through a practitioner ' s question: ' Do they feel that empathy from ChatGPT? ' (p. 15). This sentiment encapsulates widespread industry concerns that AI-driven communication may gradually weaken the foundational therapeutic alliance between worker and client. Varied user experiences complicate the narrative surrounding AI ' s empathy limitations. Jo et al. (2023) offer a critical counterperspective, /uniFB01 nding that individuals facing social stigma or intense emotional vulnerability frequently preferred engaging with AI tools. Free from the judgment inherent in human conversations, these users felt more comfortable opening up and sharing personal struggles openly. This tension raises complex ethical considerations for day-to-day practice. Ironically, the same emotional shortcomings that stop AI from supporting deep therapeutic work also make it well suited for initial outreach and triage, opening up support to marginalized groups who might otherwise shun conventional services entirely. Baum and fellow researchers further validated this practical balance in their 2024 intervention study. Their work deployed generative AI solely for care coordination tasks, never as a replacement for therapy. The tool supported outreach and appointment identi /uniFB01 cation, while community health workers retained core relational responsibilities by accompanying patients throughout the care process -strengthening, rather than replacing, human connection.

This review highlights a stark mismatch between the functional scope of mainstream LLMs and the practical demands of professional social work. Jo et al. ' s two successive CareCall studies (2023, 2025) clearly illustrate this practical mismatch. While the LLM-driven chatbot enabled /uniFB02 uid, wide-ranging conversations, it could not maintain the longterm memory needed to foster stable therapeutic rapport with clients over repeated contact. Its open-ended structure also made it hard for developers to moderate harmful content or adapt the tool for different service-user groups, such as middle-aged adults and older people living with dementia.

In line with these observations, Tan et al. (2025) showed that off-the-shelf GPT-4 systems miss key safety risks that experienced practitioners view as non-negotiable for ethical care delivery. These results highlight the need for AI tools tailored speci /uniFB01 cally to social work practice. Instead of generalpurpose chatbots, future applications in social work should be built on profession-speci /uniFB01 c knowledge bases, trained on carefully chosen datasets re /uniFB02 ecting the ethical and contextspeci /uniFB01 city of social work practice, embedded in multidisciplinary human teams, where GenAI tools in teams of community health workers, social workers, and pharmacists helped improve ef /uniFB01 ciency and patient outcomes. Baum et al. (2024) demonstrated this model effectively: embedding generative AI within teams of community health workers, social workers, and pharmacists yielded tangible gains in both service ef /uniFB01 ciency and client outcomes. Bächler et al. (2025) further suggest that domain-speci /uniFB01 c AI tools should be designed to align with the assessment scales and formats used by practitioners, as the noise-reduction effect was signi /uniFB01 cant only when the AI ' s output format matched practitioners ' assessment instruments.

## Ethical Tensions Between Algorithmic Ef /uniFB01 ciency and Relational Social Work Practice

Across all reviewed studies, a shared concern centered on the profession ' s ethical readiness for generative AI. Báez et al. (2025) recorded that many practitioners were using AI without clear organizational guidance, with one respondent noting, ' I don ' t know ethically how it sits using this unde /uniFB01 ned technology ' (p. 8). Tan et al. (2025) reported a similar concern: practitioners consistently called for clear, explicit policies for AI use, which, as a consequence, had been re /uniFB02 ected in several countries. They point out a key practice consequence: GenAI in social work means taking technology and not just technology alone, but strengthening professional standards, formal ethical standards and targeted workforce training.

The results extend beyond domain-speci /uniFB01 c applications and highlight the need for a uni /uniFB01 ed framework that includes ethics, education, and policy. The cross-sector theme of ' augmentation over automation ' is not only technical, but ethically driven since social work is human dignity and relational practice. Clinical decision support tools such as GenAI can be useful decision support tools when outputs are considered as inputs with professional context rather than as /uniFB01 nal decisions. Bächler et al. (2025) experimental /uniFB01 nding that AI-generated assessment anchors reduce noise without removing professional discretion provides a concrete model for ethically using such tools in high-stakes practice. The empathy gap observed before suggests that GenAI should not be used as a replacement for the therapeutic alliance but rather as a complement to direct client engagement.

From the practitioners ' perspective, technology advances are not ready for the workforce to respond. Current programs do not equip students to critically evaluate GenAI tools in frontline practice. Tan et al. (2025) and Báez et al. (2025) reported that practitioners expressed concerns about ethical limitations of use and that many had tried GenAI tools without training. This suggests that social work curricula should incorporate AI literacy as an essential skill that goes beyond basic digital skills and includes critical appraisal of AI outputs, algorithm bias, privacy and security, and ethics of AI-augmented practice. Supervision models should also evolve, treating AI as a stimulus for re /uniFB02 ective practice rather than as a substitute for clinical mentoring.

## Discussion and Applications to Practice

This scoping review systematically mapped seven empirical studies examining the use and adoption of Generative AI by social workers in active practice settings across /uniFB01 ve countries (Denmark, Finland, Singapore, South Korea, and the United States). Below, we return to the three research questions that guided this review to synthesize the empirical evidence and articulate their implications for future research, practice, and policy.

Regarding the speci /uniFB01 c practice domains and tasks where social workers are currently utilizing Generative AI, four domains are categorized from the scoping review. First, administrative work /uniFB02 ow and documentation support emerged as the most consistently reported application. Second, assessment and clinical decision-support represents a contested but promising domain. Third, client engagement and public health outreach have been explored through LLM-driven chatbots in practice. Fourth, professional development and supervision emerged as a nascent domain.

In terms of the perceived advantages and challenges reported by practitioners regarding the integration of GenAI into Frontline Services, across the seven studies, practitioners consistently reported ef /uniFB01 ciency gains, reduced cognitive load, and enhanced ideation support as primary advantages. However, these bene /uniFB01 ts were counterbalanced by substantial concerns such as con /uniFB01 dentiality, data security, algorithmic bias, and misinformation risks. Furthermore, developing domain-speci /uniFB01 c AI tools based on social work evidence, trained on relevant datasets, and tested for bias and noise reduction is crucial to overcome the shortcomings of generalpurpose LLMs highlighted in various studies.

In respect of how social workers navigate the ethical tensions between algorithmic ef /uniFB01 ciency and relational practice, the scoping review reveals a consistent and principled pattern across all seven studies. This ' augmentation over automation ' principle is not simply a risk-averse professional preference -it de /uniFB01 nes the core approach social workers take when engaging with algorithmic technology. Practitioners do not dismiss generative AI entirely. Rather than picking and choosing where to implement AI on an ad-hoc basis, practitioners deploy these tools only in ways that uphold professional standards and person-centered care, protecting the

irreplaceable relational work that de /uniFB01 nes their professional identity.

This scoping review is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its /uniFB01 ndings. First, the rapid evolution of GenAI technology creates a signi /uniFB01 cant temporal gap: the empirical studies included in this review examine technologies that may already be outdated, as foundation models continue to advance in capability and accessibility (Mcintosh et al., 2024). This limitation is endemic to technology-focused systematic reviews and can only be addressed through regular updating of the evidence base (Stokes et al., 2022). Second, by focusing strictly on empirical studies of frontline practitioner implementation, we excluded a substantial body of theoretical and educational literature that may contain important philosophical and ethical perspectives on GenAI in social work. Future reviews could usefully integrate these perspectives to provide a more comprehensive conceptual framework. Third, English-language databases may have geographic bias, so a new GenAI application could not be used in non-English-speaking social work settings (e.g., the Global South where resource constraints dictate different technology adoption patterns). Future reviews should include non-English databases and involve international collaborators.

Moving forward, the profession needs to address the gap between ad hoc individual experimentation and systematic, organizationally supported adoption. The results of this review point to three interrelated priorities.

For researchers, future work should focus on longitudinal studies of GenAI-assisted interventions and their impact on client outcomes over time, particularly for vulnerable groups such as children in welfare systems or socially isolated older adults. Large-scale quantitative and mixed methods studies should be conducted to study GenAI adoption rates across social work specializations and national settings. Participatory action research that involves practitioners as codesigners of AI tools will ensure technology evolves according to professional values and practice realities. Studies should also investigate whether domain-speci /uniFB01 c AI tools reduce the burden of practitioners while maintaining or improving service quality and the long-term effects of AI use on professional identity and workforce wellbeing.

For practice, social work organizations should promptly develop context-speci /uniFB01 c AI use policies to encapsulate appropriate and inappropriate applications of GenAI, such as informed consent for AI-mediated client communications, data security and con /uniFB01 dentiality, documentation for AI-aided work, professional oversight, and so on. Practitioners should treat GenAI as a supplement tool to support (rather than replace) clinical judgment, retain human authorship of all clinical records, and use AI outputs as prompts for professional decision-making, not as de /uniFB01 nitive decisions. Organizations should fund pilot programs to test GenAI tools in controlled settings before rollout, with explicit monitoring for algorithm bias and measures to ensure equitable access to AI-aided services. Meanwhile, AI literacy should be included in social work education at entry and continuing professional development levels. As Báez et al. (2025) note, the aim should be enhancement rather than automation: generative AI should strengthen the relational core of social work practice. For this, education should go beyond technical instruction and address ethical, legal, and practical aspects of AI, so practitioners can assess, use, and ethically govern generative AI in their work.

For policy development, professional regulatory bodies should de /uniFB01 ne standards of practice for AI-augmented social work that balance innovation and ethical consideration; competencies for AI use; continuing education requirements; and accountability for AI-assisted decisions. Many practitioners use AI without clear organizational guidance (Báez et al., 2025), which is ethically risky and may discourage responsible use. Professional bodies such as NASW and IFSW should have rules on con /uniFB01 dentiality, data security, algorithm bias, and limits of AI in clinical practice. Organizational policies should specify which tasks may be assisted, which require human supervision, and how AI-generated content should be documented and reviewed. On the organizational level, agencies should create interdisciplinary ethics committees of practitioners to oversee AI implementation; adopt privacyfriendly data-governance frameworks while keeping data quality; and develop career paths that recognize AI literacy as a key skill; international collaboration among social work organizations across jurisdictions would facilitate sharing ethical guidelines to different regulatory and practice contexts. Ultimately, GenAI will be successful for social work more on the profession ' s ability to shape and deploy GenAI development and deployment in ways that respect human dignity, social justice, and interpersonal relationships.
