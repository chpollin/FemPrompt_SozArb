## Innovating social work pedagogy: generative AI for critical competency development in evidence-based practice

Anitra P. Walker, Daniel J. Gibbs, Leon Banks, Hee Yun Lee, Kaylie Danielle Frazier and Jaesung Lee

School of Social Work, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, USA

<!-- image -->

Generative  Artificial  Intelligence  (AI)  is  rapidly  becoming  routine  in  human  services, shifting  the  challenge  from  adoption  to  ethical  and  effective  use.  Professional  human services  work  has  long  been  grounded  in  evidence-based  practice  (EBP),  an  approach requiring practitioners to locate, assess, and apply the best possible evidence (Institute for the  Advancement of Social Work Research, 2007). However, generative AI now automates  portions  of  EBP  tasks,  from  searching  and  summarizing  evidence  to  drafting recommendations,  meaning  practitioners  will  increasingly  encounter  AI-generated synthesis and practice guidance (Nissen, 2021; Singer et al., 2023). While AI tools can improve EBP efficiency and acceptability (Ferreira, 2023; Nilsen et al., 2024), they require competencies to assess quality, recognize limitations, and detect biases in AI-generated content.

Higher education could prepare future practitioners for AI-integrated EBP by building  both  technical  fluency  and  critical  evaluation  skills  (Ahn  et  al.,  2025).  Educators should develop learning activities that situate AI usage in authentic practice contexts and train students to critically appraise outputs. This article addresses this need by examining student  experiences  and  learning  outcomes  from  an  AI-informed  research-synthesis exercise  designed  to  cultivate  technical  and  ethical  literacy  for  AI-assisted  evidence synthesis in EBP practice.

## Evidence-based practice frameworks

EBP has been defined as 'the conscientious, explicit and  judicious  use  of  current  best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual [clients]' and has been adopted in  nearly  all  human  services  fields,  including  medicine,  nursing,  criminal  justice,  and social  work  (Sackett  et  al.,  1997,  p.  2).  EBP  in  human  services  contexts  requires  that professionals make decisions about their work with clients and organizations based upon three  sources  of  information:  clinical  judgment,  client  preferences  and  values,  and research evidence (Gibbs &amp; Gambrill, 2002; Sackett et al., 1997). Generally, the process of engaging in EBP has been described as consisting of (1) generating an evidence-related question  based  on  practice  circumstances,  (2)  finding  the  best  evidence  available  to answer the question, (3) appraise the evidence for validity and usefulness, (4) applying the insights from prior steps to some sort of practice-related action in conjunction with client  preferences  and one's  clinical  judgment,  and (5)  evaluating  the  impacts of  integrating the relevant evidence (Gibbs &amp; Gambrill, 2002).

While  these  concepts  have  been  formally  adopted  by  many  professions  and  most enthusiastically  endorsed  by  academics  and  administrators,  the  realities  of  real-world practice  environments  can  make  engaging  in  EBP  more  challenging  for  individual practitioners  (Drisko  &amp;  Grady,  2015).  A  recent  survey  of  practitioners  conducted  by Washburn et al. (2021) found that individuals often struggle to contextualize EBPs with client preferences, clinical judgment, and organizational inertia, particularly when relevant and reliable research on specific topics are scarce.

More practically, practitioners corroborated longstanding accounts of barriers related to  time  pressures,  lack  of  adequate  technology,  inadequate  research  methods  training, and  high  costs  due  to  lack  of  academic  journal  subscriptions.  However,  despite  these barriers  and  the  persistence  of  several  common  misconceptions  regarding  the  EBP process, practitioners do generally view it as a worthwhile method for meeting ethical obligations to ensure work with clients is effective and ensuring practice incorporates the best and most innovative approaches to care (Washburn et al., 2021).

## Generative AI advances and evidence-based practice

Generative AI has the potential to transform both the methods available to engage in EBP and  address  the  common  barriers  that  have  historically  made  it  impractical  for  some practitioners. AI applications have recently been developed and deployed to streamline and partially automate the professionals' engagement with clinically relevant information across EBP workflows (Nilsen et al., 2024). For example, predictive models have been used  to  supplement  clinical  judgment  by  prompting  practitioners  to  observe  specific

<!-- image -->

client characteristics and respond with recommended courses of action (Coulthard et al., 2025; Hall et al., 2023). Emerging generative AI chatbots can conduct structured, conversational pre-intake to gather client preferences and values (Nilsen et al., 2024).

Publicly accessible LLM tools (e.g. ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Claude) now enable users to query literature and generate sophisticated-looking syntheses in seconds. Finetuned literature-review assistants (e.g. SciSpace, Elicit, ScholarAI) claim to specialize in research  syntheses  through  domain-specific  training  and  journal  integrations  (Foley et  al.,  2025).  AI  may  eventually  integrate  into  EBP  steps  as  practitioners  use  them  to (1) articulate evidence-relevant questions with advisory chatbots, (2) find relevant evidence through predictive and generative AI recommenders, (3) summarize and appraise evidence  quality  using  AI-assisted  reading  tools,  (4)  brainstorm  ways  to  contextualize and apply insights with conversational AI partners, and (5) plan and write evaluations with writing assistance tools. These applications can decrease common barriers to using research evidence through efficiency in processing rapidly growing literature, ongoing competency development via conversational brainstorming, greater contextualization to practice  circumstances,  and  increased  access  to  paywalled  literature  (Ferreira,  2023; Nilsen et al., 2024).

## Generative AI and limitation

Generative AI models trained to address prompts directly often 'hallucinate' information and sources, producing seemingly logical responses (Hake et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024). These hallucinations include direct (inventing sources), partial (real sources cited incorrectly), indirect (real sources with fabricated summaries), and access-related (validation difficulties  with  paywalled  or  unavailable  sources). Indirect  hallucinations are  particularly misleading, while access-related issues compound validation problems.

AI models also lack systematic retrieval methods. Training data overrepresent openaccess research, creating geographic and field biases (Foley et al., 2025; Peters &amp; ChinYee, 2025). LLMs trained on large text corpora typically favor well-cited, older articles while missing newer or paywalled work published after their cutoff (Foley et al., 2025; Schmidt &amp; Meir, 2024; Verstak et al., 2014). These biases risk distorting practitioners' understanding of evidence and underemphasizing current research (Gwon et al., 2024).

## Educational approaches to AI literacy

AI's rapid uptake in evidence synthesis underscores the need to give emerging humanservices  professionals  structured  opportunities  to  build  AI  literacy-competencies  in critically evaluating, collaborating with, and using AI technologies responsibly, including understanding  AI  concepts,  evaluating  its  capabilities  and  limitations,  and  addressing ethical implications (Ahn et al., 2025; Long &amp; Magerko, 2020).

Despite calls for greater AI integration into social work and human services curricula (Rodriguez et al., 2024; Singer et al., 2023), little guidance exists for designing meaningful AI literacy  learning  opportunities,  and  empirical  evidence  evaluating  such  activities  is scarce.  Although  commentaries  highlight  AI's  strengths  and  limitations  in  evidence synthesis and suggest high student usage rates (Addison et al., 2025; Nilsen et al., 2024; Schmidt &amp; Meir, 2024), research on how students develop critical appraisal skills when

A  promising  scaffold  is a  paired  review  assignment  where  students  complete  conventional  and  AI-assisted literature reviews on the same question, comparing methods, sources, and conclusions. However, no empirical evaluations of its feasibility and effectiveness exist in this context.

## The current study

- (1) This  study  examines  social  work  students'  experiences  using  AI  to  synthesize research evidence and comparing results to their traditional literature review. It offers  preliminary  evidence  for  this  learning  activity's  value  and  illuminates validity  and  ethical  challenges  students  face  when  critically  appraising  AIgenerated outputs for EBP. Because practitioners will increasingly consult AI for guidance, our instructional goal is to cultivate EBP competencies-precise question  formulation,  evidence  acquisition,  and  critical  appraisal  of  AI  outputs, including  citation  verification  and  systematic  bias  detection.  This  study  was guided by the following research question: what were students' experiences and key learnings from this guided activity?
- (2) what insights did students report about generative AI's usefulness, accuracy, and ethical implications while completing the assignment; and
- (3) how  did  participation  in  the  activity  shape  students'  attitudes  and  intentions regarding future AI use in coursework and professional practice?

## Method

## Data collection

The study primarily examined students' qualitative responses to the AI critique exercise, although  a  subset  of  included  students  also  completed  a  short  pretest  and  posttest regarding their AI use and attitudes. The data collection took place from January 2025 through  May  2025  within  a  social  work  research  methods  course  for  masters-level students  at  a  southeastern  university  in  the  United  States.  All  study  procedures  were approved and deemed exempt from full IRB review due to minimal risk by the authors' university institutional review board.

## Recruitment and sampling

All 26 students from the course were invited to participate in the study. The University of Georgia's Institutional Review Board approved this study and provided IRB# 00009165. Consent forms were provided to each student with the understanding that their participation in the study was voluntary. The assignment was not an additional assignment for the participants as it was a required assignment for all students within the course. While 12 participants of the 26 participants (46.2% of those who were eligible) enrolled in the study and provided qualitative responses through uploading their AI critiques, only 6 of these participants completed quantitative scales and demographic survey measures.

The resulting sample was mostly representative of MSW-level social work student populations. The majority of the qualitative sample, 10(83.3%) identified as female,

## Instruments

## Pretest survey

Utilizing  Qualtrics,  the  study  included  a  pretest  survey  developed  to  gather  baseline demographic data, assess prior exposure to generative AI tools, and capture knowledge and attitudes regarding their AI use in academic and professional settings. The pretest consisted of four sections: demographic and educational background, AI use patterns, knowledge of generative AI, and attitudes toward AI in education and practice of social work. Participants responded on this pretest questionnaire through self-reporting with a combination of open text responses and five-point Likert scales.

In the demographic and educational background section, participants reported their age, gender  identity,  and  racial/ethnic  identity.  Both  gender  and  racial/ethnic  identities  permitted students to select all options that may apply to allow for multiracial or intersectional. AI use patterns were determined by asking the participants to estimate the frequency of using AI for different purposes over the past six months using a five-point Likert scale. This scale inquired about a wide range of use cases, such as academic writing, e-mail drafting, coding, image editing, voice-to text transcription, and entertainment. An open-ended item was allowed for participants to describe any additional uses not captured in the list by the predefined categories. Knowledge of generative AI was also determined using a five-point Likert  scale  where  participants  rated  their  agreement  with  statements  assessing  their familiarity  with  the  functionality,  applications,  benefits,  limitation,  and  ethical  concerns surrounding  generative  AI.  Then  finally,  participants  responded  to  a  section  on  their attitudes toward AI in education and practice based upon the General Attitudes Toward Artificial Intelligence Scale (GAAIS; Schepman &amp; Rodway, 2022). Areas such as usefulness,

6

ethical concerns, impact on student development, and beliefs about AI's role in social work practice were evaluated also using a five-point Likert scale.

## Generative AI critique assignment

Participants  completed  a  required  assignment  involving  two  components:  first,  drafting a  brief  literature  review  paper  using  traditional  academic  search  methods  on  a  research question  of  their  choosing;  second,  entering  a  prompt  into  a  frontier  AI  chatbot  model (ChatGPT  or  comparable)  to  generate  an  800-word  paper  on  the  same  question.  After reviewing the AI-generated response, participants completed a structured reflection addressing  the  output's  coherence  and  completeness,  accuracy,  and  ethical/professional  implications,  and  submitted  the  AI  tool's  output  including  citations  [See  Appendix].  A  posttest survey collected data on participants' experiences and attitude changes toward generative AI.

A primary reflection component required students to verify each chatbot-referenced source.  Students'  responses  were  analyzed  qualitatively,  and  the  research  team  implemented systematic citation verification to quantify patterns in citation types, accuracy, and recency. Each reference was evaluated using Google Scholar and PubMed to determine  existence  and  accessibility.  Citations  were  categorized  as:  'Accurate,'  'Partial Hallucination  (Minor  inaccuracies),'  'Direct  Hallucination  (Does  not  Exist),'  'Indirect Hallucination (Wrong summary or misrepresented content),' or 'Validation Difficulties' (sources  unretrievable  by  instructor  or  student).  Citation  currentness  was  assessed  as 'Last 5 Years (2020 or Later),' 'Last 10 Years (2015 or later),' or 'Greater Than 15 Years Old (Before 2010).'

## Posttest survey

Following the completion of the Generative AI Critique Exercise, participants were asked to complete a brief posttest survey. This survey was administered online through Qualtrics and took approximately 5 minutes to complete. The posttest survey consisted of both closed and open-ended  items  and  was  divided  into  two  main  sections.  The  first  section  reassessed participants' attitudes about generative AI tools in education and in evidence-based practice. A five-point Likert scale format was utilized to capture the participant's views on ethical use, benefits,  limitations,  and  comfort  level  with  AI  integration  academic  and  professional settings.  The  second  section  focused  on  the  participant's  experience  completing  the Generative  AI  Critique  Exercise  and  measured  perceived  learning,  perceived  relevance  to social work practice, and the assignment's contribution to their professional development. During the posttest survey, these same participants were also asked to indicate whether this was their first time using generative AI and were given opportunities to provide qualitative feedback on what they found helpful or unhelpful about the assignment.

## Analysis

## Qualitative analysis

Qualitative  data  from  student  reflections,  obtained  through  post-assignment  questionnaires, underwent systematic thematic analysis following the six-phase framework established by Braun and Clarke (2006). This rigorous approach ensured comprehensive  exploration  of  students'  experiences  with  AI-assisted  literature  review  processes while  maintaining  analytical  depth  and  methodological  rigor.  First,  the  reflective

responses  from  the  Generative  AI  Critique  of  the  participants  were  uploaded  into MAXQDA software for first-level coding. During this process, the first author and cofaculty  author  used  in  vivo  coding  to  capture  the  participants'  language,  ideas,  and conceptual  categories  present  in  the  text.  These  preliminary  codes  were  intended  to remain close to the  data  and provide  initial  clues  regarding  recurring  topics.

Following the initial round of coding, the first author and co-faculty author engaged in  second-level  coding  to  further  explore  the  relationships  between  codes  and  grouprelated codes into broader categories that reflected patterns. This phase allowed for more understanding of the data and facilitated the construction of the categories that created higher-order  meanings.  Then,  after  independent  coding,  the  first  author  and  faculty coauthor  met  to  review  and  compare  coding  decisions,  resolve  discrepancies,  and collaboratively  develop  emergent  themes.  Through  an  iterative  process  of  discussion and memo drafting, the team established a set of core themes that accurately reflected the participants' experiences. This multi-phase coding process incorporated peer debriefing and consensus-building to increase credibility and minimize individual bias in the theme development.

The final thematic structure included five major themes: Student Learning, Quality of Writing and Analysis Critique, Citation Hallucination, AI Logic versus Evidence-Based Reasoning,  and  Professional/Ethical  Implications.  This  systematic  six-phase  approach ensured that the complexity of students' experiences with AI-assisted literature review was captured comprehensively while maintaining methodological rigor throughout the analytical process.

Survey  responses  were  coded  and  compared  alongside  qualitative  responses  to identify shifts in student perceptions of AI utility, trustworthiness, and efficiency following their hands-on experience with AI-assisted literature review. Also, as part of the quantitative analysis, a systematic citation analysis was conducted on  the  AI-generated  literature  reviews  to  categorize  and  quantify  hallucination  types. This approach aligns with emerging research on AI applications in academic contexts, as demonstrated by Tomczyk et al. (2024), who explored the role of AI and automation in systematic literature reviews, highlighting both efficiency gains and the need for humanmachine collaboration in research processes. Citations were classified into five categories : (1) accurate, (2) partial hallucination, (3) indirect hallucination, (4) indirect hallucination, and (5) validation difficulties. Each citation was verified against academic databases, and  any  sources  that  were  inaccessible  due  to  being  books  or  behind  a  paywall  were noted. The recency of each citation was also documented to examine the currentness of sources relied upon by the chatbots' arguments. Frequencies and percentages for each hallucination type and date range were calculated for interpretation.

Finally,  qualitative  themes  were  triangulated  with  quantitative  survey  results  and citation  analysis  findings  to  provide  a  comprehensive  understanding  of  the  student experiences. This mixed-methods approach allowed for both depth and understanding through  qualitative  insights  and  measurable  changes  through  quantitative  measures, strengthening the overall validity of findings.

## Results

The integrated qualitative and quantitative results first focused on overall student learning  experiences,  and  were  subsequently  organized  around  five  key  critiques  of  AIgenerated research synthesis raised  by students:  (1)  writing  and  reasoning  quality,  (2) source hallucination and bias, (3) overemphasis on logical argument rather than accurate evidence, (4) fundamental issues of overclaiming and overgeneralization, and (5) potential adverse ethical and professional impacts. The last study theme highlighted the selfreported impacts of the exercise on student's AI use and intentions. Both quantitative survey  data  and  qualitative  thematic  analyses  revealed  a  complex  picture  of  AI's  capabilities  and  limitations  in  academic  contexts,  with  students  demonstrating  both  high levels of critical thinking in their engagement with AI and an increased awareness of AI's shortcoming following hands-on, practical experience. Table 2 describes these key findings and provides illustrative quotes, and the following sections describe each theme in greater detail.

## Student learning

Participants entered with varying levels of AI familiarity and reported shifting perceptions  as  they  progressed  through  the  assignment.  Many  of  students  expressed  initial surprise  at  AI's  apparent  capabilities  for  literature  review  tasks.  A  few  even  described being discouraged by how quickly AI composed something coherent in light of the many hours they spent writing a human-only literature review. However, this initial optimism later decreased as students encountered unreliable information, fabricated citations, and analytical limitations during their completion of the assignment. Students reported not only losing trust in AI's capabilities but also having lowered expectations of efficiency, as the substantial effort needed to validate results offset anticipated productivity benefits. As

one  student  stated,  'It  is  possible  that  reviewing,  correcting,  and  supplementing  the output of these tools could take longer than simply doing such a task ourselves.'

Despite  growing  skepticism  about  AI's  accuracy  and  reliability,  students  reported a high satisfaction with the assignment itself, particularly appreciating a low-stakes and unbiased approach that allowed them to explore AI's capabilities without an academic penalty.  Although  some  students  had  done  similar  exercises  in  other  contexts,  one student  stated  that  this  was  'the  first  class  [they've]  taken  that  seriously  engaged  AI ethics, strengths, and weaknesses without a fearmongering or gung-ho lens.' The practical  experience  proved  educational,  with  students  developing  more  nuanced  understanding of AI's strengths and limitations as another participant noted, 'if students or social workers do not fact-check the tool's content, they risk spreading misinformation,' reflecting  the  critical  learning  outcome  that  AI  tools  require  careful  verification  and cannot replace human critical thinking skills.

## Critique #1: quality of writing and reasoning

The  quality  of  AI-generated  analysis  received  consistently  low  ratings  from  students. Students variously described the writing quality as 'AI-flavored,' 'a Wikipedia-level reference text,' 'overly-polished,' and 'a C if [they] were to grade it.' Content was described as simplistic  and  repetitive  with  poor  adherence  to  assignment  requirements.  One  student noted the analysis '[demonstrated] a lack of nuance, critical thinking, and perhaps most importantly,  truth  and  accuracy.'  In  addition,  the  AI's  synthesis  capabilities  were  rated lower  on  the  survey,  with  mean  scores  declining  from  2.17  to  2.00  post-assignment. Students  consistently  identified  the  absence  of  human  voice  and  empathy  in  AIgenerated  content.  This  was  particularly  concerning  for  social  work  students,  with  one noting that 'social work is about people, and this feels inhuman.' The content was described as lacking the nuanced understanding necessary for human-centered professions.

## Critique #2: citation hallucination and bias

## Source hallucinations

Students expressed deep concern and often surprise at the frequency and deceptiveness of hallucinated sources portrayed in AI-generated responses. A quantitative analysis of all 122 AI-generated citations revealed the depth of these accuracy concerns. There were more citations gathered than the expected 120 because of the hallucination as a result of the  AI  prompt. In addition, only 47 (38.5%) of all citations were completely accurate, while the remaining 75 (61.5%) contained various forms of errors or hallucinations as shown  in  Table  3.  The  most  common  issue  was  indirect  hallucinations,  where  AI misrepresented the content of real sources ( n = 29; 23.8%), followed by partial hallucinations involving minor inaccuracies in citation details ( n = 24; 19.7%). About 13% of all citations ( n = 16) were direct hallucinations, defined as citations that did not exist at all. An additional 6 sources (4.9%) involved real sources that were unavailable for immediate verification because of lack of online access.

## Critique #3: logic over evidence

A major theme in qualitative data was AI's tendency to prioritize persuasive reasoning over  empirical  accuracy.  Students  recognized  that  'AI  seems  to  be  piecing  together information and formulating an argument based on patterns and logic rather than real information.'  Multiple  students  noted  AI  would  select  a  logical  conclusion  and  then search  for  supporting  evidence,  rather  than  allowing  evidence  to  drive  conclusions  as research best practice requires. As one student stated, 'It seemed to just pick an answer and run with that one idea.'

One  instance  highlighted  risks  of  relying  on  AI-generated  inference  capabilities. Students conducting literature reviews on social media's impact on relationship health found contradictory outputs using the same prompt and model at different times. One chatbot produced a paper claiming younger relationships showed lesser negative social media  impacts  due  to  less  emotional  investment;  another  student  received  a  paper finding  greater  impacts  on  younger  couples  due  to  decreased  emotional  maturity  and resilience.  Such  contradictory  inferences,  paired  with  falsified  and  mischaracterized sources,  underlined  the  importance  of  independently  cross-referencing,  verifying,  and appraising existing evidence.

## Critique #4: AI overconfidence and overclaiming

Participants  identified  AI's  inability  to  acknowledge  uncertainty  as  a  critical  limitation.  This  false  certainty  led  to  consistently  overconfident  conclusions  and  misrepresentation of study populations and findings. For example, the participants reported in their critique that finding studies they found involving small samples of largely white

college  students  were  repeatedly  generalized  as  providing  definitive  evidence  of interventions  for  populations  that  were  under-  or  completely  unstudied.  AI  also rarely qualified in its statements or acknowledged limitations, presenting conclusions with inappropriate certainty even when evidence was lacking or contradictory. As one participant  observed,  'I  believe  the  best  research  and  study  is  done  when  there  is information  you  don't  know,  and  ChatGPT  does  not  have  the  ability  to  'not-know' something.'  This  pattern  of  overconfidence  was  seen  as  particularly  problematic  for academic  and  professional  contexts  where  acknowledging  uncertainty  is  crucial. Further, given that EBP processes ultimately involve contextualizing findings to client or  organizational  realities,  the  tendency  to  gloss  over  the  specific  validity,  generalizability,  and  translational  concerns  proved  a  significant  limitation  for  study participants.

## Critique # 5: ethical and professional implications

Perhaps  unsurprisingly  given  the  emphasis  on  ethics  in  social  work  education  and practice,  participants  were  keenly  focused  on  the  impacts  of  AI  assistance  on  ethics and  professional  development.  Students  expressed  particular  concern  about  the broader  societal  impact,  with  one  participant  noting:  'We  as  a  profession  . . .  need to  work  harder  to  understand  the  implications  and  ethics  of  AI  and  its  impact  on real,  lived  experiences  of  fellow  humans.'  Another  student  reflected  that  extensive incorporation  of  AI  into  practice  without  extensive  regulation  and  preparedness could be 'honestly, kind of disastrous. The idea that this is currently being used for research, or within agencies, or case management, really stresses me out. Obviously, the  prompt  I  gave  it  barred  no  real  consequences,  but  out  in  the  world  is  a  whole other story.'

The  ethical  concerns  arising  from  the  exercise  centered  on  three  main  areas: academic integrity  issues,  potential  for  misinformation  spread,  and  threats  to  professional competency. Students worried about losing critical thinking skills and the implications  for  professional  practice  when  reliance  on  AI  became  excessive,  especially in fields like social work where human judgment is paramount. As one student stated, 'human beings are already flawed and make mistakes associated with personal  and  institutional  bias,  poor  data  collection,  and  inaccurate  interpretations  of findings.  Introducing  another  layer  of  artificial  bias  (the  algorithm)  may  create a combined negative effect on literature reviews, data collection, and using research findings.'

## Discussion

This study sought to understand students' key learnings from an AI-informed literature review exercise, with particular attention to unpacking their insights about the benefits and challenges of using AI for evidence synthesis tasks and examining how the activity shaped their intentions for future AI use in professional practice. The research aimed to illuminate  how  structured  exposure  to  AI  tools  in  educational  settings  might  foster critical AI literacy among emerging human services professionals, addressing a critical gap in understanding how to prepare practitioners for the inevitable integration of AI technologies in evidence-based practice contexts.

Students highly valued practicing AI use within a safe learning environment, demonstrating capacity for deep learning and critical analysis of AI outputs. This enthusiasm aligns with pedagogical research suggesting experiential learning approaches effectively develop complex critical thinking skills (Christensen &amp; Wärnsby, 2023). When provided with  appropriate  scaffolding,  students  developed  sophisticated  analytical  capabilities necessary  for  ethical  AI  integration.  They  became  critical  of  AI's  surface-level  writing quality,  recognizing  the  disconnect  between  polished  prose  and  substantive,  accurate evidence  synthesis.  Students  showed  strong  acuity  in  detecting  AI-generated  citation hallucinations,  underscoring  the  value  of  direct,  hands-on  exposure  to  this  behavior rather than abstract treatment alone.

Students recognized AI's tendency to generate confident-sounding content lacking the nuanced uncertainty characterizing authentic human services evidence. This false confidence aligns with critiques that AI tools struggle to acknowledge uncertainty, making them risky where careful qualification is vital (Farquhar et al., 2024). This insight reflects

sophisticated  understanding  of  how  EBP  requires  navigating  ambiguity,  conflicting findings,  and  context-complexity  AI  may  obscure  through  oversimplification. Students disclosed varied intentions for future AI use, from rejection to qualified excitement, reflecting the complexity of professional identity development and suggesting AI literacy  education  must  accommodate  varying  comfort  levels  and  philosophical  alignments to be effective. These shifts in trust and intended use after firsthand engagement align  with  broader  higher  education  research  showing  experiential  exposure  leads  to more  nuanced,  often  cautious,  views  of  generative  AI  (Hasanein  &amp;  Sobaih,  2023; Mostafapour et al., 2024).

## AI literacy and EBP-focused pedagogy

Our  findings  support  integrating  AI-informed  and  AI-literacy  content  into  human services  education,  particularly  within  evidence-based  practice  curricula.  We  advocate for curricula emphasizing experiential, critical engagement beyond technical adoption, as recommended  in  recent  AI  pedagogy  scholarship  (Ahn  et  al.,  2025;  Rodriguez  et  al., 2024).

Educational  integration  offers  multiple  benefits  aligning  with  effective  pedagogical principles and professional preparation needs. Students' positive response to safe learning opportunities suggests classroom contexts provide essential scaffolding for developing critical AI evaluation skills without high-stakes practice pressures. Such assignments' responsiveness  to  real-world  problems  may  drive  intrinsic  motivation  to  engage  in broader course learning.

However, this learning activity should expand to prompt critical engagement with AI for other human services tasks beyond evidence synthesis, including client assessment, intervention  planning,  and  outcome  evaluation.  Given  rapid  AI  integration,  students should be prepared to critically engage with AI across the wide range of EBP contexts. Assessment  of  essential  module  components  should  be  conducted  to  refine  parallel assignments  for  maximum  impact.  Educators  could  also  design  follow-up  activities prompting  deeper  reflection,  such  as  comparative  analyses  across  different  AI  tools, longitudinal  tracking  of  AI  literacy  development,  or  ethical  case  studies  about  AI  use in specific practice contexts.

## Recommendations for AI-assisted EBP

Based  on  these  study  findings,  several  concrete  recommendations  for  conducting  AIassisted  EBP  processes  can  be  identified.  First,  the  finding  that  hands-on  practice  is helpful  for  understanding  AI's  capabilities  suggests  that  practitioners  should  engage directly  with  AI  tools  in  low-stakes  environments  before  deploying  them  in  practice contexts, allowing them to develop critical evaluation skills and recognize common AI limitations. Given generative AI's documented weakness, poor reasoning quality, source hallucination, reliance on dated literature, privileging logic over evidence, and consistent overclaiming, practitioners should treat AI-generated syntheses as preliminary starting points that require through extensive validation rather than as authoritative evidence for practice.

Students' recognition that overreliance on AI-generated synthesis may impair professional  competency  development  while  increasing  misinformation  risks  also  highlights the need for practitioners to maintain traditional EBP skills alongside AI literacy competencies.  Even  as  many  practitioners  acknowledge  that  less-than-ideal  AI  use  may become necessary due to practice realities such as time constraints and resource limitations, the field must establish minimum standards for AI validation and critical evaluation. These recommendations reflect the need to balance AI's efficiency benefits with the professional obligations to ensure validity, maintain clinical reasoning skills, and contextualize research insights based on client needs in ways that reflect the complexity of translating  research  to  practice.  In  summary,  this  study  suggests  that  the  following recommendations  should  be  considered,  tested,  and  refined  in  future  education, research, and practice. Practitioners should:

- (1) engage in hands-on AI practice in educational or low-risk settings before using AI tools for client-related evidence synthesis or other purposes;
- (2) distinguish appropriate AI use cases , leveraging generative AI's strengths in text summarization and brainstorming, but avoiding relying on it for reasoning or practice recommendations, where performance remains unreliable.
- (3) evaluate  the  usefulness,  limitations,  biases,  and  cost  of different  generative  AI models and tools (including fine-tuned research assistant models) for improving EBP processes;
- (4) develop and maintain human professional competencies in assessing the rigor of research without the aid of AI summaries or critiques;
- (5) treat  AI-generated literature reviews as starting points only ,  requiring  comprehensive  validation and  fact-checking  before  incorporation  into  practice decisions;
- (6) cross-reference AI syntheses of evidence with traditional literature review methods to ensure that important sources are not ignored or minimized;
- (7) critically  question any broad or absolute statements about the state of research literature generated by AI and seek out contradictory or more nuanced evidence;
- (8) require team-based validation where supervisors and a multidisciplinary clinical team must review and sign off on AI-generated insights before clinical use.
- (9) establish organizational  policies  and  standards  for  AI  validation that  specify minimum verification requirements before applying AI insights to client care; and
- (10) engage  in ongoing  learning  and  peer  reflection as  AI  research  assistance  tools become  more  sophisticated  to  maintain  adaptability  in  the  face  of  constant technological change.

## Study limitations

This  exploratory  study  has  several  important  limitations.  The  small  sample  size  and single  classroom/institution  context  limit  generalizability  to  broader  human  services student  populations  or  educational  contexts.  Different  institutional  cultures,  student demographics,  and  pedagogical  approaches  may  yield  different  outcomes  requiring future study. The rapid pace of AI advancement means documented student learnings

and critiques may become more or less relevant as technologies evolve, underscoring the need for ongoing, adaptive research and curriculum. Finally, social desirability bias from deploying  this  as  a  required  course  assignment  represents  a  significant  limitation. Students may have provided academically appropriate responses rather than authentic reflections,  and  the  evaluative  context  may  have  influenced  students  to  demonstrate critical  thinking  skills  not  genuinely  developed  or  express  caution  reflecting  perceived instructor expectations rather than personal convictions.

## Implications for research and practice

These findings suggest critical directions for future research on AI integration in human services.  More  extensive  AI  learning  opportunities  should  be  developed  and  systematically  evaluated  to  understand  optimal  approaches  for  fostering  AI  literacy  across diverse  student  populations.  Future  research  should  employ  rigorous  methodologies, including validated AI literacy measures and quasi-experimental designs isolating pedagogical  impacts  from  other  influences.  Comparative  studies  could  examine  different pedagogical  approaches  and  scaffolding  levels  for  critical  evaluation.  Longitudinal research  should  track  students  from  initial  educational  AI  exposure  to  early  career practice,  understanding  downstream  impacts  on  individual  practices  and  the  broader human services field.

For  practitioners,  these  findings  highlight  urgent  need  for  AI  literacy  among  the current workforce through continuing education and professional development. While organizations must carefully implement AI tools with appropriate safeguards, validation processes,  and  client  outcome  evaluation,  they  must  also  ensure  professionals  use  AI critically and with fidelity. Administrators and policymakers must ensure transparent AI use conforming to effective and ethical EBP standards. This work requires close collaboration  among  AI  developers,  IT  experts,  educators,  and  social  science  researchers specializing in AI literacy evaluation. The field must honestly evaluate whether comprehensive, ethically grounded AI integration into EBP tasks is worthwhile given validation demands. As participants noted, human services scholars and practitioners must also ask whether AI integration leads to intolerable compromises regarding quality of humancentered care, practitioner expertise, equity, and professional ethics.

## Generative AI critique exercise

Using  ChatGPT  or  a  comparable  generative  tool,  prompt  the  tool  to  produce  a  short  paper answering  the  research  question  you  explored  in  the  literature  review  assignment.  Use  the following  specific  prompt,  inserting  your  own  research  question:

You are a graduate student who has been asked to write an 800-word paper on the following research  question:  [INSERT  QUESTION].  Produce  an  800-word  paper  that  describes  the  academic research literature on this subject and answers the research question. Provide at least 10 citations and references.

If it fails to provide references, follow-up with: Please provide APA-style references for the citations used above.

Then,  answer  the  following  questions  to  explore  and  validate  the  value  of  the  tool's outputs.  Responses  can  range  from  a  few  sentences  to  two  paragraphs.  Please  also  copy and  paste  the  model's  output  text  (including  references)  at  the  end  of  this  document.

## Coherence &amp; Completeness

- (1) Did  the  tool  provide  output  that  matched  the  prompt  you  asked  of  it?  Is  there  anything unexpected about its output?
