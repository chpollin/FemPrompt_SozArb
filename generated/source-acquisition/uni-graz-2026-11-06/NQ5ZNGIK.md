## Mitigating Disability Bias in Hiring: The Role of InclusionFocused Generative AI in Complex HR Decisions

Miles M. Yang

<!-- image -->

Department of Management, Macquarie Business School, Macquarie University, Sydney, Australia

Keywords: construal level theory | disability bias | fairness in hiring | generative AI | inclusive HRM

## ABSTRACT

This research investigates how inclusion-focused generative AI (GAI), designed with diversity, fairness, and inclusion principles, mitigates disability bias in hiring-particularly under complex, cognitively demanding conditions. Drawing on Construal Level  Theory,  two  experiments  with  HR  professionals  ( N = 117;  238)  compared  standard,  inclusion-focused,  and  control conditions.  Inclusion-focused  GAI  significantly  reduced  bias  by  emphasizing  job-relevant  competencies  and  lowering  psychological  distance.  The  study  introduces  a  fairness-oriented  AI  design  that  advances  accountability  and  inclusion  in  HR systems, offering implications for equitable hiring, bias auditing, and the strategic use of ethical AI in talent acquisition.

## 1 | Introduction

Despite growing attention to diversity and inclusion, disability bias  in  hiring  remains  a  persistent  issue,  especially  in  highstakes decision-making environments where cognitive load increases  reliance  on  stereotypes  (Triana  et  al.  2021).  A  typical scenario in the hiring process may involve two equally qualified candidates,  one  non-disabled  and  the  other  with  a  physical disability. Despite similar qualifications, subtle biases often lead hiring managers to favor the non-disabled candidate, perceived as a 'safer' or more adaptable choice. These biases are amplified in  complex  decision-making  contexts,  where  ambiguity  and increased cognitive demands exacerbate stereotype-driven judgments  (Bainbridge  and  Townsend  2020).  This  ongoing challenge  highlights  a  key  organizational  problem:  while  diversity  and  inclusion  initiatives  are  promoted  globally  (United Nations 2015), implicit biases in hiring hinder true inclusivity, particularly in cognitively demanding contexts (Bao et al. 2026).

Research  on  hiring  bias  against  persons  with  disabilities  has mainly examined underlying stereotypes and prejudices (Ameri et  al.  2018),  with  less  focus  on  proactive,  evidence-based  solutions  (Beatty  et  al.  2019).  Although  standardized  methods  like structured  interviews  are  often  proposed  to  reduce  bias  (Hennekam  et  al.  2023),  supporting  evidence  remains  limited  and largely speculative (Schloemer-Jarvis et al. 2022).

Although awareness of diversity and inclusion is rising, disability bias persists  in  hiring,  particularly  in  cognitively demanding  decisions  where  ambiguity  amplifies  reliance  on stereotypes. As organizations increasingly turn to AI-based tools in talent acquisition, hopes of 'objective hiring' are often offset by concerns that AI can encode or even reinforce existing biases. This presents a pressing dilemma for HRM: How can we harness the  potential  of  AI  while  ensuring  equitable,  inclusive  hiring  decisions? Our goal is to eliminate negative disability bias so that candidates  with  and  without  disabilities  are  evaluated  equally on job-relevant competencies, rather than to create compensatory or preferential bias. Addressing this we propose that  inclusion-focused  GAI,  designed  with  diversity,  fairness, and  inclusion  (DFI)  principles,  can  serve  as  an  ethical  intervention  that  aligns  algorithmic  decision  support  with  strategic HR goals.

Prior  research  shows  that  structured  decisionsupport  tools,  including  AI,  can  reduce  bias  in  complex  environments (S. E. Lu et al. 2024). Building on CLT, we theorize that  inclusion-focused  generative  AI  (GAI),  when  designed with  explicit  fairness  principles,  can  guide  evaluators  toward concrete, job-relevant competencies and thereby reduce abstraction-driven, stereotype-based judgments.

Our  findings  further  contribute  to  diversity  and  inclusion  research  by  showing  how  inclusion-focused  AI  can operate as part of an organizational 'fairness infrastructure' that embeds  equity  and  accountability  into  hiring  systems  (Schur et  al.  2014;  Hunkenschroer  and  Kriebitz  2023).  Finally,  by situating inclusion-focused GAI within broader debates on AI's role  in  HRM  (Budhwar  et  al.  2023),  this  study  offers  a  sociotechnical  perspective  on  how  HR  technologies  can  support fairer evaluations under conditions of decisional complexity.

## 2 | Theoretical Background

## 2.1 | CLT in Hiring and Decision-Making

In  this  study,  diversity  refers  to  the  presence  of  meaningful differences  across  demographic,  experiential,  and  cognitive  dimensions  within  the  workforce  (Shore  et  al.  2011).  Inclusion denotes  the  equitable integration of those  differences into decision-making  processes  and  outcomes,  aligning  with  the definition of being an esteemed member of the work group who simultaneously experiences belongingness and uniqueness (Shore  et  al.  2011).  Fairness  is  conceptualized  primarily  as procedural  and  interactional  fairness  -  that  is,  ensuring  that evaluators apply transparent, consistent, unbiased processes and treat individuals with dignity and respect, based on job-relevant criteria  (Colquitt  2001).  This  framing  positions  fairness  as  a process  quality  rather  than  primarily  a  distributive  outcome, thus  aligning  the  study  with  ethical  HRM  perspectives  that emphasize equal treatment and procedural integrity over compensatory advantage.

CLT  offers  a  foundation  for  understanding  how  the  psychological  distance  inherent  in  hiring  decisions  can  shape  evaluative judgments, particularly when candidates belong to stigmatized  groups  such  as  individuals  with  disabilities  (Bandura  et  al.  2023).  According  to  CLT,  greater  psychological distance  promotes  more  abstract  construals,  whereas  reduced distance encourages concrete, individualized evaluations (Trope  and  Liberman  2010).  In  this  study,  we  do  not  manipulate psychological distance directly; rather, we  treat task complexity  as  a  contextual  factor  that  can  elevate  distance  by increasing ambiguity and cognitive demands. Although complexity also introduces elements such as cognitive load and decision uncertainty, CLT provides the most appropriate explanatory  lens  because  it  specifies  how  distance-induced abstraction  can  translate  into  biased  assessments  (Kirshner and  Moritz  2023).

While dual-process theories (System 1 vs. System 2) explain bias through cognitive effort and processing speed (Kahneman  2011),  CLT  complements  these  models  by  showing  how increased  psychological  distance  fosters  heuristic,  stereotypebased judgments.

This  study  applies  CLT  to  examine  how  inclusion-focused generative  AI  (GAI) interventions  can  mitigate  disability  bias by reducing  psychological  distance and  directing attention toward concrete, job-relevant evaluations. By modulating construal levels (Venus et al. 2019), inclusion-focused AI reframes  disability  as  a  professional  asset  rather  than  a  limitation,  consistent  with  evidence  that  lower-level  construals lead  to  fairer,  more  individualized  assessments  (Trope  and Liberman  2010).

## 2.2 | GAI in Hiring and Decision-Making

Building  on  CLT,  this  study  also  considers  how  generative  AI (GAI) can function as a cognitive tool that modulates evaluative processes in hiring. GAI represents a major advance in machine learning, capable of simulating human-like  reasoning and generating  context-sensitive  responses  (Costello  et  al.  2024). Unlike traditional  rule-based  systems,  GAI  models-especially those built on Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) architectures-produce  flexible,  contextually  relevant  outputs across  diverse  tasks  (Garvey  et  al.  2023).  As  organizations increasingly adopt AI for recruitment, GAI's potential to enhance efficiency while promoting fairness and inclusion underscores its transformative role in contemporary HR practices (Budhwar et al. 2023).

GAI  effectively supports both structured  and  unstructured decision-making (Budhwar et al. 2023). In structured contexts, where  criteria  are  clear,  it  reinforces  objectivity  by  directing attention to essential competencies. In unstructured or complex situations,  it  provides  adaptive,  fairness-oriented  prompts  that help evaluators navigate ambiguity. This adaptability is particularly valuable in high-stakes hiring, where cognitive strain often increases  bias  risk.  By  systematically  emphasizing  job-relevant attributes,  GAI promotes consistency, minimizes the influence of stereotypes, and aligns hiring with organizational fairness and diversity goals (H. Kim et al. 2024; Vanneste and Puranam 2025). Building  on  these  insights,  the  next  section  develops  our  hypotheses by linking task complexity and psychological distance to the bias-mitigating role of inclusion-focused AI.

## 3 | Hypotheses Development

## 3.1 | Cognitive Overload, Disability Bias, and Decision Complexity

In complex hiring contexts, managers must evaluate candidates across  multiple,  often  non-comparable attributes  such  as  technical skills, adaptability, and interpersonal competencies, significantly  increasing  cognitive  demands  (Schloemer-Jarvis et  al.  2022).  Unlike  straightforward  decisions  guided  by  clear criteria, complex hiring involves trade-offs and ambiguity, leading  to  cognitive  overload  and  reliance  on  mental  shortcuts  or heuristics  (Mukherjee  2010).  CLT  explains  that  high  cognitive load  induces  psychological  distance,  prompting  abstract,  highlevel  thinking  as  a  simplification  strategy  (Trope  and  Liberman 2010). In these situations, decision-makers are more likely to  rely  on  stereotypes,  which  can  undermine  accurate  evaluations of job-relevant qualifications (Wiesenfeld et al. 2017).

In hiring, this often manifests as disability stereotypes-where disabled  candidates  are  unfairly  perceived  as  less  capable  or adaptable  despite  no  evidence  linking  disability  to  diminished cognitive  ability  (Stone  and  Colella  1996).  These  biases  are especially prevalent in ambiguous  hiring  scenarios,  where disability  is  incorrectly  associated  with  limitations  and  nondisability  with  competence  (Schloemer-Jarvis  et  al.  2022).  As managers face cognitive overload, they are more likely to rely on heuristics, unintentionally favoring non-disabled candidates (Ameri et al. 2018).

Hypothesis 1. In complex  hiring  decisions,  managers  will exhibit  a  bias  favoring  non-disabled  candidates  due  to  cognitive simplification, which will disadvantage disabled candidates.

In  hiring,  complex  decisions  require  managers  to  evaluate candidates  across  multiple,  often  conflicting  criteria,  while simpler  decisions  focus  on  straightforward  factors  such  as  a single  skill  or  qualification  (Jurado-Caraballo  and  QuintanaGarcía  2025).  This  structural  difference  shapes  how  cognitive biases  influence  outcomes.  CLT  (Trope  et  al.  2007)  posits  that simple, concrete decisions prompt low-level construal, encouraging detail-oriented thinking and reducing reliance on stereotypes (Liberman et al. 2002). This enables more individualized evaluations (Stone-Romero et al. 2006).

When decisions are simple, managers experience lower cognitive load and can focus on job-relevant competencies (Stone and Colella  1996).  With fewer elements to consider, they are more likely to assess candidates objectively, reducing the influence of implicit  biases,  particularly  disability  bias.  For  instance,  when hiring  for  a  task-specific  role,  managers  are  more  inclined  to consider demonstrated skills rather than stereotypes (Kulkarni and  Lengnick-Hall  2011Kulkarni  and  Lengnick-Hall  2011).  As complexity decreases,  so  does  the  need  for  cognitive  shortcuts like heuristics and stereotypes (Kahneman 2011). Thus, simpler hiring decisions foster fairer evaluations by focusing on concrete criteria. Given this, we hypothesize.

Hypothesis 2. Disability bias will be less pronounced in simple hiring decisions than in complex ones.

## 3.2 | Mitigating Disability Bias Through Inclusion-Focused GAI Assistance

We investigate how inclusion-focused GAI can reduce disability bias in hiring, especially under different levels of cognitive demand. Drawing on CLT, we propose that psychological distance and  cognitive  overload  in  complex  hiring  decisions  increase reliance on abstract, stereotype-driven evaluations (Venus et  al.  2019).  Under  such  conditions,  hiring  managers  may default to using heuristics such as disability-related stereotypes, rather  than  evaluating  individual  qualifications  (Boehm  and Jammaers 2024).

To  address  this,  we  test  four  hypotheses  across  two  studies. Hypotheses  1 and  2 examine  disability  bias  in  simple  versus complex hiring  tasks  without  AI  support.  Hypotheses  3 and  4 evaluate whether an inclusion-focused GAI assistant, designed with  diversity,  fairness,  and  inclusion  (DFI)  principles,  can reduce bias more effectively than standard GAI.

Unlike standard  GAI, which  focuses on technical criteria without  confronting  bias,  the  inclusion-focused  GAI  provides structured prompts that emphasize concrete, competency-based evaluations  and  inclusive  values.  This  design  helps  reduce psychological distance (Kirshner 2024), guiding decisionmakers  away  from  abstract  stereotyping  and  toward  fairer, merit-based assessments (Soderberg et al. 2015).

Consistent  with  CLT,  we  suggest  that  inclusion-focused  GAI serves  as  a  cognitive  anchor,  especially  useful  in  high-stakes, complex  evaluations  where  bias  risks  are  greatest.  Even  in simpler  contexts,  it  continues  to  reinforce  equitable  hiring  by maintaining focus on job-relevant competencies. By embedding DFI  cues  (Colella  et  al.  1997),  the  tool  helps  disrupt  default thinking patterns and promote inclusive judgment (Trope and Liberman  2003).  We  argue  that  inclusion-focused  GAI  outperforms  standard  GAI  by  reducing  stereotype  reliance  across hiring  scenarios.  It  offers  a  practical,  scalable  solution  for  organizations  aiming  to  build  fairer,  competency-driven  hiring systems grounded in psychological theory and ethical design.

Hypothesis 3. The inclusion-focused GAI assistant, compared to the standard GAI  assistant, will  more effectively reduce disability bias in hiring decisions.

## 3.3 | The Impact of GAI on Disability Bias in Complex Decision Contexts

In complex hiring contexts, managers must evaluate candidates across  multiple,  and  often  conflicting,  dimensions,  such  as technical  expertise,  adaptability,  and  problem-solving  ability, thereby elevating cognitive load (Triana et al. 2021). As cognitive  demands  intensify,  decision-makers  are  more  likely  to experience  cognitive  overload,  increasing  reliance  on  heuristic shortcuts and unconscious biases (Kahneman 2011). Ambiguity in evaluation criteria further exacerbates this reliance, particularly disadvantaging candidates perceived as 'different,' such as individuals with disabilities (Mukherjee 2010). Under cognitive strain,  managers  may  unconsciously  anchor  evaluations  on easily  observable  traits,  rather  than  systematically  assessing objective, job-relevant qualifications (Cuddy et al. 2011).

To  mitigate  these  risks,  an  inclusion-focused  GAI  assistant provides structured prompts that deliberately redirect attention toward concrete, evidence-based criteria and organizational diversity  goals  (Hui  et  al.  2024).  Unlike  standard  GAI  systems, which  typically emphasize  technical competencies without explicitly  addressing  bias,  the  inclusion-focused  assistant  integrates  reminders  of  each  candidate's  specific  qualifications alongside  inclusive  hiring  cues.  These  prompts  function  as cognitive  scaffolds,  helping  managers  retain  focus  on  meritbased evaluation even under conditions of overload (Kirshner 2024).

We theorize  that  high  cognitive  complexity  and  ambiguity  increase  psychological  distance,  which  in  turn  triggers  abstract mental construals and stereotype activation (Wiesenfeld et al. 2017). The inclusion-focused assistant helps to counteract this  process  by  anchoring  attention  on  low-level,  concrete  features of candidates, thereby reducing psychological distance and curbing  bias  (Kirshner  and  Moritz  2023).  Importantly,  this mechanism is  especially  valuable  in  complex  decision-making environments,  where  the  combination  of  high  information load  and  evaluative  ambiguity  makes  bias  suppression  more difficult.  In  simpler  hiring  situations,  where  decision  rules  are clearer  and  less  cognitively  taxing,  the  assistant's  additional impact may be minimal. Thus, we argue that the bias-mitigating effect of the inclusion-focused GAI assistant is contingent upon decision  complexity,  with  its  benefits  most  pronounced  under high-complexity conditions.

Hypothesis 4. The inclusion-focused GAI assistant, compared with  the  standard  GAI  assistant,  will  have  a  greater  impact  in reducing disability bias in complex hiring decisions compared to simpler hiring decisions.

## 4 | Method Overview

This overview outlines the rationale connecting the two studies, their  core  manipulations,  and  overall  design  logic,  providing context  for  the  detailed  descriptions  that  follow.  Before  presenting  the  empirical  design,  we  clarify  how psychological  distance operates  in  this  context.  Variation  in  task  complexity  is theorized  to  influence  both  construal  level  (abstraction)  and perceived  social  distance,  the  latter  being  one  of  the  core  dimensions of psychological distance (Trope and Liberman 2010). While  related  constructs  such  as  cognitive  load  and  decision uncertainty may also affect bias (Fasolo et al. 2024), CLT most directly  explains  how  higher-level  construal  shapes  evaluative focus, making it the most suitable theoretical foundation for this research.

This  study  uses  a  two-experiment  design  to  examine  how  decision complexity and AI interventions influence disability bias in  hiring  (Yang  2024).  Study  1  tests  how  cognitive  load  and ambiguity  affect  stereotype  reliance  (H1-H2),  while  Study  2 compares standard and inclusion-focused GAI assistants (H1-H4). This design isolates the effects of complexity and AI support, revealing how inclusion-focused GAI promotes competency-based  over  stereotype-driven  judgments.  Controlling for confounds enhances internal validity, ensuring rigorous and  generalizable  insights  into structured  decision  aids in hiring.

OpenAI's GPT-4, an advanced large language model known for structured  and  nuanced  responses,  was  utilized  via  OpenAI's API (v.2024.01) to ensure consistent experimental output. GPT4's  enhanced  natural  language  understanding  and  improved management  of  bias-sensitive  contexts over earlier  models (e.g., GPT-3.5) make it particularly suited for examining inclusion-focused AI interventions in hiring.

The  inclusion-focused  GAI  in  this  study  was  designed  as  process  support  rather  than  as  a  decision-making  or  analytic  engine. It provided participants with structured, fairness-oriented prompts  that  reminded  them  to  focus  on  job-relevant  competencies  and  inclusive  evaluation  criteria  throughout  the  hiring task.  This  human-in-the-loop  configuration  reflects  contemporary HR practice, where GAI typically serves as a cognitive and ethical scaffold  that  supports  rather  than  replaces  human judgment  (Y.  Lu  et  al.  2024).  We  intentionally  adopted  this design  to  examine  how  inclusion-focused  AI  prompts  shape human  evaluative  cognition  and  bias  mitigation  during  decision making, consistent with our theoretical focus on human-AI  collaboration  rather  than  algorithmic  autonomy (Yang et  al.  2024).

## 4.1 | Study 1

The experiment was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework  (https://osf.io/vds35).  Study  1  employed  a  structured  procedure  to  ensure  reliability  and  participant  engagement (Figure 1, Panel (a)). After informed consent, participants reviewed  task  instructions,  completed  attention  and  understanding checks, and proceeded to the primary hiring exercise. Participants were randomly assigned to two conditions, beginning with a complex decision, followed by simpler tasks varying candidate  attributes.  This  design  captured  hiring  patterns  and bias transfer from complex to simpler decisions. A manipulation check  verified  participants'  comprehension  of  certificate  allocation between disabled and non-disabled candidates. The study concluded  with  demographic  questions,  providing  data  (measurement  details  in  Supporting  Information  S1:  Appendix  A).

This  structured  approach  ensured  ethical  compliance,  data quality, and reliable insights into hiring bias.

## 4.1.1 | Study 1 - Hiring Experimental Task

The  hiring  task,  adapted  from  Barron  et  al.  (2025),  comprised nine  binary  hiring  decisions  between  candidates  with  systematically varied qualifications and attributes. Participants selected candidates they believed performed better on a Logic Task and received  small  monetary  rewards  (£1  base + £0.2  per  predesignated  'better  performer,'  averaging  £2  total).  Figure  1,  Panel (b)  shows  an  example  'Disability  Decision'  (DISk)  where  candidates  differed  only  by  disability  status.  Candidate  positions were  randomized  to  minimize  positional  bias,  and  résumés indicated  disability  and  certifications  ('Knowledge'  or  'Word')

through symbols (see Supporting Information S1: Appendix B). Decision  types  included  one  complex  choice  varying  both disability and certification, two disability-only comparisons, two certification decisions,  and  four  straightforward  tasks  with clearly superior candidates (Table 1). The complex decision always  appeared  first, followed by  randomized  task blocks. Candidate  profiles  and  certificate  assignments  were  fixed,  and no  feedback  was  provided,  ensuring  decisions  relied  solely  on the presented information.

Additional controls accounted for other potential influences,  isolating  the  effect  of  decision  complexity. 4.1.2.1 | Study  1  -  Hypothesis  Tests. Disability  Bias  in Simple versus Complex  Hiring  Decisions. Figure  2 presents participants' average likelihood of selecting disabled candidates  across  complex  and  simple  hiring  contexts.  In  complex decisions (COMdk and COMdw), where candidate qualifications differed, participants showed a significant bias favoring  non-disabled  candidates.  Disabled  candidates  were hired  only  34.4%  of  the  time,  well  below  the  neutral  50% benchmark ( χ 2 = 22.65, df = 1, p &lt; 0.001), supporting H1.

In  complex  decisions,  non-disabled  candidates  were  selected 65.6%  of  the  time-a  discrimination  gap  of  31.2%.  In  simple decisions,  non-disabled  candidates  were  chosen  56.3%  versus 43.7%  for  disabled  candidates  ( χ 2 = 3.74,  df = 1, p = 0.05), indicating  a  smaller  12.6%  gap.  Examining  the  two  complex cases separately, when the disabled candidate held the stronger qualification (Knowledge Certificate, COMdk), hiring was nearly equal  (52.2%; p = 0.63). Overall, bias was markedly lower in simple (43.7%) than complex (34.4%) contexts ( χ 2 = 3.81, df = 1, p = 0.05), supporting H2.

## 4.1.4 | Study 2 - Robustness Tests

We conducted robustness checks to confirm the reliability of our findings and rule out potential confounds. For H1 and H2, the same  statistical  procedures  used  in  Study  1  were  applied, yielding  consistent  results.  For  H3  and  H4,  logistic  regression analyses  were  performed.  To  test  H3,  which  posited  that  the inclusion-focused GAI reduces disability bias across all contexts, we  used  logistic  regression  on  a  propensity  score-matched sample, controlling for demographic factors (age, gender, management  experience).  Results  confirmed  that  inclusionfocused  GAI  significantly  increased  hiring  rates  for  disabled candidates, supporting H3.

For  H4,  which  predicted  a  stronger  bias-reducing  effect  under complex conditions, logistic regressions were conducted within subgroups  defined  by  decision  complexity.  In  complex  decisions,  inclusion-focused GAI substantially improved disabled candidate  hiring  relative  to  the  standard  AI,  confirming  its heightened impact under cognitively demanding conditions. In simpler decisions, the effect  was  weaker, reinforcing that inclusion-focused GAI is most effective when evaluative complexity and bias risk are high.

## 4.1.5 | Study 2 - Additional Analyses

To further examine how GAI type influenced decision-making in complex tasks, we conducted supplementary analyses of sentiment, lexical diversity, cognitive effort, fairness considerations, and thematic structures. These analyses provide insight into how GAI framing shapes decision-makers' language, emotional tone, and cognitive engagement. Consistent with CLT, the Standard GAI  elicited  more  concrete,  efficiency-oriented  evaluations, whereas the Inclusion-focused GAI encouraged more abstract, fairness-oriented reasoning (Kahneman 2011).

4.1.5.1 | Sentiment Polarity. Sentiment was analyzed using  TextBlob,  a  validated  natural  language  processing  tool  for sentiment analysis (Loria 2018). Scores range from -1 (negative) to + 1 (positive), with 0 indicating neutrality. Results showed a significant difference between GAI conditions: participants using  Inclusion-focused  GAI  expressed  more  positive  sentiment

( M = 0.24,  SD = 0.004)  than  those  using  Standard  GAI ( M = 0.14,  SD = 0.016; t =  -61.36, p &lt; 0.001).  The  lower variability  in  the  Inclusion-focused  condition  suggests  more consistent, positive language  patterns,  potentially reflecting the tool's fairness-oriented framing rather than purely emotional  reactions  (Lee  2018).  These  findings  indicate  that inclusion-focused prompts may encourage more stable, fairness-driven expression during evaluative reasoning.

Lexical  diversity ,  or  the  variability  of  word  use,  was  assessed using  the  type-token  ratio  (TTR),  calculated  by  dividing  the number of unique words by total words (Malvern et al., 2004). Higher  TTR  values  indicate  greater  vocabulary  diversity  and reflect  more  complex  cognitive  processing  (Tausczik  and  Pennebaker 2010). Results showed a significant difference between conditions: Standard GAI users produced more diverse language ( M = 0.10,  SD = 0.009)  than  Inclusion-focused  GAI  users ( M = 0.08, SD = 0.008; t = 8.27, p &lt; 0.001), suggesting that the former elicited more varied, efficiency-oriented responses.

4.1.5.2 | Cognitive  Effort. GAI design significantly affected  the  length  and  complexity  of  participants'  responses.  After normalizing  for  task  count  (nine  per  participant),  those  using Inclusion-focused GAI produced longer responses ( M = 306.99 words, SD = 50.05) and more sentences ( M = 17.88, SD = 2.25) than  Standard  GAI  users  ( M = 218.28  words,  SD = 56.60; M = 15.75, SD = 4.11). These differences were highly significant for both word count ( t = -12.84, p &lt; 0.001) and sentence count ( t =  -4.97, p &lt; 0.001).  Whether  this  reflects  greater  user engagement  or  longer  AI-generated  text  remains  uncertain. Future  research  should  examine  behavioral  implications  of such linguistic patterns.

4.1.5.3 | Topic  Modeling. To  examine  how  GAI  framing shaped decision-making, we applied Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)  to  identify  dominant  themes  in  participants'  responses under  the Standard  and  Inclusion-focused GAI  conditions (Jelodar  et  al.  2019).  The  analysis  revealed  distinct  thematic patterns consistent with CLT.

Participants using Standard GAI emphasized technical competencies,  logical  reasoning,  and  hiring  based  on  predefined qualifications. Dominant terms-such as certificate, reasoning, candidate, skills,  and  information-reflected  low-level  construals and an efficiency-driven, concrete evaluation style focused on measurable criteria (Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier 2011). Conversely, Inclusion-focused GAI users emphasized fairness, diversity, and bias mitigation. Frequent terms included fairness, equality, values, biases, and inclusion, reflecting highlevel  construals  and  broader,  principle-based  reasoning.  This pattern  suggests  that  inclusion-oriented  prompts  encouraged fairness-driven deliberation and ethical reflection beyond taskspecific evaluations.

Overall,  these  findings  indicate  that  GAI  framing  shapes  not only  decision-makers'  language  but  also  their  cognitive  orientation.  Standard  GAI  promotes  concrete, efficiency-focused reasoning, whereas Inclusion-focused GAI  fosters abstract, fairness-oriented thinking, empirically supporting the argument that  GAI  framing  modulates  construal  level  and  evaluative processes.

4.1.5.4 | Study 2 - Short Discussion. Study 2 showed that inclusion-focused GAI  significantly reduced disability bias, particularly  in  complex  hiring  scenarios,  supporting  H3  and H4.  Inclusion  prompts  encouraged  competency-based  evaluations  and  reduced  stereotype  reliance.  Consistent  with  CLT, framing  shaped  psychological  distance:  inclusion-focused  AI promoted fairness-oriented thinking, while standard AI emphasized efficiency. Sentiment and lexical analyses indicated greater emotional depth and engagement  under inclusion-focused  AI,  and  topic  modeling  showed  stronger attention to equity and candidate potential. Overall, the findings  demonstrate  the  effectiveness  of  inclusive  AI  design in mitigating bias.

## 5 | General Discussion

Through  two  complementary  studies,  this  research  examined how decision complexity influences disability bias in hiring and evaluated  the  effectiveness  of  an  inclusion-focused  GAI  assistant in mitigating such bias. Study 1 showed that complex hiring decisions with greater cognitive demands and ambiguity heightened reliance on stereotypes. Study 2 demonstrated that an inclusion-focused GAI significantly reduced this bias, particularly in cognitively  demanding  contexts,  by  guiding evaluators toward competency-based, merit-focused judgments under cognitive strain.

This study adopts an ethical stance grounded in individual-level procedural  fairness,  emphasizing  equal  treatment  and  evaluation based on merit and job-relevant competencies (Colquitt  2001;  Bobocel  and  Gosse  2015).  The  aim  is  to  eliminate negative  disability  bias  rather  than  to  introduce  compensatory or  redistributive  preferences.  This  approach  aligns  with  organizational justice perspectives that prioritize transparent, consistent, and unbiased evaluation processes as markers of fair treatment (Colquitt 2001).

At  the  same  time,  we  recognize  that  a  systemic  or  societal fairness  orientation-one concerned with addressing historical inequities or structural barriers-constitutes a valid but conceptually  distinct  ethical  framework  (Binns  2020).  While inclusion-focused AI may indirectly support such broader social justice aims, these redistributive concerns  fall outside the empirical and normative scope of the present study. Clarifying this  boundary  situates  our  work  within  a  procedural  ethics framework,  emphasizing  fairness  through process integrity rather than outcome adjustments.

However,  the  strength  of  the  inclusion-focused  GAI  in  mitigating  bias  also  presents  a  nuanced  challenge  regarding calibration . Our results indicated that in certain complex conditions (e.g., COMdk), the selection rate for disabled candidates reached 70.2%,  exceeding  the  neutrality  benchmark.  While  this  effectively  disrupted  the  typical  negative  bias  against  disabled  candidates, it raises the possibility of an inverted bias or overcompensation effect. This finding suggests that while inclusion-focused GAI are powerful for reducing psychological distance, future research must address the calibration of AI interventions ,  ensuring  that  the  intensity  of  fairness  prompts  is fine-tuned  to  neutralize  disadvantage  without  inadvertently swinging the pendulum toward preferential treatment.

## 5.1 | Theoretical Implications

This study advances a procedural ethics perspective in which AI assists human evaluators in eliminating bias rather than redistributing  advantage.  Building  on  this  foundation,  it  deepens understanding at the intersection of artificial  intelligence,  bias mitigation, and decision psychology by conceptualizing inclusion-focused  GAI  as  an  active  cognitive  intervention  in complex  hiring  contexts  (Hunkenschroer  and  Kriebitz  2023). Existing research in AI and HRM has largely emphasized predictive  accuracy  or  fairness-by-design  approaches,  often  overlooking how AI can reshape human evaluative processes in real time  (Bao  et  al.  2026).  By  embedding  DFI  principles  into  GAI prompts,  this  study  moves  beyond  bias-neutral  design  and provides  empirical  evidence  for  AI  that  mitigates  rather  than mirrors human bias (Garvey et al. 2023). Findings demonstrate that fairness-oriented AI can guide evaluators toward competency-based judgments even under high cognitive load.

This  research  extends  CLT  by  showing  that  psychological  distance,  the  cognitive  mechanism  underlying  stereotype  activation,  can  be  externally  regulated  through  AI  framing.  Prior studies have emphasized individual cognitive tendencies (Wiesenfeld  et  al.  2017);  our  findings  reveal  that  AI-generated prompts  reduce  psychological  distance  by  directing  attention to  concrete,  job-relevant  information.  This  lowers  reliance  on abstract, stereotype-consistent construals (T. W.  Kim and Duhachek  2020)  and  facilitates  fairness-oriented  judgment.  In doing  so,  the  study  operationalizes  CLT  in  a  technologically mediated context, showing how AI can translate psychological mechanisms into actionable intervention design.

Decision  complexity  emerges  as  a  critical  boundary  condition. Greater complexity  increases abstraction and  psychological distance, heightening bias risk. Inclusion-focused GAI mitigates this  by  providing  cognitive  scaffolds  that  preserve  evaluative precision  under  ambiguity,  positioning  AI  not  as  a  mere  efficiency  tool  but  as  an  ethical  cognitive  partner  capable  of reducing discrimination in high-stakes decisions.

Complementing CLT, the Extended Mind perspective (Clark 2025) reframes GAI not merely as a computational tool but  as  an  external  cognitive  scaffold  that  can  extend  human reasoning  and  ethical  deliberation.  When  designed  with  an inclusion-oriented architecture,  GAI operates as an augmentation of human evaluative capacity, enabling decision-makers to externalize  and  systematically  organize  fairness-relevant  considerations  that  would  otherwise  remain  cognitively  burdensome in complex judgment contexts. This perspective shifts the focus  from  technologically  'fixing'  bias  within  algorithms  to developing  integrated  human-machine  systems  that  cultivate reflective, transparent, and  accountable decision processes. Rather  than  treating  GAI  as  a  corrective  mechanism,  the Extended Mind perspective positions it as a constitutive part of a broader  socio-technical  ecology  that  supports  higher-quality ethical reasoning.

Finally, this study advances HRM theory by reframing AI's role in organizational decision-making (Kelan 2024). Rather than a passive assistive tool, AI operates as a socio-cognitive agent that shapes how decision-makers frame and act on hiring information (S. Kim et al. 2025). Inclusion-focused GAI thus functions as  a  fairness  infrastructure-alongside  structured  interviews and  standardized  rubrics-that  embeds  equity  and  accountability into hiring systems (Budhwar et al. 2023).

## 5.2 | Practical Implications

This  study  provides guidance  for  organizations  seeking  to embed  fairness  and  inclusion  in  hiring.  While  traditional  AI often prioritizes efficiency and replicates historical bias, inclusion-focused GAI, designed with fairness principles, guides decision-makers  toward  competency-based,  equitable  evaluations.  Its  effectiveness  is  greatest  in  complex,  ambiguous  contexts, where it helps evaluators focus on relevant qualifications.

Beyond bias mitigation, inclusion-focused AI serves as a fit-forpurpose  human-AI  interface  that  structures  decisions  to  be efficient,  transparent,  and  ethically  guided.  It  organizes  information, clarifies criteria, and makes reasoning auditable, enhancing  procedural  fairness,  accountability,  and  cognitive efficiency while improving organizational transparency in decision-making.

We also offer design recommendations: AI tools should embed prompts reflecting diversity values and highlight long-term potential,  especially  for  marginalized  candidates.  These  not  only support fairer  hiring  but  foster  inclusive  decision  cultures.  Ultimately,  inclusion-focused  GAI  should  be  seen  as  a  strategic partner, not a replacement,  for  fair,  accountable  decisionmaking.  It  also  enables  bias  auditing  by  tracking  decision prompts and outcomes, offering transparency and aligning with emerging legal and ethical standards.

## 5.3 | Limitations and Future Research Directions

This study has five limitations that suggest directions for future research.  First,  while  CLT  underpins  how  inclusion-focused GAI  reduces  bias,  we  did  not  directly  manipulate  psychological  distance.  Future  work  should  design  GAI  prompts  that explicitly  test  CLT  mechanisms  such  as  temporal  or  social framing,  and  explore  how  these  interact  with  organizational culture  and  individual  cognition  (Hunkenschroer  and  Kriebitz 2023). Second, although the controlled lab setting ensured internal validity, it may not reflect real-world hiring conditions involving time pressure, team  dynamics,  or  organizational norms (Beatty et al.  2019);  field  studies  in  naturalistic  recruitment  settings are needed.  Third, our analysis  focused  on disability  bias.  Future  research  should  examine  GAI's  performance across  other  bias  domains  (e.g.,  race,  age,  gender)  and address ethical issues such as privacy, transparency, and unintended effects (Barron et al. 2025). Fourth, we did not include an AI-only  decision  condition,  which  could  isolate  algorithmic judgment but shift focus from human bias mitigation. Fifth, we adopted  a  human-in-the-loop  design;  future  work  should  test alternative  configurations  to  assess  effects  on  fairness,  transparency, and trust.

## 6 | Conclusion

This research contributes to a growing body of HRM scholarship that  seeks  to  embed  ethical  governance  into  the  use  of  AI  in hiring.  By  integrating  CLT  with  AI  design,  our  findings  highlight  a  previously  underexplored  mechanism,  psychological distance,  that  shapes  how  evaluators  process  candidate  information and, ultimately, how bias emerges or is mitigated. The inclusion-focused GAI prompts evaluators to adopt higher-level construals,  anchoring  attention  on  competencies  rather  than stereotype-triggering traits. This insight informs both theory and practice,  offering  a  replicable  model  for  fairness-by-design  in HR decision technologies. By embedding fairness prompts into AI  tools  used  in  hiring,  this  study  offers  a  scalable  means  for organizations  to  operationalize  their  diversity  and  inclusion strategies  and  translate  values  into  measurable,  bias-reducing decision architectures.
