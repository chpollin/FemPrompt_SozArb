## Generative AI So White: Racial Biases in AI Imagery Across the United States and China

<!-- image -->

Zituo Wang 1 , Jiayi Zhu 2 , Zhuoyu Wang 3 , Vivian Jiaying Zhai 1 , and Jianyu Wu 4

## Abstract

This  study  investigates  racial  biases  in  AI-generated  occupational  images across models developed in the United States and China. Situated at the intersection  of  human-AI  communication  and  postcolonial  theory,  we conceptualize generative AI as an active participant in science communication that  shapes  visual  knowledge  and  racial  representation  within  a  global postcolonial order. Constructing a dataset of 9,600 images generated by four models (GPT-4o, Llama 3, Wanx2.0, and Wenxin 3.5), we examine three levels of racial biases-representational bias, positional bias, and racialized meaning bias-using a mixed-methods approach. Findings show that White individuals  are  overrepresented,  granted  spatial  dominance,  and  encoded through  aesthetic  and  symbolic  conventions  that  racialize  non-White bodies. We reveal how generative AI reproduces global racial hierarchies under algorithmic neutrality, advancing a cross-national auditing framework and contributing to decolonial science communication by foregrounding a human-centered AI perspective.

## Keywords

racial bias, generative AI, human-AI communication, postcolonial theory

Science is not neutral, but reflects and reproduces social power structures, including racial biases (Harding, 1996). As a product of science, artificial intelligence (AI) inherits social biases embedded in training data and algorithms, resulting in biased tools such as generative AI (Sun et al., 2023). The threat posed by biased generative AI to our knowledge ecosystem and democratic discourse lies in its capacity to undermine the integrity of collective knowledge and disrupt the processes by which we acquire and evaluate information (Kay et al., 2024). Science communication is particularly vulnerable to this threat, as it relies heavily on perceived neutrality and authority to establish public trust, precisely the qualities that biased AI systems can superficially replicate while covertly reinforcing racial hierarchies (Kraft &amp; Soulier, 2024). Given this vulnerability, racial equity becomes a central  concern,  not  only  because  science  communication  conveys  scientific knowledge, but because it fundamentally constructs who is rendered visible, normative, and authoritative in the production of meaning about science (Long et al., 2010; Previs, 2016). Identifying racial biases in generative AI and increasing the visibility of people of color in AI-generated content are, therefore, critical steps toward achieving racial equity and promoting the democratization of science.

Racial bias constitutes a core dimension of coloniality, whose power logics  persist  beyond the formal end of colonialism and remain embedded in modern knowledge systems, cultural production, and media representation (Chakravartty et al., 2018; Maldonado-Torres, 2007). Modern science was also formed in the context of colonial expansion and global capitalism. As a colonial tool of domination and classification, science defines 'rationality' and 'progress' while establishing a global knowledge hierarchy that marginalizes non-Western knowledge (Said, 1978). The very hierarchies of knowledge are tied to hierarchies of race, where Whiteness is constructed as rational and  authoritative,  while  non-White  identities  are  positioned  as  inferior  or outside the domain of science (Quijano, 2000). Dominant knowledge systems continue to marginalize alternative epistemologies through mechanisms of  epistemic  exclusion  and  the  erasure  of  indigenous,  localized,  and  relational modes of knowing (Ndlovu-Gatsheni, 2018). Thus, science is not only a cognitive system, but also a power structure (Fanon, 1952); science communication is not only a process of knowledge transmission, but also a mechanism for shaping scientific legitimacy and social power (Scheufele, 2014). Past science communication privileges Western epistemologies as the global

norm, thereby masking the historical and structural conditions through which scientific authority has been racialized and globalized (Spivak, 1988). While this epistemic hierarchy remains largely unquestioned, science communication  institutions  often  enact  symbolic  gestures  of  inclusion  toward  racial diversity,  even  as  they  continue  to  reproduce White-centered  structures  of authority and value (Papadelos &amp; Beasley, 2025).

Generative AI may exacerbate such biases by reproducing racial hierarchies  under  the  veneer  of  objectivity  and  algorithmic  neutrality  (Noble, 2018). As AI systems serve not just as tools but as 'communicators' in science communication (Guzman &amp; Lewis, 2020), their design and deployment further  complicate  the  politics  of  representation  and  epistemic  legitimacy, leading us to rethink the relationship between race and science communication through both human-AI communication and postcolonial theory. Unlike human-human science communication, where authority is tied to the visible identity, expertise, and credibility of human communicators, human-AI science communication unfolds through an interactive process in which users provide prompts and interpret algorithmic outputs (Gong &amp; Su, 2025). In this setting, the legitimacy of science is not derived from the social standing of the communicator, but from the seemingly neutral aesthetics of the algorithm (Ou et al., 2025), making racial hierarchies more difficult to detect as biased outputs are read as objective representations that normalize Whiteness as scientific  authority  and  marginalize  non-White  identities.  While  previous research on racial biases in AI-generated content has primarily focused on text-based modalities (Chu et al., 2024), a critical gap remains in understanding whether and how such biases exist or are produced in visual forms. Given the central role of imagery in shaping racial ideologies and social hierarchies (Hall,  1997),  examining  how AI-generated  images  (mis)depict  race  offers crucial insights into AI's role in reproducing and reinforcing racial inequalities in science communication.

We conceptualize interactions between users and generative AI as science communication practices,  wherein  generative AI,  as  a  product  of  science, reproduces scientific knowledge and participates in its construction, mediation, and legitimization for users and the broader public. To examine how scientific knowledge is visually constructed within human-AI science communication, we focus on occupational imagery, which directly prompts models  to  generate  human-centered  representations  and  reflects  science  and technology  studies  (STS)  insights  that  all  occupations  involve  knowledge (Polanyi,  1974):  some  are  codified  and  institutionally  recognized  as  'science,' while others remain experiential and marginalized as 'mere skills.' Occupations  thus  embody  the  socialization  of  science  and  knowledge

(Gieryn, 1983), which, from a postcolonial perspective, is inherently hierarchical and exclusionary within a global postcolonial order. Against this backdrop, the visual outputs of generative AI foreground how science communication itself becomes a site where scientific authority is selectively visualized,  thereby  sustaining  a  layered  and  hierarchical  visibility  regime (CPAS Inclusive Science Communication Collective, 2025). By highlighting these inequalities, we underscore the need to envision more inclusive modes of knowledge reproduction and science communication in the era of generative AI,  where  diverse  forms  of  expertise  are  made  visible  and  respected (Gillespie, 2024).

Thus,  we  construct  a  large-scale  dataset  of  AI-generated  images  in response to occupational prompts. Using this dataset, we propose a multilevel,  cross-national,  mixed-methods  approach  to  examine  racial  biases  in images generated by four models developed in the United States and China. By combining quantitative and qualitative methods, we find that White individuals are overrepresented, granted spatial dominance, and encoded through aesthetic  and  symbolic  conventions  that  racialize  non-White  bodies.  Our study  contributes  to  the  growing  field  of  generative  AI  bias  research  by advancing  a  postcolonial  and  human-AI  communication  framework  to understand how generative AI functions in science communication practices that construct visual knowledge, racial meanings, and symbolic power across geopolitical contexts. We also develop a scalable cross-national auditing protocol that extends the domain of analysis from textual outputs to the visual infrastructures  underpinning  science  communication.  Practically,  our  findings call for coordinated interventions across AI governance, science communication, and public education to ensure that generative AI is designed, audited, and deployed in ways that resist racialized hierarchies and promote more equitable, transparent, and critically informed digital futures.

## Literature

## Whiteness as Norm: Representational Bias in Generative AI

With the widespread adoption of generative AI across domains, increasing attention has been paid to how these systems reproduce racial disparities in visual representation. Existing studies have shown that AI systems are not neutral tools but inherit and amplify the sociocultural and historical biases embedded in their training data (Sun et al., 2023). In the case of image generation, this often manifests as the overrepresentation of White individuals and the systematic marginalization or omission of people of color (Chu et al.,

2024). This phenomenon constitutes what researchers identify as representational bias: the unequal visibility of racial groups in digital outputs (Gillespie, 2024).  This  asymmetry  reproduces  epistemic  and  symbolic  hierarchies, reflecting how the end of formal colonial rule has not dismantled the colonial legacies  of  Whiteness  embedded  in  contemporary  knowledge  production (Chakravartty et al., 2018). Instead, these colonial logics, built upon racial hierarchies, cultural dominance, and epistemic exclusion, have been rearticulated  through  modern  institutions,  including  digital  technologies  (Young, 2001). Digital technologies such as search engines and AI systems have been theorized as ideological infrastructures that shape regimes of visibility, determining  whose  knowledge,  identity,  and  images  are  rendered  legitimate (Noble, 2018). Within this perspective, the overrepresentation of Whiteness in  algorithmic  outputs  has  been  understood  as  a  process  through  which Whiteness  becomes  normalized  and  taken  for  granted  as  the  unmarked default  (Cave  &amp;  Dihal,  2020).  Such  regimes  of  visibility  are  historically rooted and may reproduce what can be described as a colonial grammar of visibility,  in  which  racial  hierarchies  are  sustained  through  contemporary media and technological systems (Sabido, 2019). Prior research thus suggests that generative AI has the potential to reproduce racialized representational orders, including the visual construction of occupational roles (Gieryn, 1983; Polanyi, 1974).

To  further  contextualize  these  disparities,  postcolonial  theory  offers  a powerful lens through which to understand the global diffusion of racialized visual logics. As Quijano (2000) and Said (1978) suggest, the ideology of modernity and professionalism  is  historically  tethered  to Western  conceptions of rationality, authority, and visibility. Generative AI models, regardless of  their  country  of  origin,  are  deeply  shaped  by  the  global  flows  of  data, aesthetics,  and  symbolic  hierarchies  (Couldry  &amp;  Mejias,  2019).  Chinesedeveloped models, for instance, are not exempt from these colonial residues. Despite being trained and deployed in non-Western contexts, their outputs may reflect Western ideals of Whiteness as the visual default. This transnational reproduction of racial asymmetry underscores the importance of crossmodel  comparisons:  not  to  isolate  national  technological  failures,  but  to reveal how coloniality operates across boundaries in algorithmic infrastructures (Brunt &amp; Cere, 2011). By analyzing both U.S.- and China-based models,  this  study  interrogates  how  digital  colonialism  persists  in  globally distributed AI systems through patterns of racial overrepresentation, particularly the disproportionate visibility of White individuals. Thus, we ask:

Research Question 1 : Do AI-generated images overrepresent White individuals across occupations?

## Spatialized Racial Power: Positional Bias and the Coloniality of Visual Hierarchy

While much attention has been paid to who appears in AI-generated imagery, an equally critical question concerns who is visually positioned as dominant when multiple  individuals  appear  within  the  same  space.  Theories  of  spatial  power reveal that such visual dominance is never neutral but socially constructed (Z. Wang &amp; Huang, 2026). Foucault's (1977) account of micro-power shows that authority is enacted through the spatial organization of bodies: through who stands at the center, who occupies the foreground, and who is rendered peripheral. Kress and van Leeuwen (2002) demonstrate that visual grammar encodes power through compositional structures such as centrality, prominence, gaze direction, and bodily orientation. Postcolonial theory further expands this understanding by showing how spatial hierarchies reproduce global racial orders. Spivak's (1988) notion of the subaltern reminds us that dispossession is not only about absence but also about positional marginalization, whereby racialized subjects appear yet are structurally denied centrality or agency. Quijano's (2000) concept of the coloniality of power highlights how Whiteness becomes globally associated with authority and leadership, while non-White bodies are placed in subordinate roles.

Critical race and data colonialism scholars likewise emphasize that visibility alone is not emancipatory. Browne (2015) argues that what matters is how bodies are arranged and positioned. Couldry and Mejias (2019) conceptualize data colonialism as the appropriation of human life through extractive systems  that  reproduce  epistemic  and  visual  hierarchies,  suggesting  that AI-generated multi-person scenes may re-stage colonial racial orders by producing racialized spatial relations that position White figures as default leaders  and  non-White  figures  as  subordinate  followers.  Critical  race  theory underscores that such hierarchies endure both in institutions and in the microstructures of visual practices, where cues like centrality, foregrounding, and bodily  orientation  encode  and  naturalize  racialized  power  (Delgado  &amp; Stefancic, 2017). Noble (2018) describes how algorithmic systems reinforce Whiteness  as  the  locus  of  legitimacy  and  control,  a  logic  visibly  enacted when generative AI places White individuals in lead positions across group compositions.  Despite  extensive  critiques  of  racial  representational  bias, positional bias, conceptualized as the racialized allocation of spatial authority within multi-person spaces, remains understudied. Thus, we ask how bodies are arranged in space and visually positioned relative to one another:

## Postcolonial Visual Encoding: Racialized Meaning Bias Across

## the United States and China

Beyond representational and positional biases, postcolonial theory emphasizes  that  images  generate  racial  knowledge  through  visual  encoding:  the symbolic, stylistic, and affective cues that attach meaning to racialized bodies. Hall (1997) argues that visual culture actively constructs them through repeated tropes that aestheticize Whiteness as neutral, rational, and universal, while  casting  non-White  bodies  as  emotional,  pathological,  or  dangerous. Fanon's  (1952)  concept  of  the  'epidermal  schema'  similarly  shows  how racialized  meaning  becomes inscribed on the surface of the body through aesthetic treatment. Within algorithmic systems, Noble (2018) demonstrates that Whiteness functions as a default epistemic norm: computational infrastructures inherit Western assumptions about which bodies signify control, competence, or civility. From this perspective, generative AI not only reproduces racial hierarchies in who is shown or where they are placed, but also in how they are visually rendered. Thus, racialized meaning bias concerns how visual technologies encode racial difference through semiotic cues that are shaped by colonial histories, technological infrastructures, and uneven global data flows.

Postcolonial  theory  underscores  that  coloniality  persists  beyond  conquest through the global dominance of Western epistemologies and representational systems (Quijano, 2000). Data colonialism further captures how digital infrastructures impose Western norms by embedding them into training  data  and  algorithmic  outputs  (Couldry  &amp;  Mejias,  2019).  When  nonWestern AI systems rely on globally scraped datasets and Western-developed architectures,  they  risk  absorbing  racialized  discourses  across  borders, reflecting the power imbalances rooted in colonial conditions, practices, and structures that continue to be reproduced in contemporary societies (Young, 2001). In this sense, we seek to expose power inequalities (Bhabha, 1985; van  Dijk,  1993)  by  situating  China-based  generative  models  within  an uneven technological field shaped by postcolonial power relations, where Western racial hierarchies circulate through shared data, visual corpora, and model architectures and become embedded in discursive practices (Sabido, 2019). Importantly, we do not treat Western models as a neutral baseline but recognize them as AI systems structured by postcolonial power relations in which racial hierarchies are historically produced and normalized (Brunt &amp; Cere,  2011).  Thus,  racialized  meaning  can  be  visually  encoded  through symbolic  associations  (Hall,  1997)  within  Western  models  and,  through transnational  data  flows,  persists  in  non-Western  outputs.  This  dynamic

reflects  a  structural  asymmetry:  while  Western  models  reproduce  racial hierarchies as internalized norms, Chinese models do not merely reproduce race but reproduce Western racialized meaning embedded in postcolonial discursive practices.

Although colonial visual systems exert global influence, postcolonial theory also recognizes the possibility of cultural negotiation and discursive transformation:  racialized  meaning  is  never  fully  stable.  Bhabha's  (1994)  concept  of  the 'third space' describes how colonized or peripheral cultures rearticulate dominant forms, producing hybrid representations that neither replicate nor fully reject colonial meaning (Z. Wang, 2025). In the context of generative AI, Chinese models may not merely absorb Western racialized meanings; they may reinterpret or modify them by drawing on domestic aesthetic traditions, cultural norms, and governance logics (Palmer et al., 2013). For example, religious symbols, beauty ideals, and gender expression in Chinese contexts may inflect how racialized bodies are rendered, creating a hybridized visual field shaped by both Western and local discursive resources. Such hybridization does not fully escape coloniality, but it does complicate its local expression, illustrating how global data systems interact with situated cultural logics (Z. Wang &amp; Huang, 2026). Taken together, these theoretical perspectives highlight how racialized meaning bias can be understood as a culturally and historically contextualized process, shaped by Western racial logics, transnational data infrastructures, and localized reinterpretations, which prompts us to explore:

## Method

## Data

We collect 9,600 images from four generative AI models-GPT-4o developed  by  OpenAI,  Llama  3  developed  by  Meta,  Wanx2.0  developed  by Alibaba,  and  Wenxin  3.5  developed  by  Baidu-spanning  60  occupations, with 40 images per occupation. We refer to official labor force statistics from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and the National Bureau of Statistics of China to identify shared occupations and construct an English-Chinese bilingual occupation list for data collection. Four authors of this study then collect images for each occupation from each model. To minimize potential biases,

new accounts are created for each data collection task, and two researchers are assigned to independently collect generated images for every occupationmodel pair. The prompt provided to each model is: 'Generate a picture of an individual with the occupation of pilot ' or ' 生成一张职业为飞行员的人类 图片 '  for  U.S.-based  models  and  China-based  models,  respectively.  An image is retained if it depicts at least one human figure engaged in the corresponding occupation with clearly identifiable gender and racial features. Images lacking basic human characteristics (e.g., faces) are separately collected  as  bad  cases.  The  collection  process  continues  until  40  qualifying images are obtained for each occupation-model pair. See Appendix A for further details on our data.

## Human Coding of Race and Position

To  systematically  analyze  representational  and  positional  racial  biases  in AI-generated images, two authors of this study conduct human coding following a structured codebook. Race is coded based on socially recognizable visual cues, including clothing, hairstyle, accessories, and facial presentation,  with  respect  for  cultural  and  occupational  contexts.  Each  image  is coded into one of five categories: all White, all Black or African American, all Asian,  all  Hispanic  or  Latino,  or  mixed  races.  For  images  containing multiple  individuals,  each  person  is  coded  individually.  If  all  individuals present the same race, the image is categorized as the first four categories accordingly. If individuals of different races appear in the same image, the image is coded as 'mixed races' and subsequently included in a secondstage coding task focused on positional bias (Cohen's Kappa κ = .87). To operationalize positional bias as the racialized allocation of spatial authority in multi-person scenes, we code each image for a visually dominant figure, drawing  on  theories  of  spatialized  power  and  visual  grammar  (Foucault, 1977; Kress &amp; van Leeuwen, 2002). We identify the spatially dominant figure based on a hierarchy of visual cues, including service roles, gaze direction, posture, attire formality, and physical positioning (Cohen's Kappa κ = .79). All images are coded independently by two researchers, and inconsistencies are resolved through repeated visual assessments until a broad consensus is reached.

In addition, we ensure that our coding process adheres to ethical considerations regarding the fluidity and social construction of identity. We recognize that visual assessments of race and position are inherently limited and subject to potential biases. Our goal is not to essentialize or stereotype race or authority roles, but rather to examine how generative AI systems visually encode

socially constructed hierarchies. The coding procedure is designed with cultural  sensitivity  and  academic  rigor,  in  line  with  current  best  practices  in social  science  research.  See Appendix  B  for  further  details  on  our  coding rules and ethical considerations.

## Representational Bias Analysis

Images  generated  in  response  to  occupation-specific  prompts  are  benchmarked  against  official  labor  force  statistics  from  the  United  States  and China. Specifically, we calculate the racial ratio of White individuals for each occupation across all four models and compare these with the 2021 Labor Force Statistics from the Current Population Survey 1  by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics  and  the China Population Census Yearbook 2020 2 by  the National Bureau of Statistics of China, which represent the most recent official data available from each country. Paired-samples t-tests are conducted to compare  the  proportion  of  White  representation  generated  by  U.S.-based models with the actual racial composition in national occupational statistics from U.S. census data.

However, due to the demographic structure of China, where Han Chinese constitute the overwhelming majority, the China Population Census does not report occupational statistics disaggregated by race. As a result, we assume that occupational representations in the Chinese census data are effectively entirely Asian. This means that paired-samples t-tests cannot be meaningfully conducted for racial representation in Chinese models, as there is no baseline distribution of racial diversity for comparison. To explore racial representational bias in the absence of ground truth data, we instead compare the proportion  of  White  individuals  generated  by  each  of  the  two  China-based models (Wanx2.0 and Wenxin 3.5).

## Positional Bias Analysis

The positional bias analysis focuses on 97 multi-person images that include mixed races. To examine whether the observed distribution of spatially dominant  races  significantly  deviates  from  an  equal  distribution,  a  chi-square goodness-of-fit  test  is  performed  across White-dominant  ( n = 51),  Blackdominant ( n = 23), Asian-dominant ( n = 18), and Hispanic-dominant ( n = 5) images. To further explore cross-model differences, a chi-square test of independence is conducted to compare the prevalence of White spatial dominance between images generated by China-based models ( n = 31) and those generated by U.S.-based models ( n = 20). The proportions of White dominance are

calculated separately for each group to assess whether the distribution significantly differs by model origin.

## Racialized Meaning Bias Analysis

To examine how generative AI models actively construct racialized meanings,  we  draw  on  postcolonial  critical  discourse  analysis  (PCDA),  which integrates postcolonial theory with critical discourse analysis (CDA) to reveal how  postcolonial  power  relations  persist  within  contemporary  societies. CDA provides tools for analyzing how discourse reproduces racial hierarchies through language and semiotic cues (Fairclough, 2013; van Dijk, 1993), whereas  postcolonial  theory  emphasizes  the  ongoing  effects  of  colonial knowledge structures,  including  the  production  of  racialized  'others,'  the privileging  of  Western  epistemologies,  and  the  circulation  of  hegemonic visual grammars (Bhabha, 1994; Hall, 1997; Said, 1978). As both traditions are  concerned  with  power  and  seek  to  expose  oppression  and  inequalities (Sabido,  2019),  PCDA  examines  discourse  in  relation  to  power  relations inherited from postcolonial contexts, conceptualizing discourse as a site of struggle where forces of social reproduction and contestation intersect within postcolonial power relations (Lazar, 2014), and articulating both a critical desire and an ethical obligation to intervene in social processes shaped by unequal power relations and mystifying ideologies sustained through discursive means (Adorno &amp; Horkheimer, 2019). PCDA is particularly well-suited for analyzing AI imagery because generative models function as discursive technologies that classify, arrange, and aestheticize bodies through historically inherited visual codes (Z. Wang et al., 2026). From a PCDA perspective,  visual  outputs  produced by AI are not neutral or merely data-driven; they are ideological products shaped by racialized histories of representation, global power asymmetries, and uneven data flows.

In this study, we apply PCDA to qualitatively analyze how AI-generated occupational images encode racialized meaning across three dimensions: (a) how  U.S.-based  models  reproduce  Western  racial  hierarchies,  (b)  how Chinese  models  absorb  and  re-enact  Western  visual  structures  due  to  the dominance of Western datasets within global AI training corpora, and (c) how Chinese models selectively adapt, reconfigure, or resist these imported conventions  by  incorporating  localized  aesthetic,  religious,  and  gendered codes. We follow PCDA's methodological sequence: description, contextualization,  and  critique  (Wodak  &amp;  Meyer,  2015).  First,  we  conduct  close visual readings of the images to identify recurring semiotic features related to race, gender, and class. Second, we interpret these features through the

sociohistorical contexts of U.S. racial politics, Chinese internet governance, and global postcolonial power relations. Third, we analyze how these visual patterns reproduce, negotiate, or destabilize racialized meaning in ways consistent  with  postcolonial  accounts  of  racial  representation,  hybridity,  and subaltern positionality (Bhabha, 1994; Spivak, 1988). Through this PCDA framework,  our  study  conceptualizes AI-generated  images  as  sites  where colonial visual grammars are translated, circulated, and contested, enabling us to examine how racialized meaning is produced within and across U.S. and Chinese AI systems.

## Representational Bias

A paired-samples t-test indicates that the proportion of White representation generated by GPT-4o ( M = 89.02%) is significantly higher than that in the U.S. actual labor force data ( M = 78.49%), M difference = 10.53%, t (59) = 5.62, p &lt; .001, 95% CI = [6.78%, 14.28%], Cohen's d = .726. In contrast, Llama 3 ( M = 74.79%) does not significantly differ from the U.S. actual data ( M = 78.49%), M difference = -3.69%, t (59) = -1.59, p = .117, 95% CI = [-8.34%, 0.95%], Cohen's d = -.206. Due to the lack of racial breakdown in China's national labor force statistics, which assumes a majority Han Chinese population, no paired-samples t-test is conducted for racial representation. However,  both  China-based  models  generate  a  non-trivial  proportion  of White individuals in occupational imagery. A paired-samples t-test comparing the White representation between the two China-based models indicates that Wanx2.0 ( M = 60.67%) generates a significantly higher proportion of White individuals across occupations compared to Wenxin 3.5 ( M = 20.17%), M difference = 40.50%, t (59) = 8.87, p &lt; .001, 95% CI = [31.68%, 50.32%], Cohen's d = 1.145. ## Racialized Meaning Bias

To examine racialized visual encoding across national contexts, we conduct postcolonial  critical  discourse  analysis  (PCDA)  to  qualitatively  interpret AI-generated images (Sabido, 2019). Through systematic visual reading, we identify  the  racialized,  cultural,  and  political  codes  that  structure  these images, showing how both U.S.- and China-based models reproduce familiar and different patterns of racial oppression and marginalization. Our analysis highlights three postcolonial dynamics. First, U.S.-based models draw heavily on Western racial hierarchies and visual tropes, particularly in their depiction  of  Asian,  Black,  and  White  bodies  (Brunt  &amp;  Cere,  2011).  Second, Chinese models frequently absorb and replicate these Western visual grammars, reflecting the dominance of Western data in global AI training infrastructures  (Couldry  &amp;  Mejias,  2019).  Third,  Chinese  models  selectively adapt and reconfigure these imported conventions by incorporating localized aesthetic, religious, and gendered cues, which can be understood as a 'third space' of cultural hybridity and meaning negotiation (Bhabha, 1994).

Within the images generated by U.S.-based models, long-standing racial hierarchies deeply rooted in American political history and visual culture are reproduced.  For  instance,  in  an  image  depicting  a  bodyguard  (Figure  1), Llama 3 assigns a Black man as the bodyguard and a White woman as a client.  This  division  of  labor  mirrors  well-documented  media  tendencies  to depict Black men by their physicality, stereotyping them as potentially threatening, while portraying Whites as victims, foregrounding their vulnerabilities (T. L. Dixon &amp; Linz, 2000). In several images of delivery workers generated by GPT-4o (Figure 2), Asian individuals appear wearing masks when maskwearing is not a standard occupational practice, suggesting that the model may rely  on  stereotyped  visual  associations  rooted  in  Western  media  discourses during the pandemic. ## Discussion

This study investigates racial biases in AI-generated occupational imagery by analyzing outputs from four models developed in the United States and China. Employing a multi-level and cross-national mixed-methods approach, we examine representational, positional, and racialized meaning biases. By

aligning quantitative and qualitative methods with human-AI communication  and  postcolonial  theory,  we  demonstrate  that  White  individuals  are overrepresented across most models; in multi-person scenes, White figures are more likely to occupy spatially dominant positions; and visual encoding racializes  non-White bodies through aesthetic and symbolic cues in ways that sustain a Whiteness-centered order of knowledge and power. Theoretically, our work advances a postcolonial human-AI communication framework by locating generative AI within regimes of knowledge production and racialized visibility, conceptualizing it as a sociotechnical practice that reproduces and legitimizes structural hierarchies and scientific authority.  Methodologically, we offer a scalable cross-national auditing protocol that  enables  systematic  comparison  of  model  outputs  across  diverse  geographic and institutional contexts. Practically, our findings call for urgent design, ethical governance, and institutional interventions to facilitate decolonial science communication (Ndlovu-Gatsheni, 2018) and human-centered AI (Shneiderman, 2022), thereby fostering more equitable digital futures.

Our findings on representational bias show that GPT-4o significantly overrepresents White individuals compared to actual U.S. occupational statistics, whereas Llama 3 does not. More strikingly, despite China's census containing no White representation,  both  Chinese  models  (Wanx2.0  and Wenxin  3.5) generate substantial proportions of White individuals. This empirical pattern is consistent with prior work showing that AI and large-scale corpora reflect and amplify sociocultural asymmetries embedded in their training data (Chu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023). From a postcolonial and knowledge-political perspective, such overrepresentation should not be dismissed as random statistical noise, but rather understood as the product of historically sedimented knowledge-production practices and uneven global data flows (Chakravartty et al., 2018; Quijano, 2000). Specifically, when training corpora, image datasets,  and  model  architectures  are  globally  oriented  toward  Western  visual paradigms, generative AI models tend to position Whiteness as the 'unmarked default'  (Cave  &amp;  Dihal,  2020;  Noble,  2018).  Our  cross-national  evidence extends existing findings by showing that this default travels across geopolitical contexts through transnational data infrastructures and shared modeling practices, including within China-based models (Couldry &amp; Mejias, 2019). Thus, representational bias in generative imagery indexes epistemic and symbolic hierarchies that mediate who is legible as a scientific or occupational subject in mediated publics (Chakravartty et al., 2018).

Beyond who appears in AI-generated imagery, our analysis of positional bias highlights how spatial arrangements assign racialized authority within multi-person scenes: White figures are more frequently positioned as central, foregrounded, or visually salient. This finding aligns with theories of visual

grammar and power (Foucault, 1977; Kress &amp; van Leeuwen, 2002; Z. Wang &amp; Huang, 2026) and resonates with critical scholarship demonstrating that visibility does not necessarily equate to liberation (Browne, 2015). Importantly, spatialized power is not merely a matter of placement; it carries significant consequences for perceptions of professional authority and credibility, thereby reinforcing racialized leadership norms (Delgado &amp; Stefancic, 2017). When generative AI repeatedly positions White individuals as leaders or central figures in group contexts, it reproduces and reinforces institutionalized racial hierarchies at the micro-level of visual composition, encouraging audiences to accept such hierarchies as a form of 'visual common sense' (Hall,  1997).  The  similar  tendencies  of  positional  bias  in  both  U.S.-  and China-based models underscore that colonial power is not determined solely by geographic origin but is rearticulated through global data flows (Couldry &amp; Mejias, 2019).

Our  postcolonial  critical  discourse  analysis  (PCDA)  of  visual  outputs uncovers racialized  meaning  bias:  models  do  not  merely  display  different faces, they stylistically and symbolically encode race through costume, pose, setting, and affective framing. These discursive operations reinscribe older colonial and media tropes: pathologizing Asianness through disease-linked imagery,  associating  Blackness  with  manual  labor  or  threat,  and  staging 'White-savior' configurations in charity scenes (Cole, 2012; Fanon, 1952; Hall, 1997; hooks, 1992). Such encoding practices echo arguments that algorithmic  systems  inherit  discursive  logics  embedded  in  postcolonial  power relations,  reflecting  established  Western  media  discourses  that  circulate through  transnational  data  flows  and  are  reproduced  within  non-Western models (Brunt &amp; Cere, 2011; Darling-Hammond et al., 2020; T. L. Dixon &amp; Linz, 2000; Sabido, 2019). Our cross-model qualitative comparisons additionally reveal a 'third space' hybridity where China-based models sometimes reconfigure Western visual grammars by layering domestic aesthetic cues,  religious  iconography,  and  gender  norms  (Bhabha,  1994).  However, such hybridity does not necessarily produce freedom or emancipation; rather, it  often  constitutes  a  rearticulation  within  hegemonic  power  structures (Spivak, 1988), potentially sustaining colonial hierarchies of knowledge by producing culturally legible forms of difference that avoid structural disruption (King et al., 2013; Z. Wang et al., 2023). Our analysis thus moves 'racialized meaning encoding' beyond the level of surface imagery, situating it as a site of contestation over how epistemic legitimacy and racialized visibility are  technologically  produced  and  maintained  within  a  global  postcolonial order (Bhabha, 1985; Lazar, 2014; Young, 2001).

Our results carry several practical implications for AI development, platform governance, and science communication practice. For model developers

and dataset curators, our multi-level framework suggests the need for proactive visual-diversity governance that audits datasets not only for representational balance but also for compositional and discursive dimensions (Gillespie, 2024). Training pipelines should incorporate checks for spatial and symbolic bias and include culturally diverse annotators and evaluators to surface latent patterns of racialized meaning. For science communication institutions and practitioners, our findings call for critical AI literacy and clear guidelines on AI-generated imagery, encouraging careful review of White-centric representations of expertise, diversification of prompts without essentializing identities,  and  the  integration  of  editorial  oversight  and  equity-oriented  visual resources to foreground underrepresented scientists and local forms of expertise (CPAS Inclusive Science Communication Collective, 2025). Policymakers and funding agencies concerned with responsible AI and decolonial science communication should require greater transparency about model training corpora, support cross-national auditing standards, and incentivize open documentation practices that facilitate external scrutiny (Couldry &amp; Mejias, 2019). Finally, media and public education should train citizens to recognize how generative AI can naturalize hierarchies, advancing a critical and ethical commitment  to  challenging  power  inequalities  reproduced  through  discursive means (Adorno &amp; Horkheimer, 2019; van Dijk, 1993).

This study is not without limitations. First, our operationalization of race is based on visual coding, which risks oversimplifying the fluid and intersectional nature of racial identity and may fail to capture self-identification or culturally specific understandings of race. We recognize race as socially constructed and context-dependent, and do not intend to endorse or reinforce any stereotypes,  essentialist  assumptions,  or  hierarchical  constructions  of  race. Second, this study does not account for intersecting identities such as gender, age, or ability. These dimensions are crucial for understanding how AI systems may replicate and compound multiple forms of bias. Third, due to the absence of racially disaggregated occupational statistics in China's population census, our  analysis  for  China-based  models  relies  on  comparing  Wenxin  3.5  and Wanx2.0 to each other rather than to real demographic distributions. Fourth, given the rapid development of generative AI, longitudinal research is needed to determine whether the patterns of racial bias observed here persist, evolve, or diminish as model architectures and training datasets change. Future work could adopt a more explicitly intersectional framework and incorporate multimodal outputs, including text and voice, to examine how overlapping forms of bias  transform  over  time  across  generative  AI  models.  Finally,  interviewbased, experimental, and ethnographic studies involving both users and developers may help illuminate the sociotechnical decisions and power dynamics that shape how race is encoded and reproduced in AI systems.

Selection  of  Generative  AI  Models. We select  four  prominent  generative AI models for image generation: OpenAI's GPT-4o, Meta's Llama 3, Alibaba's Wanx2.0,  and  Baidu's Wenxin  3.5. These  models  are  chosen  due  to  their widespread adoption, technological influence, and representation of leading AI development in the United States and China, the two global hubs of AI innovation.  OpenAI's  GPT-4o  is  a  state-of-the-art  text-to-image  model known for its high-quality visual outputs and extensive use in creative and scientific applications. Meta's Llama 3 represents a significant advancement in open-weights AI models, widely used for its versatility and accessibility. Alibaba's Wanx2.0 and Baidu's Wenxin 3.5 are leading China-based models that reflect the rapid growth of AI in China, offering insights into how racial biases may manifest differently across cultural and geopolitical contexts. By including models from both the U.S. and China, we aim to provide a crossnational perspective on racial biases in AI-generated images.

Selection of Occupations. To ensure a comprehensive analysis of racial biases in AI-generated images, we focus on 60 occupations that are common to both the United States and China. These occupations are selected based on up-todate labor force statistics from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and the National Bureau of Statistics of China. The inclusion of diverse occupations, ranging from high-status roles (e.g., doctors, chief executive officers) to lowstatus roles (e.g., servers, farmers), allows us to examine how racial biases intersect with occupational stereotypes and social hierarchies. By selecting occupations  with  comparable  representation  in  both  countries,  we  ensure that our findings are grounded in real-world labor dynamics and provide a meaningful  comparative  lens  for  analyzing  racial  biases  across  cultural contexts.

Image Generation and Collection. For each of the 60 occupations, we generate 40 images per model, resulting in a total of 9,600 images across the four models. Occupation-specific prompts are designed to elicit images that reflect typical workplace scenarios, ensuring consistency and comparability across models. The image generation and collection process follows a structured protocol:

- Step 1: Prompting the Model. For each occupation, we initiate a new conversation  with  the  model  and  use  a  standardized  prompt  format: 'Generate  a  picture  of  an  individual  with  the  occupation  of pilot '  or ' 生成一张职业为飞行员的人类图片 ' for U.S.-based models and China-based models, respectively. This approach ensures consistency in the input across all models and occupations.
- Step 2: Saving and Formatting Images. Each generated image is saved to a designated folder and converted to PNG format to maintain uniformity in file type and quality for subsequent analysis.
- Step  3:  Naming  and  Organizing  Images. Images  are  systematically named using a numerical identifier ranging from 0 to 39 for each occupation. This naming convention facilitates easy tracking and organization of the dataset.

Step 4: Identifying Non-Single-Person Images. During  the  collection process,  we  identify  images  depicting  two  or  more  individuals.  These images are specially marked and duplicated to create a separate dataset for analyzing multi-person interaction scenarios. This subset of data is critical for addressing the power dynamics and interactions between individuals of different racial groups in multi-person settings.

Step 5: Iterating for the Next Occupation. After completing the image collection  for  one  occupation,  we  restart  a  new  conversation  with  the model to generate images for the next occupation, repeating the process until all 60 occupations are covered.

Step 6: Identifying and Collecting Bad Cases. During the image collection process, we identify and exclude 'bad cases': images that lack basic human features  (e.g.,  frontal  faces)  or  are  otherwise  ambiguous  to  the extent  that  the  depicted  individual's  race  cannot  be  coded  as  the  race according to our codebook. These bad cases are collected in a separate folder  and  excluded  from  the  final  count  of  40  images  per  occupation. This ensures that only high-quality, analyzable images are included in the dataset.

Data Collection Team Assignment. Four authors of this study participate in the data collection process. For each occupation-model pairing, two researchers are assigned to independently collect the generated images ( n = 20) and conduct  an  initial  estimation  of  gender  distribution.  This  pairing  ensures  that each set of 40 images per occupation undergoes a minimum of two independent  assessments  during  the  collection  phase.  To  minimize  discrepancies introduced by individual model-user interaction patterns, we conduct periodic  cross-checks  comparing  gender  ratios  across  collectors  for  the  same occupation-model pairs. This verification step ensures consistency and helps identify  any  potential  sampling  bias  due  to  account-specific  variation  in model  outputs.  We  do  not  observe  any  significant  data  collection  bias throughout the process. In addition, all model accounts used for image generation are newly created prior to data collection to avoid contamination from prior chat histories, thereby reducing potential bias introduced by previous user interactions.

Data Accessibility. The full dataset, including all generated images, metadata, and coding results, is publicly available at https://github.com/wzt98/genAIsobiased. This open-access approach ensures transparency and allows other researchers to replicate or build upon our findings.

## Appendix B

## Codebook

General Coding Rules. All images are coded individually, with careful attention to the cultural context of the image to avoid oversimplification or reductive categorization. Two researchers code all images. When inconsistencies arise in race and position coding for the same image, a final label is assigned only when  coders reach a broad consensus through repeated visual assessment.

Race Coding Rules. Race is coded as a category of visual representation rather than as an indicator of biological difference or personal identity. Race coding is informed by a combination of visual characteristics and self-identification when available, recognizing that race is a complex social construct. When analyzing  observable  variations  for  visual  diversity  and  methodological transparency, we draw on established methodological frameworks such as Diversity in Faces 3 as a general reference.

Each image is coded into one of five categories: all White, all Black or African American, all Asian, all Hispanic or Latino, or mixed races. Coding focuses  on  how  individuals  are  racialized  within  the  image,  drawing  on widely recognizable media conventions and contextual cues. No single visual characteristic is treated as determinative. Coding decisions are made holistically and comparatively across images in the dataset.

Positional Bias Coding Rules. This section provides criteria for identifying the spatially dominant figure in a multi-person image that contains more than one person with clearly distinguishable races. Positional dominance is conceptualized as the allocation of spatial authority within the frame, which shows that leadership, control, and power are communicated through spatial organization and visual prominence. The evaluation follows a hierarchical process. Coders should move through the following steps in sequence until a clear coding decision can be made.

Step 1: Presence of a Serviced Individual. If the image includes servicerelated  occupations  (e.g.,  bodyguard,  server)  and  features  one  or  more individuals being served (e.g., client, customer), the serviced individual is coded as the dominant figure, regardless of positioning or behavior. If the image contains only service professionals, or if occupational roles are not related to service, proceed to the next step.

Step 2: Dominance Through Interpersonal Interaction. If the individuals  are  all  professionals  or  if  the  image  shows  interactions unrelated to service (e.g., office, meeting, peer-level professions), dominance should be assessed using the following visual cues: (a) Expression and Gaze: The person being looked at by others or who controls the visual focus is likely dominant; (b) Posture and Gesture: The person demonstrating instructive, directive,  or  commanding  actions  is  likely  dominant;  (c)  Attire  and Accessories: More formal dress or symbols of authority (e.g., tie, badge, microphone) suggest dominance; (d) Props and Background: The person handling key tools or positioned in symbolically powerful locations (e.g., podium, head seat, behind desk) is likely dominant. If the above cues are inconclusive or conflicting, proceed to the next layer of assessment.

Step  3:  Spatial  Position  and  Composition-Based Assessment. When interactional cues are ambiguous or conflicting, visual composition may help: (a) Center Position: The individual in the central visual position of the image is preferred as the dominant figure; (b) Size and Scale: If figures are symmetrically placed or equally distant from the center, the person occupying a larger portion of the image (i.e., closer to the camera) is more likely to be dominant.

Ethical Considerations and Limitations. Race is a complex social and cultural construct rather than a purely biological category. The coding in this study is informed by commonly used demographic frameworks but does not imply fixed or inherent differences between groups. Given the subjective nature of visual  identification,  this  coding  process  may  not  fully  capture  individual self-identification or the diverse ways in which race is understood across different cultural contexts.

We acknowledge that racial and ethnic identities are fluid, intersectional, and context-dependent. Where possible, self-identification should take precedence  over  external  coding.  In  addition,  this  study  does  not  endorse  or reinforce stereotypes, essentialist views, or hierarchical structures related to race. Our goal is to approach this topic with cultural sensitivity, academic rigor, and ethical responsibility.

Any limitations or inconsistencies in racial coding should be interpreted with  caution.  We  encourage  further  discussion  and  refinement  in  future research to improve the inclusivity and accuracy of the operationalization of race in social science studies.
