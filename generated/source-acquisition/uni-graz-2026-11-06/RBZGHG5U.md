## 4.1  Generative AI and AI Agents

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) technology, terms like Generative  AI,  Traditional  AI  Agents,  and  Agentic  AI  have  emerged.  Although these  terms  sound  similar,  there  are  significant  differences  in  their  operational mechanisms, objectives, and levels of autonomy.

## 4.1.1 Generative  AI

When we have questions, we ask AI instead of using a search engine. Generative AI  learns  from  vast  amounts  of  data  to  converse  naturally  like  a  human,  write poetry,  and  create  art.  It  reacts  to  user  requests,  known  as  prompts,  to  generate new  content-  such  as  text,  images,  audio,  video,  and  codethat  did  not  previ ously exist (U.S. Government Accountability Office, 2023). The primary purpose of Generative AI is content creation, and it operates only when there is user input. Programs  like  ChatGPT,  Midjourney,  and  Gemini  generate  oneoff  responses  to questions and can produce various forms of content. For instance, when asked to 'write a short poem suitable for today's weather,' it functions as a creative writing tool. However, we are now encountering a concept that goes one step further: the 'Agent.'

## 4.1.2  Traditional AI Agents

Traditional  AI  agents  operate  according  to  prestablished  rules  set  by  humans. They  function  as  a  set  of  commands:  'If  situation  A  occurs,  perform  action  B.' For example, in the context of social work eligibility, a rule might be programmed as:  if  a  household's  income  is  at  or  below  50%  of  the  median  income  (Situation A), then automatically classify them as eligible for financial assistance (Action B). Since they operate based on predefined rules or algorithms, their ability to adapt to change or set goals is limited, making them relatively simple forms of AI (Hughes et al., 2025). Due to these limitations, Traditional AI Agents are primarily used in chatbots that provide answers according to fixed scripts.

## 4.1.3  Agentic AI

In contrast,  Agentic  AI has evolved  from  Traditional  AI  Agents by using 'reasoning power' of Generative AI as its engine to achieve true autonomy. It goes beyond simply answering questions; it understands what is needed to reach a goal, creates a plan (Planning), and directly utilizes necessary tools (Tool Use) to com plete  the  execution  (Action).  Agentic  AI  is  a  system  that  uses Large  Language Models (LLMs) as  its  'brain'  to  autonomously  perform  multiple  steps  and  leverage various tools, including Generative AI (Shen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024).

This  system  possesses  a  high  level  of autonomy ,  initiating  actions  without human intervention and perceiving its environment to proactively adjust behavior. Rather than remaining in a passive state of waiting for input, it continu ously analyzes the gap between the current situation and ultimate goals, modifying its execution path accordingly. In this sense, it can be described as proactive . For example,  when  given  the  goal  of  'scheduling  a  meeting  for  next  week,'  the  AI agent independently carries out a complex sequence of tasks such as 1) searching for the optimal time slot, 2) sending emails to participants, 3) reserving a meeting room,  and  4)  drafting  preliminary  meeting  materials  to  successfully  accomplish the objective.

## 4.2  The Emergence of Agentic AI

The concept of an 'AI  Agent' is not new.  Within the discipline of computer science, an AI  agent  has  long  been  a  broad  concept  encompassing  any  intelligent  system that perceives its environment, reasons, and acts. However, the recent emergence

of the term 'Agentic AI' has ushered in a new era that is fundamentally different from the past (Russell &amp; Norvig, 2021).

While Traditional AI followed fixed rules or static models, Agentic AI independently  understands  complex  and  abstract  goals.  It  formulates  plans,  autono -mously utilizes tools such as web searches and external apps, and even engages in self-  reflection regarding its own actions (Niketan &amp; Batatia, 2025). This represents an evolution toward a level of proxy performance for human tasks, significantly surpassing the limitations of traditional AI.

The  best  way  to  understand  Agentic  AI  is  to  think  of  it  as  an  'autonomous personal assistant' that achieves goals. Imagine the goal of planning an overseas vacation. Agentic AI divides the goal into steps and determines their sequence. It autonomously plans flights, accommodations, and itineraries, and then selects and uses the necessary external tools-  such as web browsers, calendars, and map apps. If the results do not align with the goal, it evaluates itself and modifies the plan. It is, in essence, the portrait of a perfect assistant.

Why has all of this only recently become possible? The core driver is the rapid advancement of LLMs. LLMs are massive AI models designed to understand, gen erate, and reason through human language by learning from vast amounts of text data.  They  serve  as  a  humanlevel  'brain'  for  Agentic  AI  ( Naidu &amp; El-   Gayar,  2025).

First, LLMs have an immense scale in terms of parameters. This vast scale allows them to deeply learn complex patterns and contexts of language, creating richer and more consistent sentences. Their operating principle is also fascinating; they predict and generate the word with the highest probability of following a given sentence or word. For example, if the subject is 'The sky,' the model determines that 'blue' has a higher probability of following than 'delicious' or 'yellow' to complete the sen tence. Through this probabilistic approach, it understands complex human questions and commands, generating natural responses like those of a human.

Next is the capacity for reasoning , which refers to the ability of an LLM-  based agent  to  achieve  a  complex  goal  and  process  it  logically.  While  Traditional  AI Agents relied primarily on predefined rules or limited data, LLM-  based Agentic AI possesses human-  level  language-  understanding,  reasoning,  and  planning  capabil ities  (Shen  et  al.,  2024 ).  In  other  words,  the  LLMbased Agentic AI  can  analyze complex situations, infer the optimal steps to achieve a goal, and express and execute that plan in natural language.

We can see  how  innovative  the  reasoning  and planning capabilities  of  LLMbased Agentic AI are through the following example: 'ordering pizza for dinner.' If given the goal 'I need to order pizza for dinner with friends tonight, and one of them  is  a  vegetarian,'  the  LLM  understands  the  complex  context  (friends,  vege -tarian, dinner) like a human and infers 'pizza with vegetarian options' as the core condition. The LLM-  based Agentic AI then independently determines the optimal sequence (Step 1: Use a web search tool to find nearby restaurants. Step 2: Filter reviews  of  vegetarian  options.  Step  3:  Select  the  place  with  the  highest  rating. Step 4: Launch a delivery app tool to proceed with the order). Finally, LLM-  based Agentic AI completes the order at Restaurant A, which offers vegetarian options. This example confirms that an LLM agent understands the situation (reasoning),

What  mechanisms  allow  for  these  autonomous,  lifelike  assistant  behaviors? While  the  LLM  serves  as  the  'brain,'  it  is  the  organic  integration  of  additional components-  Memory and Action-  that empowers Agentic AI.

Task  Decomposition and Chain-  of-  Thought . Instead of providing an immediate answer, the LLM explains its logical thought process step-  by-  step. Through this process, the LLM independently breaks down complex goals into executable sub-   plans and determines their sequence ( Shu et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024). For instance, if commanded to 'find and order a birthday gift my friend would like,' the AI agent does not act immediately but follows these steps: 1) Identify information regarding the friend's preferences. 2) Use tools like SNS search or past conversation memory to understand those preferences. 3) Once preferences are identified, research candidates, prices, and ratings. 4) Select the final gift and place an order at the lowest price. Continuous  Memory  and  Learning. Agentic  AI  remembers  its  experiences  and goals,  constantly  learning  and  evolving.  This  memory  prevents  the  agent  from repeating the same mistakes and enables it to work in a way that better fits the user over time (Leroux et al., 2024).

Coordinated Autonomy. While typical chatbots lack the ability to handle complex requests like 'planning a trip' on their own, Agentic AI excels with collaboration. When  multiple  AIs  work  together,  it  coordinates  and  integrates  their  actions  to produce a perfect result (Shu et al., 2024; Gabriel et al., 2024). For example, con  sider  the  goal  of  'providing  the  most  effective  customized  support  for  a  lowincome family facing financial difficulties.' Three specialized agents-  Financial Support, Health Care, and Housing Environment-  might experience conflict while emphasizing their individual goals. At this point, the central coordination module derives an optimized support plan based on the overall objective. It might, for instance, set a compromise that secures financial and housing support while changing the health care component from a general hospital visit to a home-  visit service .

In  summary, Agentic AI  is  a  system  that  autonomously  sets  goals,  collaborates with multiple agents to solve complex problems, and continuously learns and adapts. This signifies the evolution of  AI into a system with a level of 'intent' that can achieve goals without human intervention. In the future, AI will go far beyond being a simple assistant to become a core decision-  making entity (McNamara &amp; Marpu, 2025).

## 4.3  What Is Retrieval-  Augmented Generation (RAG)?

## Box 4.1  Beyond the Search Bar: Reclaiming Social Work with RAG AI

Instead of  Steve  spending  hours  manually  searching  for  details,  the  assistant  traverses  a dedicated database of official documents to retrieve the most relevant and up-  todate information. It then synthesizes this data into clear, precise answers regarding grant  eligibility  and  required  documentation.  This  tool  not  only  saves  time  but also  ensures  that  his  advice  is  reliable,  thereby  fostering  deeper  trust  within  the community.

## 4.3.1  Fundamental Limitations of LLMs and Breakthrough

LLMs  learn  from  vast  amounts  of  data,  but  this  data  is  inevitably  'frozen'  at  a specific point  in  time.  For  example,  if  a  model's  training  ended  in  early  2023, the  model  cannot  know  about  the  latest  news  or  events  that  occurred  afterward. Because of this, LLMs face the following challenges:

- Knowledge Cut-  off :  LLMs  cannot  answer  questions  about  the  latest  information, real-  time data, or rapidly changing facts that emerged after their training ended (Oche et al., 2025).
- Hallucination: When the exact information is missing from the training data, the model may respond with 'hallucinations'-  convincing but entirely false information (Lewis et al., 2020).
- Limited  Internal  Knowledge : LLMs  cannot reason about or answer questions regarding private information not included in the training data, such as company's internal documents, personal emails, or confidential industry data (Lewis et al., 2020).

To overcome these limitations, RAG was developed. RAG is a technology that combines  'searching'  and  'generating.'  It  dramatically  improves  the  accuracy and reliability of AI by searching for relevant information in external databases or documents and using it as a reference (Oche et al., 2025).

Consider LLMs as a very smart student taking an exam. Without RAG, the stu dent must rely solely on their memory. With RAG, the student is allowed to 'look at  an  open  textbook'  before  answering.  Before  generating  a  response,  the  system retrieves  ( Retrieval )  the  most  relevant,  upto-  date  information  from  an  external knowledge base and provides  it  to  LLMs  to  strengthen  ( Augment ) their final  answer.

## 4.3.2 Understanding  How  RAG  Works

RAG operates through three core stages, essentially instructing LLM to 'find the evidence for the answer' from external sources.

- Retrieval: When a user enters a query, the RAG system quickly searches external knowledge repositories-  such as internal corporate documents, the latest news articles, or web data-  to find the documents or passages most relevant to the question (Oche et al., 2025).
- Augmentation: The retrieved pieces of information (the retrieved knowledge) are added to the LLM's input prompt along with the user's original question. This allows  the  LLM  to  generate  an  answer  based  on  the  'context'  of  upto-  date knowledge, rather than relying solely on its memory (Oche et al., 2025).
- Generation: The  LLM combines its internal knowledge with the newly provided external information to generate an accurate and wellevidenced response (Lewis et al., 2020).

This RAG mechanism is  particularly  useful  in  the  United  States,  where  wel -fare guidelines differ by state and change frequently. For example, suppose a social worker  in  Texas  asks,  'What  is  the  income  eligibility  for  Emergency  Assistance (EA) in 2026?'

- Retrieval : The RAG system immediately finds the '2026 Texas Health and Human Services Commission (HHSC) Policy Handbook' from the latest database.
- Augmentation: The  system  combines  the  specific  income  limits  and  asset requirements for 2026 with the social worker's question and feeds this data into the LLMs.
- Generation: Instead  of  providing  generalized  or  outdated  information,  the  LLM uses the retrieved Texas-  specific guidelines to generate answers, such as 'For 2026,  the  household  income  must  not  exceed  the  Federal  Poverty  Level  by more  than  X  percent.'  Consequently,  the  RAGbased  system  serves  as  a  reli -able  assistant,  helping  practitioners  navigate  complex  and  localized  welfare regulations without administrative errors.

For clients inquiring about the income threshold for a public assistance program, standard LLMs might provide figures that were accurate a year ago but have since changed. Even worse, it might guide a user toward a discontinued support system as if it were still active. RAG  solves  these  problems  by  updating  a  vector  database  in  real  time  with the  latest  legislation,  government  notices,  and  guidelines  from  national  and  local authorities.  By  referencing  the  full  text  of  current  notices,  the  AI  can  provide  a response such as: 'As of 2026, the maximum annual allowable income for a threeperson  household  is  $23,000,  which  is  a  slight  increase  from  last  year.'  Such  an answer is not only timely and accurate but is also backed by verifiable sources, ensuring that the AI is grounded in facts rather than hallucinations.

## 4.4.2  Ensuring Consistency in Legal Interpretation

Another type of hallucination occurs when LLMs attempt to interpret complex cases requiring professional social work knowledge. For instance, a social worker might ask: 'What are the specific eligibility criteria and asset calculation rules for a child with special needs when the parents apply for the Disability Child Allowance?'

Because these criteria often involve intricate legal nuancessuch as distinguishing between recognized assets and exempted property or applying spe cific  income deduction rates for disability-  related expenses-  a standard LLM might incorrectly combine laws, bring in irrelevant regulations, or provide interpretations from similar but unrelated systems. This can lead to inconsistent guidance among practitioners or even regulatory violations (Pipitpne &amp; Alami, 2024).

RAG  mitigates  these  risks  by  searching  for  actual  social  workrelated  cases and  administrative  precedents.  Instead  of  relying  on  speculative  or  generalized answers, RAG utilizes official policies, social work case histories, and complex counseling  records  stored  in  the  database.  Consequently,  RAG  can  provide  con -sistent, reliable, and legally sound guidance that reflects actual practice rather than theoretical assumptions .

## 4.4.3  Enhancing Personalized Accessibility for Users

Beyond  professional  support,  RAG  empowers  clients  by  improving  accessibility  to tailored  information.  Potential  clients  often  struggle  to  navigate  the  complexities  of

public welfare systems, potentially leaving them unaware of available programs. RAG greatly simplifies this process. For example, if a user asks, 'Am I eligible to receive Supplemental Nutrition  Assistance Program in  Arizona?' or 'can I apply for Medicaid in Georgia?' a traditional chatbot might provide vague or incomplete answers.

In contrast, RAG identifies the most relevant legal provisions, from the specific state's current guidelines, feeds the data into the LLMs, and allows the models to generate a clear, personalized response based on the user's specific situation. This response not only explains the complex eligibility requirements in simple terms but also  guides  the  user  through  the  application  procedures,  document  requirements, and  deadlines.  Most  importantly,  LLMs  powered  by  RAG  cite  the  legal  clauses supporting  advice,  enhancing  credibility  and  trust.  Since  public  welfare  systems are frequently revised, RAG ensures that the AI remains current by reflecting the latest announcements and policy changes in the database (Gupta et al., 2024).

In summary, RAG represents a significant leap forward in integrating AI into social  work  practice.  It  enhances  the  accuracy,  consistency,  and  accessibility  of information  for  both  professionals  and  the  public,  ensuring  that  social  work  ser -vices remain fair, efficient, and aligned with the latest standards.

## 4.4.4  Challenges and Ethical Considerations

Despite being a powerful innovation, RAG has some practical operational concerns to consider, including costs, technical hurdles, and ethical issues. The necessity of building and maintaining a retrieval system, potential time delays due to the add -itional search step, and increased costs are significant factors. Furthermore, when providing  personalized  information,  ethical  and  legal  risks  such  as  security  and privacy breaches must be carefully managed (Gupta et al., 2024). While RAG is a formidable tool for  expanding  the  knowledge  capabilities  of AI,  its  effective  and safe  use  requires  continuous  investment,  performance  optimization,  and  rigorous security management.

## 4.5.1  Machine Learning and Deep Learning

## 4.5.1.1  Machine Learning (ML)

ML is the heart of the field of AI. It is a method where a system 'self-  discovers' and learns rules through vast amounts of example data. Instead of following explicitly  programmed  instructions,  this  technology  enables  computers  to  learn  from

experience and make predictions or decisions. Just as humans accumulate know -ledge and solve new problems through experience, ML aims to learn from data and apply that learning to solve novel challenges (Du et al., 2024).

The components of ML include the data used as learning material, a model (the mathematical structure that learns patterns from that data), and an algorithm (the method  used  to  train  the  model).  Evaluation  is  the  process  of  testing  the  trained model's accuracy using metrics like error rates.

ML analyzes data and finds rules on its own. For example, when creating a spam filter, instead of setting rules like 'if words such as 'Investment,' 'Urgent,' or  'Final  Notice'  exist,  then  classify  as  spam,'  the  system  is  trained  to  identify characteristics  of  spam  from  thousands  of  legitimate  and  spam  emails.  Through training  on  images  of  apples  and  oranges,  ML  can  predict  that  a  'round,  orange fruit photo' has a 95% probability of being an orange.

In  social  work,  ML  plays  a  crucial  role  in  enhancing  policy  efficiency  and fairness and proactively identifying high-   risk  groups  ( Song,  2025).  In  solitary  death and crisis household prediction systems, algorithms are required to predict scientific causal relationships. ML collects vast information (e.g., gender, age, income, assets,  occupation,  debt)  from  past  cases  to  analyze  patterns  and  causalities.  By discovering  these patterns, the system  allows  practitioners to intervene before a  crisis  occurs.  ML  helps  social  workers  identify  hidden  danger  signals  without manually checking every data point, ensuring resources are focused on those in the most urgent need.

## 4.5.1.2  Deep Learning (DL)

DL is a subfield of ML that uses artificial neural networks, specifically deep neural networks with multiple layers. It can be compared to a child's brain growing more complex  and  sophisticated;  as  thinking  skills  develop,  the  ability  to  distinguish objects in photos or interpret human emotions improves, eventually evolving into an adult capable of solving abstract and complex problems (Song, 2025).

While DL is a type of ML, it differs significantly in  how  it  solves  complex problems. The key difference lies in 'Feature Extraction.' While ML often relies on human-  defined rules, or features, DL allows the AI to discover and understand features on its own, going beyond the given data (Ruano-  Ordás,  2024 ).

Let's use the example of identifying a cat:

- In  ML : An  engineer  must  first  define  a  cat's  features,  such  as  'pointed  ears,' 'whiskers,'  and  'four  legs.'  The  model  then  calculates  the  probability  of  the image being a cat based on these predefined traits. If the cat is lying down and its ears aren't visible, the ML model might fail because it relies strictly on those human-  set  rules.
- In DL: The model uses deep neural networks to extract features itself. The initial layers  learn  basic  patterns  like  lines  and  edges,  while  deeper  layers  combine these into an 'abstract concept of a cat.' Even if the cat is in an unusual pose or its ears are hidden, DL captures the complex, holistic characteristics to identify it accurately.

This  characteristic  of  DL  is  extremely  useful  in  social  work.  While  ML  is suited for qualifying beneficiaries based on clear criteria, DL is used for analyzing abstract and complex signals, such as counseling records for suicide prevention. AI can independently learn  and  detect  subtle  patterns  of  'hopelessness,'  'isola -tion,'  or  'negative  outlooks'  in  text  without  humans  having  to  define specific keywords.

DL pushes the boundaries of traditional ML in social work by analyzing unstruc -tured data. It is most active in early detection of safety and health anomalies, such as  detecting  falls,  dementia  signs,  or  depression.  For  instance,  AIbased  Action Recognition  models  in  nursing  homes  or  homes  of  older  adults  living  alone  can analyze CCTV footage. When someone collapses or shows no activity for a long time, the system immediately triggers an emergency alarm.

Furthermore, in  child  social  work  centers  and  daycares,  DL  can  detect  threats to child safety. It analyzes CCTV to identify physical violence (hitting, pushing), suspected abuse (prolonged neglect, coercive restraint), or signs of bullying among children. DL's advantage is its ability to capture subtle signals that humans might miss  by  learning  all  the  various  documented  forms  of  violent  behavior  (Song,  2025). Smart daycare safety systems send real-  time alerts to supervisors and store footage for evidence and preventive education. Additionally, DL-   based Face Recognition is highly effective in locating missing people. DL plays a decisive role in solving high-  dimensional problems-  language, images, and complex situations-  where it is difficult for humans to define specific features.

NLP allows computers to 'listen,' 'understand,' and 'respond to or summarize' the languages or sentences we use in our daily lives.

As a subfield of AI that leverages ML to enable computers to comprehend and process human language, NLP research has played a pivotal role in ushering in the era  of  Generative  AI,  ranging  from  the  communication  capabilities  of  LLMs  to image generation models that understand user prompts. NLP has already become a  staple  in  many  people's  daily  lives,  powering  search  engines,  enabling  voice commands for customer service chatbots, and operating voiceactivated GPS systems and digital assistants like Amazon's Alexa, Apple's Siri, and Microsoft's CoPilot.

NLP is broadly classified into five core functions (Jegan &amp; Henrich, 2025).

- Language Understanding: It breaks down sentences into word units and analyzes grammar to identify the user's underlying intent.
- Language Generation: Based on data, it creates natural, humanlike  sentences  to draft consultation responses or reports.
- Language Transformation: It enhances information accessibility and removes language  barriers  through  translation  and  Speechto-  Text/  Text-  to-  Speech (STT/ TTS) technologies.
- Analysis and Classification: It  detects  negative  sentiments  in  reviews  or  consultation logs and automatically filters out harmful content.
- Dialogue Processing: It  enables  interactive  communicationsuch  as  answering questions  or  providing  psychological  supportby  remembering  the  context of previous conversations. This is the core technology behind chatbots and AI assistants (Shao, 2024).

Together, these five functions form the foundation upon which NLP supports human-  centered AI  applications.  These  technologies  serve  as  a  fundamental  tool for social workers, enabling them to analyze community needs more precisely and design services that are both ethical and efficient.

How, then, are these NLP functions being utilized in social work?

- Information  Extraction  from  Unstructured  Text : NLP  automatically  identifies and extracts specific types of information from unstructured text. A prominent example  is Mental  RoBERTa ,  an  LLM  specifically designed  and  trained  to analyze text related to mental health. The  AI scans the text (like client messages, notes,  or  online  posts)  and  flags  patterns  that  may  indicate  high  anxiety  or depressive  language. ## 4.8.4.1  Instructions:

- 1  Choose Your Document.

- 2 Set Up Your Environment.
- Use any tools of your choice (e.g., Python, LangChain or Haystack, FAISS or Chroma, Streamlit or Gradio).

- Use an LLM (e.g., GPT-  5, Gemini) to generate answers using both the query and retrieved content.
- Integrate retrieval and generation into a full RAG pipeline.
- 6 Design the Chat Interface.
- Use Streamlit or Gradio to build a simple web-  based chatbot.
- Allow users to input questions and receive answers with source references.
- 7 Test and Evaluate.
- Try sample questions like:
- o 'Who qualifies for housing support?'
- o 'What documents are needed for disability benefits?'
- Evaluate your chatbot based on:
- o Accuracy of answers.
