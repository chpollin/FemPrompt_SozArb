## Abstract

Purpose: This study investigates the inter-rater reliability between human experts (a forensic psychologist and a social worker) and a large language model (LLM) in the assessment of child sexual abuse statements. The research aims to explore the potential, limitations, and consistency of this class of AI as an evaluation tool within the framework of Criteria-Based Content Analysis (CBCA), a widely used method for assessing statement credibility. Materials and methods: Sixty-five anonymized transcripts of forensic interviews with child sexual abuse victims (N = 65) were independently evaluated by three raters: a forensic psychologist, a social worker, and a large language model (ChatGPT, GPT-4o Plus). Each statement was coded using the 19-item CBCA framework. Inter-rater reliability was analyzed using Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), Cohen’s Kappa (κ), and other agreement statistics to compare the judgments between the human-human pairing and the human-AI pairings. Results: A high degree of inter-rater reliability was found between the two human experts, with the majority of criteria showing “good” to “excellent” agreement (15 of 19 criteria with ICC > .75). In stark contrast, a dramatic and significant decrease in reliability was observed when the AI model’s evaluations were compared with those of the human experts. The AI demonstrated systematic disagreement on criteria requiring nuanced, contextual judgment, with reliability coefficients frequently falling into “poor” or negative ranges (e.g. ICC = -.106 for “Logical structure”), indicating its evaluation logic fundamentally differs from expert reasoning. Discussion: The findings reveal a profound gap between the nuanced, contextual reasoning of human experts and the pattern-recognition capabilities of the LLM tested. The study concludes that this type of AI, in its current, prompt-engineered form, cannot reliably replicate expert judgment in the complex task of credibility assessment. While not a viable autonomous evaluator, it may hold potential as a “cognitive assistant” to support expert workflows. The assessment of child testimony credibility remains a task that deeply requires professional judgment and appears far beyond the current capabilities of such generative AI models.

Keywords: Criteria-Based Content Analysis (CBCA); artificial intelligence; inter-rater reliability; child sexual abuse; statement credibility; forensic social work

## Introduction

In legal proceedings, one of the fundamental elements influencing judicial decisions is the credibility of witness statements, particularly in cases with limited evidence, such as child sexual  abuse  and  domestic  violence  (Griesel  &amp;  Yuille,  2007;  Herman,  2005).  This  reality places a substantial professional and ethical responsibility on professionals working in the fields  of  child  protection  and  forensic  social  work.  According  to  estimates  by  Hans  and Vidmar (2013), testimony constitutes the most heavily weighted evidence in approximately 85%  of  legal  cases.  This  underscores  the  critical  importance  of  testimony  assessment  in forensic decision-making, a reality that is even more pronounced in cases of child sexual abuse.

Globally, approximately 17% of girls report experiencing sexual abuse during childhood, with rates around 11% in Asia and China, and varying between 10% and 20% in different European studies (Barth et al., 2013; de Haas et al., 2012; Pan et al., 2021). In Turkey, official data from 2023 indicate that 25,685 children were reported to law enforcement agencies as victims  of  sexual  offenses,  and  these  children  were  subsequently  required  to  provide statements regarding their traumatic experiences (TÜİK, 2024). In such cases, the frequent lack  or  complete  absence of physical evidence regarding the alleged crime often leads to cases hinging on a 'statement-versus-statement' dilemma between the child's account and the suspect's (Steller &amp; Boychuk, 1992). Since false positive (accepting a fabricated claim as true)  or  false  negative  (rejecting  a  genuine  claim)  decisions  can  have  traumatic  consequences for the child, the accused, and other involved parties, a careful assessment of the child's statement for consistency and credibility is imperative.

While  various  terms  have  been  used  to  describe  the  nature  of  statements,  the  most frequently referenced concept is 'credibility.' Whereas some authors define credibility as the statement's truthfulness and correspondence to facts (accuracy), in this article, 'credibility'  is  used  to  mean  the  believability  of  a  statement,  independent  of  its  historical accuracy (Hauch et al., 2017). Particularly in children's forensic allegations, the credibility of a victim's claims does not necessarily mean that every detail of the statement is entirely accurate (Ceci et al., 2007). This is because children may provide statements that do not fully  align  with  reality  due  to  factors  such  as  suggestibility,  developmental  limitations  in comprehension, or inadequate narrative skills (Pezdek et al., 2004). Understanding these dynamics  is  a  core  professional  competency  for  social  workers,  who  are  tasked  with applying  trauma-informed  and  developmentally  sensitive  interviewing  techniques. Therefore,  this  complexity  underscores  the  imperative  for  social  work  professionals  to move beyond intuitive assessments and to evaluate statement credibility using evidencebased, objective, and valid methods. This study addresses this challenge by investigating the potential and limitations of using a sophisticated artificial intelligence (AI) model, specifically a large language model (ChatGPT), as a tool for credibility assessment, comparing its judgments directly with those of human experts.

## The CBCA framework for credibility assessment

Among  the  various  tools  developed  to  assess  statement  credibility,  Criteria-Based Content Analysis  (CBCA) is  one  of  the  most  widely  accepted  and  empirically  studied methods,  particularly  in  cases  of  alleged  child  sexual  abuse  (Steller  &amp;  Koehnken,

<!-- image -->

1989).  Rather  than  functioning  as  a  standalone  'lie  detector,'  CBCA  is  the  analytical core  of  a  broader,  more  integrative  process  known  as  Statement  Validity  Assessment (SVA)  (Köhnken  et  al.,  2015).  The  SVA  framework  holistically  evaluates  a  testimony by  systematically  examining  case  file  documentation,  the  conditions  of  the  semistructured  interview,  and  a  final  validity  checklist  to  consider  contextual  factors, with  the  CBCA  providing  the  central  content  analysis  (Volbert  &amp;  Steller,  2014).  The theoretical  foundation  of  CBCA  is  the  'Undeutsch  Hypothesis,'  which  posits  that narratives  based  on  genuine  personal  experiences  are  qualitatively  and  quantitatively richer  and  more  complex  than  fabricated  accounts  (Amado  et  al.,  2015;  Undeutsch, 1967).

To  operationalize  this  hypothesis,  the  CBCA  method  involves  coding  an  interview transcript  for  the  presence  of  19  specific  criteria  standardized  by  Steller  and  Koehnken (1989). These criteria are grouped into several categories, assessing General Characteristics such  as  logical  structure  and  unstructured  production;  Specific  Contents  like  contextual embedding, reproduction of conversations, and unexpected complications; and Motivation-Related Contents, which include markers like spontaneous corrections, admitting lack of memory, or self-deprecation. The guiding principle is that the presence of these criteria supports a statement's credibility, but their absence does not automatically signify deception, as narrative quality can be influenced by a child's age, trauma, or developmental stage (Manzanero et al., 2019; Oberlader et al., 2016).

Despite its widespread use and discriminative power (Arce et al., 2015; Vrij, 2008), the application of CBCA is not without significant challenges, with inter-rater reliability (IRR) being a central and persistent concern. The user-dependent nature of the method means that  its  objectivity  hinges  on  the  extent  to  which  different  evaluators  can  reach  similar conclusions.  While  studies  often  report  satisfactory  IRR  for  the  total  CBCA  score,  this overall consistency frequently masks poor agreement on individual criteria (Niveau et al., 2015;  Welle  et  al.,  2016).  Research  has  consistently  shown  that  criteria  requiring  a  high degree of subjective interpretation - such as 'unusual details' or 'details characteristic of the  offense'  -  are  particularly  prone  to  low  rater  agreement.  This  problem  is  further compounded in multidisciplinary settings, where professionals from different backgrounds struggle  to  apply  the  criteria  uniformly  (Bilginer  et  al.,  2021).  These  persistent  reliability issues highlight a critical need to either refine the tool itself or to explore novel approaches, such  as  the  use  of  AI,  that  could  potentially  augment  human  judgment  and  enhance consistency in forensic assessments.

Although  a  valuable  tool,  Criteria-Based  Content  Analysis  (CBCA)  has  significant limitations.  One  of  the  most  fundamental  issues  is  the  method's  positive  bias;  while  the presence  of  CBCA  criteria  supports  the  claim  that  a  statement  is  based  on  an  actual experience,  their  absence  cannot  be  considered  proof  of  fabrication  (Volbert  &amp;  Steller, 2014). Therefore, CBCA cannot be used in isolation as a 'lie detector.' The user-dependent nature of the method makes inter-rater reliability (IRR) a critical issue. The extent to which different  raters  reach  similar  conclusions  when  analyzing  the  same  statement  is a  prerequisite  for  the  method's  objectivity  and  its  admissibility  in  forensic  settings. However,  empirical  studies  in  this  area  are  limited  and  present  contradictory  findings. For instance, studies by Anson et al. (1993) and Raskin and Esplin (1991) have shown that while  the  overall  IRR  was  high  in  samples  of  child  sexual  abuse  statements,  significant variation existed among raters on individual CBCA criteria.

<!-- image -->

Consistent  with  international  literature,  many  studies  report  a  high  IRR  for  the  total CBCA score while revealing that the reliability of individual criteria is highly variable and often unacceptably low. In a study of 95 forensic cases in Switzerland by Niveau et al. (2015), despite a high reliability for the total score (Kendall's W = 0.84), reliability was extremely low  for  critical  indicators  such  as  'details  characteristic  of  the  offense'  (W = .43)  and 'unusual details'  (W = .40).  Similarly,  Welle  et  al.  (2016)  reported  a  satisfactory  IRR  for the total score (ICC = .76) but emphasized that inter-rater consistency does not necessarily translate into successful differentiation between truthful and fabricated accounts. A study in Turkey by Bilginer et al. (2021) also found a strong IRR for the total score (ICC = .79) while identifying weak reliability for criteria known to be problematic, such as 'admitting lack of memory' and 'details characteristic  of  the  offense.'  The  most  significant  finding  of  this study was the inability of experts from different disciplines, such as child psychiatrists and psychologists, to reach consistent agreement on most criteria. This indicates that statement credibility cannot be assessed solely through CBCA and that standardizing the method in multidisciplinary forensic practices presents significant challenges.

## The role of artificial intelligence in forensic evaluations

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have made groundbreaking advancements across various fields such as medicine, finance, and engineering, and are increasingly recognized  as  supportive  tools  in  decision-making  processes  (Russell  et  al.,  2021;  Singer et al., 2023). Subfields of AI, including natural language processing, pattern recognition, and machine  learning,  hold  significant  potential  for  analyzing  large  volumes  of  data  and conducting complex evaluations. Although interest in AI applications has grown in forensic sciences  and  psychology,  its  use  in  sensitive  areas  such  as  the  content  analysis  of  victim statements remains novel and exploratory (Galante et al., 2023; Ketsekioulafis et al., 2024; Tynan,  2024).  While  earlier  NLP  models  focused  on  linguistic  feature  analysis  to  detect deception,  the  recent  rise  of  Large  Language  Models  (LLMs)  offers  a  more  nuanced approach, capable of understanding context and complex narrative structures.

The unique  contribution  of  this  study  lies  in  incorporating  an  advanced  model  from a  subset  of  NLP  known  as  Large  Language  Models  (LLMs)  - specifically,  ChatGPT  -  as a rater alongside traditional human evaluators (a forensic psychologist and a social worker) in  the  Criteria-Based  Content  Analysis  (CBCA)  of  statements  given  by  child  victims  of sexual abuse. Unlike earlier rule-based or statistical NLP models that often struggled with contextual understanding, LLMs offer the potential to produce more nuanced, consistent, and rapid evaluations based on predefined criteria (Boetto, 2025). Whether this potential can help resolve the consistency issues often observed among human raters, or at least add a  standardized  dimension  to  the  evaluation  process,  constitutes  a  significant  research question (Lehtiniemi, 2024). However, this potential must be weighed against significant risks, including model biases, a lack of transparency in their decision-making processes, and their inability to process non-verbal cues crucial to human judgment.

While  our  study  focuses  specifically  on  statement  credibility,  it  is  situated  within a broader context where AI's role in forensic science is rapidly expanding and diversifying. For instance, beyond the analysis of physical evidence like ballistic markings or crime scene reconstruction (Piraianu et al., 2023), AI is making significant inroads into highly interpretive  domains.  In  forensic  mental  health,  machine  learning  models  are  now  being

<!-- image -->

explored to assess the risk of reoffending, to profile offender behavior from case files, and to analyze  speech  patterns  for  signs  of  psychological  distress  or  deception  (Olawade  et  al., 2025). Similarly, in postmortem examinations, AI is used not only to estimate the time of death from biochemical markers but also to assist in 'virtual autopsies' by analyzing CT and MRI scans to identify internal injuries (Piraianu et al., 2023).

These diverse applications underscore a common trend: AI is increasingly positioned not as a replacement for the human expert, but as a powerful cognitive tool to augment their capabilities.  It  excels  at  processing  vast,  complex  datasets  with  speed  and  consistency, potentially mitigating human factors like fatigue or subjective bias. However, this potential is tempered by significant and recurring challenges that are central to the debate across all forensic  applications.  As  noted  by  both  Piraianu  et  al.  (2023)  and  other  comprehensive reviews  (Olawade  et  al.,  2025),  fundamental  issues  related  to  data  privacy,  algorithmic transparency  (the  'black  box'  problem),  and  the  risk  of  perpetuating  systemic  biases found  in  historical  data  remain  paramount  (Filippis  &amp;  Foysal,  2024).  Ultimately,  these discussions consistently circle back to the irreplaceable role of human judgment in interpreting AI-generated outputs and making the final, context-aware decision.

## The current study

This study is the first of its kind in Turkey and represents a pioneering inquiry into the role and  reliability  of  AI  in  the  evaluation  of  children's  forensic  statements,  especially  through comparative analysis with human  raters from different professional backgrounds. Investigating inter-rater reliability (IRR) among human experts from distinct disciplines and an AI model will offer new insights into the robustness of the CBCA method, as well as the potential applications and challenges of AI integration into forensic psychology practices.

The  primary  aim  of  this  study  is  to  examine  and  compare  the  reliability  (inter-rater reliability - IRR) of assessments made by human experts from different disciplines (a forensic psychologist and a social worker) and an advanced AI model (ChatGPT) in the application of CBCA to statements made by child victims of sexual abuse. The study aims to uncover the potential role, consistency, and limitations of AI as a solution or support mechanism to the consistency problems often seen among human raters in the application of CBCA. In doing so,  the  study  serves  as  a  pioneering  exploration  of  the  integration  of  AI  into  complex, interpretive assessment processes in forensic psychology. This research seeks to address the following questions: (1) What is the level of inter-rater reliability between human evaluators from different professions (forensic psychologist and social worker) in applying the CBCA method to statements from child victims of sexual abuse? (2) What is the level of inter-rater reliability between the AI model (ChatGPT) and the human evaluators (forensic psychologist and  social  worker)  in  the  same  assessments?  (3)  In  comparison  with  human  experts,  on which specific CBCA criteria does the AI model demonstrate higher or lower consistency?

## Method

## Research design

This  study  employs  a  quantitative  research  design  aimed  at  examining  the  inter-rater reliability  between  evaluators  from  different  disciplines  (a  forensic  psychologist  and

<!-- image -->

a  social  worker)  and  an  artificial  intelligence  model  (ChatGPT)  in  the  application  of Criteria-Based  Content  Analysis  (CBCA)  to  statements  made  by  child  victims  of  sexual abuse.  This  research  was  approved  by  theRecep  Tayyip  Erdoğan  University  Social  and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (Approval Date: 12/06/2024, Protocol No: 2024/260), and necessary permissions were obtained from the Ministry of Justice (Permit No: 20241139).  All  procedures  involving  human  participants  were  in  accordance  with  the  ethical standards  of  the  institutional  and/or  national  research  committee  and  with  the  1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

## Study sample

The  study  sample  was  drawn  from  anonymized  case  files  of  child  victims  whosw statemants  were  recorded  by  forensic  interviewers  between  2019  and  2024.  The  data originates  from  judical  interviews  units  within  a  province  in  the  Black  Sea  region  of Turkey.  Initially,  files  from  a  total  of  95  children  were  reviewed.  The  final  sample  was determined  after  applying  several  exclusion  criteria.  We  excluded  cases  involving  children diagnosed with intellectual disabilities or psychiatric conditions that could significantly  affect  the  evaluation  process,  as  well  as  cases  where  children  made  substantial changes to their statements during investigation or prosecution. Additionally, interview recordings  with  insufficient  audio  or  video  quality  for  analysis  were  also  excluded. Based  on  these  criteria,  30  cases  were  excluded,  resulting  in  a  final  sample  of  65  child statements included in the study. The sample comprises 48 girls and 17 boys, with ages ranging from 7 to 17 ( M = 14.6, SD = 2.37).

## Data collection process

## Interview and transcription

The forensic interviews, conducted in designated interview rooms and recorded on video, were first  transcribed  by  authorized  personnel.  Subsequently,  the  transcripts  were  anonymized  by  the  researcher  in  accordance  with  ethical  guidelines.  All  identifying  personal information  (e.g.,  names,  addresses,  school  names)  was  removed  or  encoded  to  ensure confidentiality. Data were used solely for research purposes and securely stored. The human evaluators  signed  confidentiality  agreements  before  beginning  the  scoring  process,  confirming their commitment to the ethical use of the data.

## Raters and evaluation framework

Three raters independently evaluated the statements using the CBCA framework. The first rater was a forensic psychologist specialized in working with children and adolescents and trained in the theoretical and practical applications of CBCA. The second rater was a social worker engaged in forensic social work, with experience in interviewing child protection and  sexual  abuse  victims  and  training  in  CBCA  methodology.  The  third  rater  was  an artificial  intelligence  model,  specifically  an  implementation  of  ChatGPT  (GPT-4o  Plus), a large language model developed by OpenAI. The human raters were not provided with any additional information about the cases beyond the anonymized transcripts (e.g., other

<!-- image -->

evidence, child history) and conducted their evaluations independently without discussing or comparing scores.

## Training and implementation of the AI model

It is important to note that our methodology relies on prompt engineering with a publicly available  LLM  (ChatGPT,  GPT-4o  Plus),  rather  than  fine-tuning  a  custom  model.  This approach was chosen for its accessibility and to reflect how practitioners might realistically interact with such tools. Consequently, our findings reflect the capabilities and limitations of this specific model and interaction method.

To  enable  ChatGPT  (Rater  3)  to  conduct  CBCA-based  evaluations  within  this prompt-engineered  framework,  a  structured  preparation  phase  was  implemented. The  model  was  supplied  with  foundational  literature  and  documentation  covering CBCA's  theoretical  basis,  detailed  explanations  of  the  19  criteria,  application  principles,  and  scoring  guidelines.  In  collaboration  with  the  researcher  -  an  expert  in forensic  social  work-five  child  statements  outside  the  main  study  sample  were  used for  preliminary  testing  with  ChatGPT  (GPT-4o  Plus).  The  model's  interpretation  of each criterion was monitored, and corrective feedback was provided for any misinterpretations  or  omissions,  with  an  emphasis  on  contextual  sensitivity,  nuances,  and figurative  language.

To enhance consistency and interpretation accuracy, ChatGPT was instructed to provide direct excerpts from the transcripts to support any instance where it marked a criterion as 'partially  present'  or  'definitely  present.'  This  approach  aimed  to  minimize  arbitrary scoring and ensure transparency in the evaluation logic. To test the reliability of the model's outputs,  a  few  additional  statements  were  evaluated  using  another  large  language  model (Google  AI  Studio),  which  produced  results  similar  to  ChatGPT,  providing  additional confidence in the model's scoring capacity (ICC = .887, 95% CI [.815, .931]).

Following this preparation, all 65 anonymized statements were individually submitted to ChatGPT, which analyzed and scored them based on the 19 CBCA criteria.

## CBCA scoring

The  study  used  the  19-item  CBCA  version  developed  by  Steller  and  Koehnken  (1989), which is  widely  used  in  the  field.  Each  statement  was  evaluated  for  each  criterion  using a three-point scale: 0 points for an absent criterion, 1 point for a partially or weakly present criterion, and 2 points for a strongly or clearly present criterion. Each evaluator (forensic psychologist,  social  worker,  and  ChatGPT)  assigned  scores  to  all  19  criteria  for  each statement. The minimum total CBCA score for a statement is 0, and the maximum is 38.

## Statistical analysis

Statistical  analyses  were  conducted  using  IBM  SPSS  Statistics  Version  29.0  and  Jamovi Version  2.6.19.  The  following  statistical  methods  were  employed  to  evaluate  inter-rater reliability and rating patterns:

Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC): Used to assess the agreement among the three raters (forensic psychologist [PS], social worker [SW], and artificial intelligence [AI - ChatGPT]) on total CBCA scores and each of the 19 individual criteria. The ICC (2,k) model (two-way random effects, absolute agreement, average measures) was applied. Confidence intervals (95% CI) were

calculated,  and  values  were  interpreted  using  Koo  and  Li's  (2016)  guidelines: &lt; 0.50 = poor, 0.50-0.75 = moderate, 0.75-0.90 = good, &gt; 0.90 = excellent reliability.

Cohen's Kappa (κ): Applied to measure agreement between raters on categorical CBCA scores  (0,  1,  2)  for  each  criterion.  Kappa  values  were  interpreted  following  Landis  and Koch's (1977) classification: &lt; 0.00 = poor, 0.00-0.20 = slight, 0.21-0.40 = fair, 0.41-0.60 = moderate, 0.61-0.80 = substantial, 0.81-1.00 = almost perfect agreement.

Kendall's W Coefficient of Concordance: Used to assess rank-order consistency among raters  for  each  CBCA  criterion.  Values  range  from  0  (no  agreement)  to  1  (perfect agreement).

Krippendorff's  Alpha  (α):  Calculated  as  a  general  reliability  coefficient  that  can  be applied across various measurement levels (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) and is robust to missing data.

Percent  Agreement  and  Criterion  Incidence  Analysis:  A  simple  agreement  measure reflecting  the  proportion  of  identical  scores  given  by  raters  for  each  criterion.  Mean incidence values were calculated to represent the likelihood of each CBCA criterion being marked as present (1 or 2) across evaluators.

## Results

This  section  presents  the  findings  of  the  comparative  reliability  analyses  conducted  to answer the central research questions. Within the scope of the study, the statements of 65 child victims were independently coded using Criteria-Based Content Analysis (CBCA) by a  forensic  psychologist  (PS),  a  forensic  social  worker  (SW),  and  an  artificial  intelligence model (AI). The presentation of findings begins with an analysis of the inter-rater reliability between the two human experts (PS and SW). Subsequently, the reliability between the AI model and each human evaluator (AI - PS and AI - SW) is examined separately. Inter-rater reliability (IRR) was assessed using multiple statistical methods for each of the 19 CBCA criteria,  including  Intraclass  Correlation Coefficient  (ICC), Cohen's Kappa (κ), Kendall's W, Krippendorff's Alpha (α), and Percent Agreement.

Table 1 demonstrates that there is generally strong inter-rater reliability between the two human experts from different disciplines (forensic psychologist and social worker) in their CBCA evaluations.  For  17  out  of  the  19  criteria,  the  Intraclass  Correlation  Coefficients (ICC) indicated strong agreement, with the majority falling within the 'good' or 'excellent' reliability range (15 of 19 criteria with ICC &gt; .75). Notably high agreement was observed for criteria such as 'Admitting lack of memory' (CBCA 15; ICC = .959) and 'Pardoning the perpetrator' (CBCA 18; ICC = .937).

However,  this  overall  consistency  does  not  apply  to  two  specific  criteria.  Agreement between  the  evaluators  was  poor  to  non-existent  for  'Logical  structure'  (CBCA  1;  ICC = .342),  which  requires  a  holistic  narrative  assessment,  and  'Details  characteristic  of  the offense'  (CBCA  19;  ICC = .351),  which  is  highly  open  to  interpretation.  Particularly  for CBCA 19, the negative Krippendorff's Alpha value (α = -.172) and very low percent agreement (40%) reflect systematic disagreement among raters for this criterion.

## Discussion

This study aimed to examine the potential and current limitations of artificial intelligence (AI) integration in the forensic field by comparing human expert judgments with those of an AI model in the CBCA evaluation of statements by child victims of sexual abuse. The findings highlight two critical insights: First, human experts from different disciplines were largely able to apply CBCA with high consistency, though they faced difficulties with certain inherently  subjective  criteria.  Second  -  and  more  significantly  -  even  a  highly  advanced language model was far from capable of consistently replicating human judgment in such complex  evaluative  tasks,  revealing  a  substantial  gap  between  human  and  machine reasoning.

## Inter-rater reliability between human experts: a consistent baseline with methodological gaps

The  study's  initial  findings  confirmed  a  high  level  of  inter-rater  reliability  between  the forensic psychologist and the social worker (Table 1). For most criteria (15 out of 19 with ICC &gt; .75), agreement ranged from 'good' to 'excellent,' supporting the existing literature suggesting  that  CBCA  can  be  applied  consistently  by  professionals  with  diverse  backgrounds  (Bilginer  et  al.,  2021;  Niveau  et  al.,  2015).  This  consistency  indicates  that  the standardized structure of CBCA effectively reduces subjective variance among raters.

However, this general consistency also exposed two chronic methodological weaknesses within CBCA. No statistically significant agreement was found between raters for 'Logical structure' (CBCA 1) and 'Details characteristic of the offense' (CBCA 19) - criteria that require holistic and interpretive judgment. The negative Krippendorff's Alpha (α = -.172) for  CBCA  19  in  particular  suggests  systematic  disagreement  regarding  the  definition  or application  of  this  criterion.  As  also  noted  by  Vrij  (2008)  and  Welle  et  al.  (2016),  such findings  highlight  the  lower  reliability  of  certain  CBCA  criteria  and  reinforce  the  call  to refine  these  problematic  items  or  address  them  more  explicitly  in  practitioner  training, rather than focusing solely on total scores.

## Disparities between AI and human judgment

The most striking  finding  of  the  study  was  a  stark  divergence  from  the  high  agreement observed between human professionals once the LLM rater was introduced into the process. In  both  AI  -  psychologist  (Table  2)  and  AI  -  social  worker  (Table  3)  comparisons, significant  and  systematic inconsistencies were observed between AI and human evaluations. Reliability coefficients for most criteria remained in the 'poor' range, with negative ICC values found for fundamental items such as 'Logical structure' (CBCA 1), 'Quantity of details' (CBCA 3), and 'Superfluous details' (CBCA 9). These negative coefficients indicate not just randomness but systematic divergence in how AI and human raters interpret these criteria.

Nonetheless, important clues emerged regarding where and why AI struggles. The model showed moderate success in evaluating more concrete criteria identifiable through textual markers or structural patterns - such as 'Accounts of subjective mental state' (CBCA 12) and  'Reproduction  of  conversation'  (CBCA  6)  -  by  detecting  emotion-laden  words  or

quoted dialogue. This suggests that the AI performs sophisticated pattern recognition and lexical  classification  rather  than  genuine  understanding.  Essentially,  the  AI  executes a surface-level lexical analysis without reaching the contextual, integrative, and psychological depth required by CBCA (Gardner et al., 2021). Implicit meanings, cultural references, and  the  psychological  dynamics  behind  narrative  inconsistencies  -  all  intuitively  interpreted  by  human  experts  -  appear  to  lie  beyond  the  current  capabilities  of  the  promptengineered model used in this study.

It is plausible that a fine-tuned model specifically trained on a large dataset of CBCAcoded transcripts  might  perform  differently.  Therefore,  further  research  should  examine whether these limitations in grasping implicit meanings and cultural references are inherent to current LLM architecture or a product of the prompt-engineering approach used here.

## Rethinking AI's role in forensic settings: assistant, not judge

In  light  of  these  findings,  it  is  necessary  to  realistically  reposition  the  role  of  LLMs  in forensic  evaluation  processes.  The  data  clearly  demonstrate  that  the  model  used  in  this study, in its current configuration, cannot function as an autonomous 'judge' or decisionmaker comparable to a trained professional (Shneiderman, 2020). This inability to replicate nuanced expert reasoning presents serious ethical and practical concerns for using any AI as a final decision-making authority in such high-stakes contexts (Galante et al., 2023).

However,  this  does  not  render  the  LLM  useless.  Its  potential  lies  in  supporting  the workflow  of  human  experts  as  a  'cognitive  assistant'  rather  than  replacing  them  (Mao et  al.,  2024).  In  this  assistive  capacity,  it  could  serve  as  a  powerful  efficiency  tool.  For instance, an LLM could rapidly scan lengthy transcripts to highlight all sections potentially relevant to a specific CBCA criterion, such as all instances of dialogue or sentences containing  emotion-laden  words.  This  would  not  replace  the  expert's  analysis  but  would  allow them to focus their limited time and cognitive resources on the most critical parts of the testimony.

Furthermore, the model could function as a standardization aid or a 'second reader.' By systematically flagging potential narrative contradictions, temporal inconsistencies, or the presence of rare details that a human rater might overlook during a manual review, it could act as a valuable control mechanism. This could help reduce individual rater variance and encourage  a  more  thorough  and  consistent  application  of  the  evaluation  criteria  across different cases and experts.

Finally, it holds promise as an interactive training platform. A system that automatically identifies and presents clear, textbook examples of each CBCA criterion from a database of anonymized statements could serve as an effective teaching tool. Novice practitioners could use such a system to hone their skills in a controlled environment, comparing their own judgments  against  the  model's  suggestions  and  the  consensus  of  senior  experts,  thereby accelerating their learning curve.

Even in these promising supportive roles, the ultimate responsibility for interpretation, contextualization, and final decision-making must remain firmly with human experts. The professional's ability to understand the broader context of a case, the non-verbal cues not present  in  a  transcript,  and  the  unique  human  dimensions  of  a  child's  testimony  are capabilities that, for the foreseeable future, remain uniquely human.

## Limitations

While  this  study  represents  a  pioneering  effort  in  Turkey,  several  limitations  should  be considered when interpreting the findings.  A primary  limitation  is  its  focus  on  a  single, albeit advanced, large language model (ChatGPT) accessed via prompt engineering. As our findings are specific to this model and interaction method, they should not be overgeneralized to the entire field of AI, as different architectures like fine-tuned models might yield different results.

Further limitations relate to the study's data and context. The sample size ( N = 65), while adequate for general patterns, may limit the statistical power for evaluating rarely observed criteria.  A  more  critical  limitation  is  the  exclusive  reliance  on  written  transcripts,  which meant that vital non-verbal cues (e.g., tone of voice, pauses) were entirely excluded from the analysis.  Another  important  constraint  is  the  cultural  context;  CBCA  was  developed  in Western  cultures,  and  its  alignment  with  the  narrative  styles  of  Turkish  children  is  an unexamined variable. Finally, our use of a commercial, cloud-based LLM without a secure 'sandbox'  environment  represents  a  significant  ethical  and  data  security  limitation  that future research must address.

## Implications for future research

This study's findings open several critical avenues for future research at the intersection of AI and forensic credibility assessment. A key priority should be to move beyond promptengineered models and test the performance of fine-tuned or purpose-built models trained on large, diverse datasets of forensic interviews. This would clarify whether the limitations observed here are inherent to current LLM architecture or a product of our methodology.

Furthermore, future studies must incorporate multimodal AI systems capable of analyzing not just text, but also audio for emotional prosody and video for non-verbal cues. Such an  approach  would  more  closely  mimic  the  holistic  evaluation  performed  by  human experts.  Investigating  an  AI's  ability  to  understand  and  adapt  to  cultural  nuances  in narrative styles is another crucial research direction, which could involve training models on data from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds.

Finally, as this field develops, robust ethical frameworks and technical safeguards must be  co-designed  by  AI  experts,  legal  professionals,  and  social  work  practitioners.  This includes establishing clear guidelines for data governance, ensuring algorithmic transparency,  and  pioneering  the  use  of  secure  'sandbox'  environments  to  protect  vulnerable populations while responsibly exploring the potential of these powerful technologies.

## Conclusion

This  study  investigated  the  reliability  of  a  large  language  model  in  the  sensitive  and interpretive  task  of  evaluating  child  sexual  abuse  statements,  comparing  its  performance to that of human experts. Our findings reveal a stark contrast: while human experts from different  disciplines  achieved  high  consistency  in  applying  the  CBCA  framework,  this consistency disappeared when the AI model's evaluations were included. The model consistently exhibited systematic disagreement with human judgments, particularly on criteria requiring holistic and contextual understanding.

<!-- image -->

This  discrepancy  underscores  that  while  a  prompt-engineered  LLM  can  recognize surface-level lexical patterns, it currently struggles to grasp the deep psychological and contextual  nuances essential  for  interpreting  a  child's  testimony.  Therefore,  this  study concludes  that  assessing  the  credibility  of  a  child's  statement  remains  a  task  that  lies far  beyond  the  capabilities  of  the  current  generation  of  accessible  AI  tools.  The evaluation  of  such  sensitive  testimony  is  not  merely  a  technical  task  of  pattern recognition  but  a  profoundly  human  endeavor  requiring  expert  judgment,  empathy, and  contextual  awareness.  The  promise  of  AI  in  this  field,  for  the  foreseeable  future, lies  not  in  replacing  this  expertise,  but  in  supporting  it  wisely  and  responsibly  as  an assistive  tool.
