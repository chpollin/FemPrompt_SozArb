## Introduction

In  2023,  over  15  billion  images  were  generated  using  AI.  This  surge  in  AI-generated images has outpaced traditional photography by producing the equivalent of 150 years' worth of  photos  in  less  than  a  year  (Alina  Valyaeva  2023).  Such  a  massive  influx  raises pressing concerns about the potential of these systems to reinforce and amplify specific representations if these images circulate across mass and social media. Like other forms of media,  AI-generated  visuals  have  the  power  to  shape  individual  opinions  through  the mechanism of 'priming,' a process whereby repeated exposure to biased content heightens  its  influence  (David  Domke,  Dhavan  V  Shah  and  Daniel  B  Wackman  1998). Furthermore, the widespread use of AI-generated content can lead to a feedback loop where  content  serves  as  training  data  for  future  AI  models,  thus  reinforcing  and

reproducing  dominant  representations  and  biases  (Luhang  Sun,  Mian  Wei,  Yibing  Sun, Yoo Ji Suh, Liwei Shen and Sijia Yang 2024).

Against  this  backdrop,  our  research  offers  a  novel  contribution  by  analyzing  how feminism  is  visually  represented  by  AI.  This  analysis  is  particularly  timely,  as  feminism, while widely present in public discourse, remains a subject of debate and does not enjoy unanimous support or a shared understanding across society. Recent surveys show that young men are increasingly disengaged from gender equality (e.g., Duffy, Campbell, and Skinner  2024)  and  more  likely  to  adopt  masculinist  views  (Mahaut  Chaudouet-Delmas 2025). The way feminism is visually depicted by AI may influence how future generations perceive feminism.

Feminist scholars and activists have long argued that the experiences of women are shaped by the intersection of various identities and power relations (such as race, class and sexuality), and that a one-size-fits-all approach to feminism is inadequate and exclusionary  (Giada  Bonu  Rosenkranz  2024;  Porismita  Borah,  Shreenita  Ghosh,  Jiyoun  Suk, Darshana Sreedhar Mini and Luhang Sun 2023; Joseph and Bouko 2025; Sam E Morton, Judyannet Muchiri and Liam Swiss 2020). By analyzing AI imagery, we sought to understand whose feminist voices are amplified, whose are silenced, and what implicit values are embedded in these representations.

Situated  at  the  crossroads  of  machines,  humans,  and  visual  culture  (see  Bouko  and Laba 2026), AI imagery is examined through this threefold perspective in Section 1. From a machine perspective, we consider the impact of training data. From a human perspective, we discuss the act of prompting, highlighting how it can lead to the essentialisation and homogenisation of abstract concepts, like feminism. Third, we address the perspective of visual culture, focusing on how AI-generated visuals are shaped by an emphasis on the liberal value of positivity.

To  analyze  feminism  in  AI-generated  imagery,  we  conducted  a  content  analysis  of 2,400 images (see Section 2). The results, presented in Section 3, illustrate the extent to which  and  the  ways  in  which  the  three  AI  systems  differ  in  their  representations  of feminism. Broadly, their distinctive tendencies can be summarized as follows: salad  bar feminism for  DALL·E  3, painterly  touches  of  feminism for  MidJourney,  and denotative women's portraiture for Stable Diffusion, manifesting, as we will see, in seven postfeminist trends.

## Theoretical framework

## The machine perspective: the pivotal role of training data

Research in visual generative AI has already revealed gender bias at different levels, for example in depicting fewer women in traditionally male-dominated roles (Geoffrey Currie, George John and Johnathan Hewis 2024; Anna M Gorska and Dariusz Jemielniak 2023; Eric J York, Eva Brumberger and Verne La Abe Harris 2024), while over-representing them in professions deemed feminine (Francisco-José Garcí-a-Ull and Mónica Melero-Lázaro 2023; Sun et al. 2024). The default norm appears to be characterized by whiteness, youth, low BMI, and no visible signs of disability (Bianchi et al. 2023; Currie, John, and Hewis 2024; Petra Jääskeläinen and Cecilia Åsberg 2024; York, Brumberger, and La Abe Harris 2024).

AI  generates  visuals  by  endlessly  recombining  elements  from  its  training  data. Therefore, the training data plays a pivotal role in shaping the representations produced in  AI-generated  visuals.  The  datasets  used  for  training  remain  largely  opaque.  OpenAI (2022) vaguely stated that DALL·E 2 was trained on millions of captioned images sourced from the internet. Stable Diffusion was trained on image-caption pairs from the LAION5B  dataset,  itself  compiled  from  large-scale  web  scrapes  such  as  Common  Crawl (Christoph Schuhmann, Romain Beaumont, Richard Vencu et al. 2022). MidJourney may also  have  been  trained  on  LAION  (Stephanie  Kirmer  2024),  although  this  has  not  been officially  confirmed  by  the  company.  Among  the  online  sources  used  for  training  generative models, stock photography is likely one of the most prominent, largely due to its extensive  availability  (Gustav  Westberg  and  Gunhild  Kvåle  2024).  Stock  photography reinforces  neoliberal  gender  ideals  by  presenting  traditional  roles  as  personal  choices and sources of fulfilment,  promoting  the  happy  housewife  ideal  that  merges  domestic work and leisure (Alpay Jeong, 2022; Caroline West 2018).

Stock  photography  has  shifted  from  bearing  witness  to  functioning  as  a  symbolic system,  valued  less  for  what  it  represents  than  for  its  adaptability,  genericity,  and  decorative  versatility  across  contexts  (David  Machin  2004).  This  shift  is  reflected  in  AIgenerated  visuals,  which  often  reproduce  the  stock-photo  characteristics  of genericity noted by Machin (see e.g., Thomson et al. 2026).

As Roland Meyer (2025) puts it, AI images reflect a visual mode streamlined to conform to predefined labels and familiar patterns-an approach that not only reinforces a world of clarity and algorithmic legibility, but also enhances their commercial appeal by making them easily marketable across a range of contexts.

Westberg  and  Kvåle  (2024)  emphasize  the  repertoire  of conformist  diversity ,  where characters within a group appear ethnically and gender-diverse, yet ultimately conform to standardized  visual  norms.  Within  feminist  theory,  'glossy  diversity,'  as  described  by Laura  Favaro  and  Rosalind  Gill  (2018),  obscures  power  relations  while  promoting a corporate-friendly version of inclusion. In this context, we sought to examine to what extent  AI-generated  images  of  feminism  reproduce  the  repertoire  of  conformist  and glossy  diversity  in  terms  of  apparent  gender,  skin  colour,  body  type,  age,  religion,  and visible disability (RQ1).

## The human perspective: essentialising complexity through prompts

Classification  is  fundamental  to  how  individuals  organize  knowledge  and  understand social  identity  (e.g.,  Richard  Jenkins  2000).  Yes,  the  shift  from  viewing  photography  as a witness  to  reality toward understanding it  as  a symbolic  system reduces  complex and abstract ideas into simplistic, essentialized visuals. Attempting to condense the variety of human experiences  into  a  single  overarching  narrative  tends  to  marginalize  less  privileged perspectives (Trischa Goodnow 2006). This is particularly evident in protest imagery: while identity politics can play a vital role in mobilization and visibility, they are inherently limited  in  their  ability  to  reflect  the  full  spectrum  of  participants'  backgrounds  and priorities.  In  this  perspective,  our  study  investigates  the  extent  to  which  and  how  AI visually frames feminist protest, including the struggle for labor equality, and the extent to which AI systems mobilize the icons of the Venus Cross, which reproduces the gender binary, and the iconic figure of Rosie the Riveter to visually represent feminism (RQ2).

4

Essentialization  is  produced  by  AI  models  but  it  also  often  stems  from  the  prompts provided by users, which force the system to condense fluid and multi-faceted categories into static visual archetypes (Bianchi et al. 2023, 1496): for example, 'the very notion of generating an image of 'the face of a poor person' is problematic in and of itself, as race, class,  and  other  social  categories  are  not  immutable,  and  more  broadly,  images  of particular characteristics that are or are meant to be uncorrelated with visual attributes cannot  be  generated  without  making  dangerous  assumptions.'  These  visual  outcomes rely  on  discredited  physiognomic  frameworks  (Claudia  Benthien  2002)  and  perpetuate harmful,  deterministic  associations  between  outward  appearance  and  identity,  which echoes racial theories from previous centuries.

## The perspective of visual culture: neoliberal and postfeminist logics in AI-generated imagery

Under late capitalism, contemporary culture has come to be understood as fundamentally promotional  (Norman  Fairclough  1993).  Feminism,  too,  has  been  reshaped  by  these neoliberal dynamics  (see e.g., Sarah Banet-Weiser, Rosalind Gill and Catherine Rottenberg  2020),  generating  a  tension  between  the  fight  for  equal  rights  and  'postfeminist sensitivity' (Gill 2007), in which tropes once criticized by feminism, such as the objectification and stereotyping of women's bodies, are reinterpreted through consumer culture  and  reframed as  symbols of  empowerment and femininity (Edward Wigley and Vevila R. C Dornelles 2022). Postfeminist sensitivity is often driven by an 'entrepreneurial subjectivity' (Gill 2014) which sees women's bodies as a source of value, and which thus renews  the  regimes  of  desirability  and  perfection,  highlighting  by  default  the  white middle-  and  upper-class  woman.  Following  in  the  footsteps  of  stock  photography, DALL·E  3  embodies  the  marketization  of  discourse  at  its  core,  translating  into a  pervasive  sense of  promotional  positivity (Westberg  and  Kvåle  2024),  through  the absence of negative content. Notably, in stock photography, neoliberal feminism appears in  corporate-glamorous  depictions  of  blue-collar  women,  whose  polished  and  relaxed portrayals erase the realities of labor and class while aligning with ad-friendly aesthetics (West 2018). Other researchers describe this market-oriented nature of visual AI imagery in terms of staginess, idealization, and commercial gloss (Meyer 2025; Daniel Palmer and Katrina  Sluis  2024).  Neoliberal  feminism  highlights  the  entanglement  of  feminism  with neoliberalism, which prioritizes professional and economic success (Rottenberg 2018). For Rottenberg (in Banet-Weiser, Gill, and Rottenberg 2020), neoliberal feminism differs from postfeminism  in  that  it  acknowledges  gender  inequality,  while  they  both  disavow  the socio-economic and cultural structures shaping our lives.

The  notions  of  postfeminism  and  neoliberal  feminism  also  offer  a  useful  lens  for analyzing sexualization in generative AI imagery. Postfeminism has framed sexual agency as  potentially  liberating.  Yet  critics  argue  that  such  valorizations  of  sexual  capital  often reproduce the very hierarchies they seek to resist. Through neoliberal and platform logics, sexualization  is  recast  as  a  marketable  form  of  empowerment-spectacular,  shareable, but politically ambivalent (Carol Queen and Lynn Comella 2008). Analyzing sexualization in  AI  images  thus  requires  addressing  the  double  bind:  between  agency  and  market assimilation, between feminist resistance and corporate-friendly visibility. In this context,

we aimed to investigate whether sexualization is visualized as a site of liberation or as an expression of neoliberal feminism within patriarchal structures (RQ3).

In  sum,  to  address  feminism  in  AI-generated  imagery,  we  formulated  the  following research questions:

- RQ1:  To  what  extent  do  AI-generated  images  of  feminism  reproduce  conformist diversity  in  apparent  gender,  skin  colour,  body  type,  age,  religion,  and  visible disability?
- RQ2: How do AI systems represent protest and reinterpret the Venus Cross and Rosie the Riveter?
- RQ3: Is sexualization visualized as a site of liberation or as an expression of neoliberal feminism within patriarchal structures?

We present our method to address these research questions in the next section.

## Method

## Data collection

We designed eight prompts, divided into four types:

-Type 1: underspecified prompts (i.e., 'feminism,' 'feminisms,' 'feminists'), in order to avoid influencing the results and let the AI models reveal their representations. Two prompts were in the plural to implicitly stimulate more diversity than with the prompt in the singular.

-Type  2:  'féminisme'  (in  French)  and  'feminismo'  (in  Spanish)  to  uncover  potential cultural  and  language-based  differences.  We  selected  these  two  languages  because  1) they are widely spoken globally and 2) we are proficient in them and familiar with their cultural  contexts,  which  was  important  in  case  cultural  elements  were  included  in  the visuals.

-Type 3: two more specific prompts (i.e 'feminism diversity' and 'feminism intersectionality')  to  invite  the  AI  models  to  specifically  design  visuals  that  reveal  some diversity.

-Type 4: 'photorealistic feminism,' a popular visual genre among AI-generated visuals, to uncover the potential impact of the style on the representations provided.

We  generated  100  visuals  per  prompt  for  each  of  the  three  selected  AI  models  in the  summer  2024.  In  total,  we  thus  generated  2,400  visuals,  using  Stable  Diffusion XL  (via  stablediffusionweb.com,  using  default  guidance  scale  of  7  and  1:1  aspect ratio),  DALL·E  3  (accessed  through  ChatGPT  4.0  using  temporary  chat  sessions  to minimize  personalization  effects),  and  Midjourney  v5.2  (via  Discord  interface). Midjourney  offers  a  raw  mode  that  tones  down  the  platform's  default  aesthetic styling,  which  we  did  not  activate;  all  images  were  generated  using  the  platform's standard  settings.

## Quantitative content analysis

Two independent coders applied the codebook to a 10% sample, and we assessed intercoder agreement by calculating Krippendorff's alphas (see Table 1). After completing the coding  process,  we  performed  Fisher's  exact  tests  (with  Monte  Carlo  simulation)  to determine whether the differences observed across the eight prompts and the three AI models were statistically significant.

The variables are grouped into four types of patterns (see Table 1). Prior to code them, we  first  counted  the  number  of  represented  people  in  each  visual,  by  means  of  five variables: no human represented, one person, 2-4 people (small interactions), 5-9 people (little social groups) and 10+ people (large groups and crowds).

An  executive  summary  of  the  coding  variables,  along  with  the  full  codebook,  is provided  as  supplementary  material.  The  dataset  is  available  in  a  Zenodo  repository: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.14621520.

Narrative representations focus on actions and processes (like raising one's fist) while conceptual  representations  aim  to  depict  entities,  qualities,  or  categories,  notably  in a  more  classificatory  manner  (Catherine  Bouko  2024a,  2024b;  Gunther  Kress  and  Theo van Leeuwen 2006). This distinction allowed us whether actions or features are used to visually represent feminism.

The variable 'rainbow flag' was used to assess how feminism intersects with LGBTQIA+ issues.

## Results

## Statistical significance of the differences across the eight prompts and the three ai models

Fischer's  tests  indicate  statistically  significant  differences  between  the  three  AI models  as  well  as  between  the  prompts  (the  only  exceptions  are  in  bold  in Table  2).

Analyzing these differences both quantitatively and qualitatively allowed us to observe seven trends that favour neoliberal feminism:

- (1) Symbolic engagement with feminism vs. symbolic void
- (2) Celebrating feminism in pop collages
- (3) Commodity diversity
- (4) The aestheticization of urban protest
- (5) Sexualization
- (6) Cuteness
- (7) Rosie the Riveter and the celebration of pastness

The  quantitative  results  below  are  visualised  by  means  of  a  purple  gradation  (ranging from 0 to 100%); the numerical results are available in the appendix (see Table 7).

## Symbolic engagement with feminism vs. symbolic void

A  large  proportion  of  the  images  generated  by  Midjourney  and  Stable  Diffusion contain  none  of  the  examined  references  to  feminism  ('none  of  these'  in  Table  3): these two AIs frequently depict characters (mostly women) without any connection to  feminism,  like  the  Stable  Diffusion  example  in  Figure  1.  This  discrepancy  likely stems from differences in the training data of Stable Diffusion: we hypothesize that Stable  Diffusion's  training  dataset  includes  fewer  feminism-related  examples.  This underrepresentation  may  also  account  for  the  frequent  appearance  of  glitches  in this  dataset  (like  in  Figure  3b):  when  a  model  is  trained  on  limited  data,  it  lacks sufficient  examples  to  render  certain  features  correctly,  resulting  in  glitches  or unrealistic  depictions  in  the  outputs.  Midjourney's  focus  on  aesthetics  might explain  the  limited  used  of  specific  symbolic  references:  it  may  prioritize  visual style  and  beauty  over  semantic  fidelity.  We  call  this  trend  in  Midjourney painterly touches  of  feminism to  emphasize  that  most  outputs  are  painterly  in  style,  yet  only a  few  actually  depict  feminism. These  modern  visuals  feature  almost  exclusively  young, white  model  aesthetics.  Worksite  conditions  are  carefully  staged:  the  women appear  relaxed  and  smiling,  their  clothing  impeccably  clean,  and  they  often  strike deliberate  poses,  even  when  depicted  as  blue  collarworkers.  While  this  may  signal a  self  assertive  display  of  agency,  it  ultimately  operates  within  a  liberal  feminist framework.  Other  images  perpetuate  the  smiling  working  mother  ideal,  effortlessly combining  career  and  childcare.  This  reflects  the  neoliberal  imperative  to  smile, especially  in  gendered  service  work,  where  emotional  display  is  commodified  and detached  from  genuine  feelings,  especially  when  the  working  conditions  are  precarious  (see  e.g.,  Anna  Fyrberg  Yngfalk  and  Markus  Fellesson  2025).  Furthermore, the  neoliberal  emphasis on smiling and positivity positions fulfilment and resilience as  individual  responsibilities,  blending  economic  incentive  with  affective  investment  (see  e.g.,  Adrienne  Evans  and  Sarah  Riley  2023).

In  contrast  to  Midjourney  and  Stable  Diffusion,  very  few  of  the  DALL·E  3  images lack  any  of  the  examined  symbolic  references.  The  following  trend  examines DALL·E  3's  symbolic  diversity,  notably  in  collages,  and  the  strategy  we  refer  to  as salad  bar  feminism .

In these collages, DALL·E 3 produces what might be called a salad  bar  feminism .  This metaphor,  borrowed  from  studies  on  extremism,  originates  with  USA  FBI  Director Christopher  Wray  (Anna  Meier  2023;  Christopher  Wray  2020),  who  used  it  to  describe extremists  who  select  elements  from  a  'salad  bar  of  ideologies,'  picking  and  mixing ideological  components  at  will,  with  little  concern  for  the  coherence  of  the  resulting combination. ## Commodity diversity

DALL·E 3 displays the greatest diversity of skin colors, with a relatively balanced representation across the four tones (see Table 4). In contrast, our results show a dominance of 'ivory-fair  pale'  and  'beige-light  brown' skin  tones  in  Midjourney and Stable  Diffusion, except for prompts explicitly designed to generate diversity (i.e., 'feminism diversity' and 'feminism intersectionality', see also Bouko 2026).

This greater diversity in DALL·E 3 is, however, accompanied by a strategy of commodity diversity (Elaine  Swan 2010), based on the tokenization (Rosabeth Moss Kanter 2008) of racialized  women.  This  produces  symbolic  visibility  for  one  or  a  few  tokens  without redistributing  power.  Most  DALL·E  3  visuals  are  crowd  scenes  (see  Table  4)  in  which a  few  racialized  individuals  are  included.  While  they  appear  in  these  crowd  images, however, they are completely absent from visuals depicting individual characters. They are also never present in images representing working conditions (see Section 3.3). In this way, DALL·E 3 reflects a form of post-race feminism (Roopali Mukherjee 2016) that conceals the  intersectional  links  between  race  and  material  inequality,  portraying  capitalism  as

Tokenism  masks  the  systemic  rules  that  govern success  under  capitalism  (Cloud  1996). ## The aestheticization of urban protest

Visualizing protest is the most recurring feminist pattern across all three datasets (see the fight symbol in Table 3), appearing mostly in crowd scenes generated by DALL·E 3 and Stable  Diffusion,  whereas  MidJourney  primarily  produces  images  of  single  individuals, with  small  interactions  of  two  to  four  people  occurring  less  frequently  (see  Table  4). Beyond the use of characters who conform to dominant aesthetic norms, the aestheticization  of  resistance  is  further  reinforced  by  neutral  or  smiling  facial  expressions  (see Section 3.2). Most figures appear staged, further distancing the representation from any gritty or realistic portrayal of activism. The negative facial expressions by Midjourney (see Image 3) stand out as an exception. By contrast, some protest images generated by Stable Diffusion adhere more closely to the conventions of documentary, indexical-like photography, without any glossy veneer or idealized portrayal of the demonstrators.

Stable Diffusion consistently depicts protest in urban settings, while MidJourney nearly never  does,  favoring  artistic  backgrounds  that  offer  a  symbolic,  abstract,  and  timeless vision of feminism, largely devoid of protest signs (featuring only raised fists). DALL·E 3 combines both approaches. Depicting collective street protests reinforces a purist vision of  feminist  activism-one  that  is  inherently  public,  visible,  and  collective-while  also reflecting a  Western-centric perspective. Indeed, in authoritarian contexts where public demonstrations  are  repressed,  feminist  resistance  often  takes  more  subtle,  less  overtly public  forms  (Ilana  Eloit  2021).  The  urban  approach  renders  invisible  rural  feminist struggles  and  the  forms  of  activism  that  emerge  outside  dominant  urban  imaginaries (Pascale  Dufour  and  Dominique  Masson  2019).  This  may  also  reinforce  the  disconnect some rural women feel toward feminism, which is often perceived as urban and Westerncentric (Marie-Josée Massicotte 2020). However, a few images generated by DALL·E 3 do depict women in rural settings, though they are all white and model-like.

In addition to urban protest, DALL·E 3 also generates representations of women (very exceptionally with men) in idealized natural settings, depicting yoga/meditation sessions and celebrations of nature (see Figure 4).

These  images  reflect  a  form  of  spiritual  ecofeminism,  sometimes  drawing  on  polytheistic  or  animist  beliefs  that  celebrate  nature  as  an  immanent  asexual  or  feminine divinity.  Such  spiritual  ecofeminism  can  carry  a  subtle  form  of  sexism,  insofar  as  this aesthetic of gentle sisterhood reactivates an essentialist association between women and nature,  echoing  patriarchal  uses  of  this  equivalence  by  assigning  women  the  role  of 'guardians of the Earth' and mobilizing a naturalized view of their bodies and reproductive  functions  (Anne-Line Gandon 2009). This type of essentialization reduces the complexity  of  feminist  struggles  to  a  supposedly  organic  harmony  with  the  environment, obscuring  the  inherent  conflicts  in  gendered  power  relations,  particularly  regarding environmental destruction driven by profit-oriented, masculinist logics (Maria Mies and Vandana  Shiva  1993).  Furthermore,  by  focusing  on  personal  growth  through  yoga  or meditation, these visuals reflect the individualistic orientation of neoliberal feminism.

## Sexualization

Table 6 shows that sexualized aesthetics are entirely absent from the DALL·E 3 images. In contrast, sexualization is particularly present in Stable Diffusion's 'feminism photorealistic' prompt, as well as in four of Midjourney's prompts, though this cannot be attributed to  the  nature  of  the  prompts  themselves.  Midjourney  often  combines  the  markers  of sexualization-subordination,  body  display,  and  feminine  touch-in  the  same  images and thereby intensifies the sexualization of women.

The  shift  from  sexual  objectification  to  sexual  subjectification  in  postfeminist  media culture recasts women not as passive objects but as active, desiring subjects who embrace forms of self-presentation once deemed sexist (Rosalind Gill 2008). Critics, however, contend that this  supposed empowerment ultimately upholds patriarchal norms, permitting choice  only  within  the  confines  of  the  expectations  around  marriage,  motherhood,  consumerism, beauty, and sexuality. Ultimately, AI-generated visuals that reproduce patterns of  sexualization  (particularly  in  Midjourney,  see  Table  6)  tend  to  reinforce  at  least  four heteronormative  codes  of  eroticism,  thereby  intensifying  pressures  around  beauty  and sexuality rather than expanding women's empowerment. First, the women depicted conform strictly to  Western beauty codes of sexualization: they are uniformly young, modellike  (and  nearly  all  white),  with  long  hair,  makeup,  and  hairless  bodies.  Scholars  have critiqued  postfeminist  rhetoric  for  rendering  the  labor  of  beauty  invisible  and  obscuring the social and emotional costs of noncompliance while reinforcing neoliberal, postfeminist ideologies  and  economies  (see  e.g.,  Ashley  Mckay,  Shannon  Moore  and  Wendee  Kubik 2019).  Second,  while  Gill  (2008)  emphasizes  the  role  of  pleasure  and  enthusiasm  in postfeminist  sexual  empowerment,  it  is  striking  that  the  facial  expressions  of  the  figures suggest  the  opposite:  none  of  the  women  smile,  and  some  even  convey  negative  emotions.  Third,  the  few  images  that  depict  more  than  one  woman  (all  generated  by Midjourney) reproduce the 'hot lesbian' trope (Gill 2009), framed primarily for heterosexual male pleasure, in two ways. First, they operate within the heterosexual beauty standards outlined  above.  Second,  the  choice  of  characters  reinforces  heteromasculine  fantasies: women  are  either  paired  in  stark  contrast-for  example,  a  Black  woman  with  a  fairskinned  blonde,  echoing  soft-porn  scenarios  organized  around  'types'  or  'flavors'-or they are depicted as near-doubles, a configuration that invokes the fantasy of duplication frequently exploited in pornography (see Figure 5). ## Cuteness

The visuals generated by Stable Diffusion using the prompt 'féminisme' are notable for their  neoliberal  aesthetics  of cuteness ,  which  does  not  appear  elsewhere in  the dataset (see  Figure  6).  Indeed,  many  of  these  images  depict  female  characters  with  childlike features:  compact,  small  bodies,  often  with  exaggerated  proportions  such  as  oversized heads and eyes. Nearly all the characters are smiling.

These  traits  exemplify  the  visual  codes  of  cute  aesthetics:  smallness,  compactness,  formal  simplicity  (Sianne  Ngai  2012).  This  aesthetic  of  powerlessness  is  intertwined with ambivalent empathy, highlighting the asymmetry  of power  in cuteness.  Cute  objects  are  non-threatening,  prompting  viewers  to  perceive  them as  weaker  and  in  need  of  care,  while  also  sometimes  provoking  a  desire  to diminish  them  further  (Ngai  2012).  In  other  words,  cute  is  the  'aestheticization  of powerlessness  ('what  we  love  because  it  submits  to  us'),'  the  'commodification  of social  difference'  (Ngai  2012,  59).

Cute  aesthetics  have  already  been  appropriated  in  postfeminist  contexts  to support  personal  entrepreneurship,  for  example  through  non-threatening  online personas embodied by baby-doll women. Research shows this reflects a  postfeminist  contradiction,  where  agentic  freedom  is  expressed  through  a  cute hyperfemininity  (Adrienne  Evans  and  Riley  Sarah  2017;  Anthony  P  McIntyre  2020). A  tension  also  emerges  in  depicting  feminists  as  adorable  and  vulnerable  in  these AI-generated  visuals,  reinforcing  the  very  power  imbalances  feminism  seeks  to challenge.  In  this  way,  their  objectification  is  pushed  to  an  extreme,  as  'the  cute

thing  is  the  most  reified  or  thinglike  of  things,  the  most  objectified  of  objects  or even  an  'object'  per  excellence'  (Ngai  2012,  93).  For  Angela  McRobbie  (2004),  the postfeminist  'undoing  of  feminism'  operates  by  notably  keeping  the  female  subject  in  the  position  of  a  modern  but  silent  'girl,'  an  effect  further  reinforced  by  the aesthetics  of  cuteness  in  such  visuals.  However,  these  cute  representations  may also  align  with  a  non-violent  stance-a  more  humanized,  care-oriented  vision  of existence  that  acknowledges  individuals'  vulnerability  and  interdependence,  rather than  competition  and  the  survival  of  the  fittest-and  this  applies  not  only  to women.  In  these  visuals,  though,  the  cute  and  these  related  values  are  applied exclusively  to  women  (men  are  not  portrayed  as  cute  and  vulnerable).

## Rosie the Riveter and the celebration of pastness

While DALL·E 3 largely reproduces the original Miller's Rosie the Riveter poster, nearly all MidJourney visuals generated with the prompt 'feminism' depict an aestheticized version of Rosie (see Table 3), mostly infusing it with a sense of pastness . With model-like, almost exclusively  white  figures,  these  images  reinforce  the  nostalgic myth of  Rosie,  which already  bore  little  resemblance  to  the  realities  of  World  War  II  factory  life  (James J  Kimble and Lester C Olson 2006): created in 1943 for the Westinghouse company for business purposes rather than to support the war effort, Miller's Rosie the Riveter was far from the feminist icon she would later become from the 1980s onwards.

As  Roland  Barthes  notes,  'the  function  of  myth  is  to  empty  reality.  [. . .]  Myth  acts economically: it abolishes the complexity of human acts, it gives them the simplicity of essences' (1972, 269). David Lowenthal's (1985, xvii) reflections on nostalgia help explain this mythic recuperation. As he writes, 'the past is a foreign country whose features are shaped by today's predilections, its strangeness domesticated by our own preservation of its vestiges.' Nostalgia alienates people from the present. The same applies to Rosie in AIgenerated visuals: she is valued not for the specific historical conditions she evokes (and which are mythic), but for the comforting ambience of an imagined past. This reuse of Rosie's image thus performs the very process of pastness creation: she is less a historical figure  than  a  mythic  artefact  shaped  to  serve  neoliberalism.  More  broadly,  pastness  is mirrored stylistically in the nostalgic aesthetics of many AI-generated visuals: these often reconstruct earlier decades' aesthetics by blending heterogeneous references, from documentary photography to pop album covers and video game imagery (Meyer 2025).

In 1943, Rosie was a tokenised representation of female power, an exception among the  series  of  42  posters  created  by  Miller  at  that  time,  which  as  a  whole  reinforced women's subordination (Kimble and Olson 2006). The Black Rosie in Midjourney's visuals is also a form of tokenization, being a Black model-like figure who remains an exception among the representations of Rosie in this dataset (as in Figure 7).  By presenting such model-like exceptions (which also aestheticize intersectionality), these tokenized representations  obscure  the  structural  inequalities  that  persist  between  races,  classes,  and bodies.

For Belinda Du Plooy (2021), Rosie has become an empty signifier of empowerment and of nostalgic traditional femininity. She epitomizes the way neoliberalism appropriates the  cultural  energy  of  feminism,  repackages  it,  and  sells  it  back  to  women,  emptied  of political  substance.  In  this  sense,  she  embodies  the  'double  entanglement'  typical  of

For Ulrika Dahl (2014, 2), Rosie embodies the allure of vintage '1950s kitsch femininity' : a 'white bourgeois fantasy of the past that was and remains far from universally available.' Midjourney reproduces this fantasy in an aestheticized, and occasionally tokenized, nostalgia. Another perspective would be to view the Black Rosie as an illustration of the 'echoic' nature of Rosie-a type of iconic image in which power and meaning echo across different contexts (María Del Mar Rivas Rivas-Carmona  and  Manuel  Balsera-Fernández  2015),  such  as  Little  Bit's  dark-skinned Latinx  pin-up  (Kathleen  M  Ryan  2021). ## Discussion and conclusion

Feminism is a notion shaped by the intersection of multiple identities and power relations, for  which  a  one-size-fits-all  approach  is  inadequate  and  exclusionary  (Bonu Rosenkranz 2024;  Borah  et  al.  2023;  Morton,  Muchiri,  and  Swiss  2020).  Consequently,  AI-generated visuals of feminism may reflect this contested social space. However, it appears that these visuals rarely serve as a site where different feminist approaches coexist and confront one another; rather, they mostly tend to reproduce postfeminism (Gill 2007) and its underlying neoliberal  values  (Rottenberg  2018;  Banet-Weiser  2018),  reinforcing  white,  bourgeois, urban voices as normative. Our analyses show that the three AIs develop distinct representational  strategies.  DALL·E  3  tends  toward salad  bar  feminism ,  providing  variety through  the  inclusion  of  all  the  examined  feminist  symbols  and  all  the  visual  trends except sexualization and cuteness. This eclectic approach creates a tension between the denunciation of structural struggles and an ultimate privileging of neoliberal positivity. Midjourney displays painterly touches of feminism , favouring aesthetic coherence aligned with neoliberal feminism and the visibilization of individuals who best fit capitalist codes of fashion and beauty. By privileging positivity, beauty, and diversity stripped of structural critique, these AI models reinforce the cultural economy of what Ahmed (2010) described as the promise of happiness , where feminism is acceptable if it is cheerful. Stable Diffusion,

<!-- image -->

for  its  part,  offers  mostly denotative  women's  portraiture ,  presenting  individual  women without  any  connection  to  feminism,  while  simultaneously  exploiting  the  aesthetics  of cuteness (which may diminish women) and of sexualization, which reproduces cisheteronormative fantasies. The reasons for this contrast between a denotative approach and cuteness/sexualization  remain  opaque,  and  cannot  be  explained  by  the  nature  of  the prompts used.

Understanding the differences between the three generative IAs exposes how they are cultural actors that reproduce, reframe, and erase certain visions of feminism. Each model, through  its  visual  logic,  participates  in  constructing  what  feminism  is  perceived  to  be: a  pluralistic  yet  ultimately  neoliberal  movement  in  DALL·E  3,  an  individualized  and market-friendly ideal in Midjourney, or a feminized but mostly non-feminist imagery in Stable Diffusion. Studying these representational patterns allows us to grasp how their underlying  aesthetic  choices  participate  in  the  normalization  of  particular  social imaginaries.

Taken together, the seven observed trends reflect the dominance of neoliberal values and the double entanglement (McRobbie 2004) typical of postfeminism, which, under the guise of pseudo-diversity and empowerment, ultimately reproduces existing power relations  and  invisibilizes  dissenting  voices.  The  quantitative  analysis  shows,  however,  that not all visuals align with neoliberal feminism: for instance, Stable Diffusion's indexical-like representations of protest deviate from it. Some images confirm the neoliberal tendency not when analyzed in isolation, but insofar as they function as tokenized exceptions to the overarching norm of white, bourgeois dominance.

On  the  one  hand,  the  neoliberal  approach  to  feminism  in  AI  imagery  comes  as  no surprise: as West observed regarding women in stock photography, 'we simply can't look to  market  logic  for  social  justice'  (2018,  p.  438).  OpenAI  (behind  DALL·E)  attributes  the representational biases in DALL·E 2 to the skewed nature of the internet-sourced images used during training (OpenAI 2023). Yet, the fact that visuals retrieved via Google Images often appear less biased than those produced by DALL·E 2 suggests additional factors are at  play  (Sun  et  al.  2024).  One  such  factor  could  lie  in  how  image-text  pairs  were  constructed:  while  tech  companies  increasingly  align  their  messaging  with  feminist  and LGBTQIA+ rights, these commitments are frequently undermined by coders working in silos,  disconnected  from  both  these  values  and  upper  management  (Alexander  Monea 2022). On the other hand, the dominance of neoliberal feminism cannot be reduced to an algorithmic  issue,  nor  should  it  be  seen  as  inevitable:  it  is  also  partly  driven  by  user prompts, which potentially ask AI to essentialize fluid, abstract, and complex concepts. Moreover,  user  preferences  for  dominant  representations  are  likely  to  further  reinforce these patterns via their prompts, the visuals they select to interact with the AI (as a basis for  generating  new  images),  and  the  images  they  ultimately  choose  to  share  in  their communication. This neoliberal dominance can be countered by crafting prompts that explicitly  address  its  bias  and  by  selecting  images  that  diverge  from  or  subvert  the aesthetic  codes  of  neoliberal  feminism.  Images  such  as  those  of  the Black  Rosie could, for instance, be mobilized in contexts that seek to deconstruct structural inequalities. The history of feminism is punctuated by acts of reappropriation by feminists reclaiming the very  visual  codes  and  symbols  they  seek  to  dismantle.  In  the  age  of  AI,  engaging  in feminist struggles also entails resisting the neoliberal ideologies embedded within these AI  models.  In  doing  so,  AI  can  also  serve  as  a  site  for  feminist  reappropriation,  where
