## Abstract

Purpose -This study examines how AI literacy shapes workplace equality and competitive advantage, with particular attention to the mediating role of psychological capital.

Design/methodology/approach -Using survey data from 467 owner-managers, managers and senior staff in small- and medium-sized enterprises in Surabaya, Indonesia, the study applies partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) to test the proposed relationships.

Findings -AI literacy exhibits a statistically significant yet modest direct effect ( ß 5 0.133, p &lt; 0.05) on workplace equality and competitive advantage. However, its primary influence operates indirectly through psychological capital (strong path: ß 5 0.600, p &lt; 0.001), which fosters self-efficacy, resilience, optimism and hope, thereby driving equality ( ß 5 0.531, p &lt; 0.001) and firm performance. Psychological capital, in turn, significantly promotes workplace equality, emerging as a pivotal antecedent to competitive advantage ( ß 5 0.494, p &lt; 0.001). These patterns underscore AI literacy's role as a human-centered amplifier in resourceconstrained SMEs.

Practical implications -Beyond technical training, integrate PsyCap-building interventions (e.g. resilience workshops paired with AI ethics modules) into SME support programs, drawing on evidence from Indonesian contexts to foster inclusive AI adoption and align with UNESCO equity goals.

Originality/value -By conceptualizing AI literacy as a socially embedded capability, this study demonstrates that AI functions as a social amplifier: its performance and equality outcomes depend less on technology itself than on how it reshapes human agency and psychological capacity in organizational contexts.

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become a central driver of organizational transformation, with a growing body of literature showing that AI adoption enhances firm performance, productivity and innovation, thereby contributing to competitive advantage. Prior research emphasizes how AI enables firms to develop cost-efficient capabilities and align strategic opportunities with datadriven decision-making to generate sustained value (Kemp, 2023). However, as AI technologies become increasingly inexpensive, standardized and widely accessible, their ability to serve as a durable source of competitive advantage diminishes. This development challenges the assumption that AI adoption alone guarantees superior performance and calls for greater attention to the organizational and human conditions under which AI creates value (Wingate et al ., 2025).

As a result, AI adoption generates uneven productivity and growth effects across firms and value chains, raising concerns that accelerated diffusion of AI may reinforce existing economic and organizational inequalities, particularly among smalland medium-sized enterprises (Aghion et al ., 2024).

Beyond performance outcomes, AI increasingly raises critical questions about workplace equality and social policy. International organizations such as UNESCO (2021) argue that AI literacy promotes equality by enabling individuals to access new opportunities in education, employment, and digital services (Wang et al ., 2022). Despite growing recognition of AI literacy as a critical capability, prior research predominantly conceptualizes it as a technical or cognitive competence for interacting with complex AI systems, including awareness of ethical and compliance risks (Benlian and Pinski, 2025). What remains underexplored is how AI literacy operates within organizational contexts as a socially embedded capability that shapes employees' confidence, agency and capacity to engage with AI-mediated work. In particular, little empirical research examines whether and how AI literacy contributes to workplace equality, especially in SMEs operating in emerging economies, where organizational structures are less formalized and resource constraints are more pronounced (Iram et al ., 2024; Li et al ., 2025).

This study fills this contextual gap by examining the relationship between AI literacy and competitive advantage, with psychological capital and workplace equality as mediating factors. Psychological capital offers a theoretically grounded mechanism through which AI literacy may translate into inclusive and productive workplace outcomes. By integrating insights from dynamic capabilities theory, inequality regimes and positive organizational scholarship, this study advances a human-centered perspective on AI adoption that moves beyond technological determinism.

## Literature review

AI literacy and competitive advantage

The resource-based and dynamic capabilities theory shows that value creation arises from integrating knowledge, interpreting AI outputs and reconfiguring resources under uncertainty (Teece, 2023). AI allows firms to build a competitive advantage not simply by automating tasks but by integrating knowledge and capabilities across domains in novel ways (Krakowski et al. , 2023). AI literacy strengthens competitive advantage by enabling firms to convert AI from a generic technology into a strategic resource.

As a strategic resource, AI enhances organizational creativity and productivity, allowing firms to generate sustained value from AI-driven intangible assets and support innovation-led growth (Brynjolfsson et al ., 2021). However, firms can no longer rely solely on AI adoption to differentiate themselves, as AI technologies become increasingly standardized and widely accessible (Wingate et al ., 2025). Instead, competitive advantage increasingly depends on firms' capabilities to seize opportunities, generate innovations and reconfigure resources in AI-enabled environments.

AI literacy plays a pivotal role in strengthening the firm's dynamic capabilities by enabling managers and employees to recognize where AI creates value (awareness). As firms effectively deploy AI systems, critically evaluate AI outputs and anticipate ethical implications (ethics), AI literacy supports opportunity sensing and informed decisionmaking (Wang et al ., 2022). These competencies enable firms to move beyond experimental

adoption toward purposeful AI deployment, thereby enhancing their capacity to seize innovation opportunities amid business uncertainty (Gama and Magistretti, 2025).

Empirical evidence highlights the importance of such capability-based differentiation. Babina et al . (2024) show that AI investment drives firm performance primarily through product innovation rather than cost reduction, with gains concentrated among firms that effectively integrate AI into their strategic and organizational processes. At the same time, AI's predictive advantages remain constrained under conditions of Knightian uncertainty, where novelty and competitive recursion limit full computability (Townsend et al ., 2024). Under these conditions, AI literacy becomes critical for managerial decision-making, enabling firms to interpret AI outputs, recognize their limitations and reconfigure resources accordingly.

Research on human-AI partnership further reinforces that organizations achieve superior problem-solving outcomes when humans and AI interact through structured hybrid processes -autonomous, sequential and interactive search -particularly in exploratory tasks (Raisch and Fomina, 2024). Such hybrid processes require high levels of AI literacy to coordinate human judgment and algorithmic capabilities effectively. Together, these insights suggest that AI literacy serves as a microfoundation for dynamic capabilities, enabling firms to translate ubiquitous AI technologies into sustained competitive advantage (Gao et al ., 2025).

## AI literacy and workplace equality

AI literacy promotes workplace equality by enabling responsible governance of AI systems. Inequality regimes theory and responsible AI scholarship show that fairness improves when organizations actively manage access, transparency and accountability in AI use (Healy et al. , 2019). Research on AI governance highlights the role of informed human oversight and participatory design in fostering inclusive outcomes (Danaher, 2024; OECD, 2022). Through these mechanisms, AI literacy empowers organizational members to identify, question, and correct biased practices, supporting more equitable workplaces.

AI supports equality only when organizations adopt a balanced approach to its design, deployment and governance (Wingate et al ., 2025). Structural factors such as access, control, surveillance and digital divides shape unequal AI outcomes, often reproducing existing power asymmetries. Bircan and € Ozbilgin (2025) demonstrate how these dynamics generate inequality while arguing that co-ownership of AI systems and legal recognition of AI agents strengthen accountability and promote fairness. In contrast, when organizations allow AI systems to rigidly dictate decisions, execution or creative outputs, they restrict human judgment, improvisation, and creativity, leading to suppression, alienation or excessive dependence on technology (Ma and Su, 2025).

AI changes how organizations approach equality by forcing them to make choices about access, design and deployment. These decisions can either support equal opportunity or change the technology's moral direction (Danaher, 2024). Gregory et al . (2021) show that AI systems learn by interacting with users, creating feedback loops. More use improves performance and draws in more people, supporting inclusion. Milkau (2019) shows that AI data platforms create value by turning data into insights and improving services. These platforms support userorganization interactions, but the distribution of benefits depends on governance decisions.

AI affects workers and consumers through automation and decision support. This impact relates to rights, equality, job quality and education. The OECD (2022) notes that these effects depend on responsible design and governance rather than the technology itself. Research on inequality situates these effects within established theoretical frameworks. Healy et al. (2019) and Nkomo and Rodr � ıguez (2019) demonstrate that Acker's inequality-regimes model has broad applicability. It is used across corporate, public and agricultural areas and is extended to intersectional and global contexts.

## AI literacy and psychological capital

Psychological capital studies primarily draw on resource-based frameworks such as conservation of resources, psychological resource theory and the job demands-resources model, which conceptualize capital as a set of interrelated psychological resources (Reichard et al ., 2025). Hence, AI shapes workplace equality through organizational decisions about how firms design, access, interpret and govern systems, rather than through technological inevitability (Danaher, 2024; Wingate et al ., 2025). Deploying AI without sufficient human oversight, unequal access, surveillance asymmetries and rigid automation amplifies the existing workplace inequalities (Bircan and € Ozbilgin, 2025; Ma and Su, 2025).

AI-literate staff engage in governance that promotes transparency, accountability and access. They examine data sources, monitor feedback and adjust system use to address unequal outcomes (Gregory et al ., 2021; Milkau, 2019). AI literacy allows members to assess algorithmic fairness and challenge automated choices that may be unethical or noninclusive (Wang et al ., 2022). These practices fit with the inequality regimes theory. This theory holds that everyday routines and rules may drive systemic inclusion or exclusion (Healy et al. , 2019; Nkomo and Rodr � ıguez, 2019).

- H3. AI literacy positively determines psychological capital, which in turn promotes workplace equality.

## Psychological capital and competitive advantage

Psychological capital (PsyCap) refers to an individual's positive psychological state of development, entailing four interrelated resources: self-efficacy, hope, optimism and resilience, which yield substantial motivational and performance benefits through transformational leadership (Luthans et al ., 2007). PsyCap also demonstrates collective psychological resources that foster entrepreneurial activity and support organizational success (Tjimuku et al ., 2025).

PsyCap helps firms gain an edge by building employees' ability to perform during uncertainty, pressure and opportunity. People with high PsyCap keep working through challenges, find new solutions and stay motivated. These behaviors improve productivity and performance (Gr € ozinger et al ., 2022). PsyCap also supports adaptable, resilient practices. For example, Akroyd et al. (2025) show that management systems remain stable within the existing culture but adapt to new demands. This example shows how intangible resources support both stability and innovation.

By building positive psychological resources, organizations use PsyCap to drive performance. PsyCap improves decision-making, problem-solving and teamwork, letting firms respond to change better than rivals. Leadership style, culture and setting matter too. Loghman et al . (2023) show that PsyCap's effects on performance, engagement and burnout are shaped by these factors. This confirms its importance for long-term advantage.

## Psychological capital and workplace equality

PsyCap advances workplace equality by enabling employees to engage constructively with organizational challenges. Previous studies combined inequality regimes theory and conservation of resources theory to show that employees with higher psychological capital cope more effectively with stress, manage perceptions of unfairness and participate more fully in organizational processes (Tafvelin and Keisu, 2024; Lee et al ., 2022). Further evidence

indicates that psychological capital empowers underrepresented groups to leverage opportunities when supportive leadership and policies are in place (Widiati et al ., 2025).

Psychological capital functions as a nonstigmatizing, inclusive resource that directly promotes workplace equality by enhancing employees' resilience, optimism, self-efficacy and hope. Employees with higher PsyCap cope more effectively with stress, manage perceived unfair treatment and engage constructively with colleagues, thereby reducing the adverse effects of inequality and fostering equitable participation in organizational processes (Luthans and Broad, 2025; Lee et al ., 2022). For instance, Widiati et al . (2025) show that female managers' digital innovation behavior is driven by PsyCap and high-quality leader-member exchange, demonstrating how PsyCap empowers underrepresented groups to leverage opportunities in supportive organizational contexts.

Scholars caution that individual literacy alone cannot overcome these structural disparities, as privileged groups control the majority of access to AI education and skill-building. Fair and inclusive AI use requires socio-relational responsibilities, prompting future interventions to move beyond anti-discrimination techniques toward technical and policy tools that embed deeper ethical and equity values (Giovanola and Tiribelli, 2023). Moreover, PsyCap strengthens the impact of workplace equality policies by enabling employees to transform structural and procedural interventions into positive experiences and outcomes. Research shows that gender moderates the relationship between PsyCap and well-being, indicating that organizations need both PsyCap development and gender-sensitive policies to achieve equal outcomes (Momin and Rolla, 2024).

H5. Psychological capital positively determines workplace equality.

## Competitive advantage and workplace equality

Dynamic capabilities theory suggests that firms pursuing long-term performance invest in fair compensation, employee participation and inclusive governance (Teece, 2023). Moreover, there has been a dispute over whether equality drives competitive advantage or whether competitive advantage drives equality. When organizations promote equality, they expand access to broader talent pools, strengthen employee commitment and stimulate creativity and innovation (Pratono et al ., 2024). These equality-driven outcomes become valuable, rare and hard-to-imitate resources, enabling firms to generate sustainable competitive advantage (Urbancov � a et al ., 2020). Herlitah (2025) confirms that equality plays a pivotal role in unlocking underutilized leadership talent, broadens strategic perspectives and strengthens dynamic capabilities.

Teece (2023) suggests that competitive advantage reinforces equality when it focuses on innovation, dynamic capabilities and ecosystem competition rather than mere cost efficiency. By shifting competition policy away from static models that favor incumbents with scale advantages toward frameworks that recognize innovation-driven competition, organizations reduce entrenched market power, limit excessive economic rents and promote broader welfare gains -thereby creating fairer competitive conditions and supporting more inclusive economic outcomes. Competitive advantage strategies, therefore, do not merely coexist with inequality; they actively shape and structure it, and firms succeed only when perceived inequalities align with employees' fairness expectations (Bao et al ., 2025).

Measurement

This study employs four constructs: AI literacy, workplace equality, psychological capital and competitive advantage, and measures them using reflective, perception-based items adapted from established literature. To measure workplace equality, the study adapts items from

Tafvelin and Keisu (2024), who define equality as respondents' perceived access to influence, resources and opportunities within the workplace. Consistent with this conceptualization, the items capture respondents' perceptions of their influence over work goals and decisionmaking, access to career development opportunities, job security, compensation and benefits, and the extent of respectful treatment at work.

This study operationalizes AI literacy using the refined measurement framework proposed by Wang et al . (2022). The items assess respondents' ability to identify AI technologies embedded in applications, use AI tools effectively to support daily work, improve work efficiency, select appropriate AI-driven solutions, choose suitable AI applications for specific tasks and adhere to ethical principles in AI use. These indicators reflect both the functional and ethical dimensions of AI literacy emphasized in recent research.

To measure psychological capital, the study adapts items from Luthans et al . (2007), Santos et al . (2018), and Reichard et al . (2025), who conceptualize psychological capital as a positive psychological state comprising self-efficacy, hope, resilience and optimism. Following this framework, the items assess employees' confidence in presenting their work and mastering new procedures, their ability to generate solutions and pursue work goals, their capacity to recover from setbacks and grow through challenges, and their optimistic expectations regarding future work outcomes.

Finally, the study measures competitive advantage using subjective comparative assessments adapted from prior strategy research (Schilke, 2014; Shafiee, 2021; Pratono, 2022). Consistent with strategic management theory, the study conceptualizes competitive advantage as a firm's relative superiority over competitors rather than absolute performance. This perceptual approach has received broad empirical support and fits survey-based research contexts in which objective financial data are often unavailable or lack comparability across firms.

## Data collection

The study focuses on business organizations operating in Surabaya City, predominantly smalland medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), where the Surabaya Statistic Bureau (2025) estimates a population of approximately 150,000 business owners and entrepreneurs. To obtain a relevant and accessible sample, we employed snowball sampling supplemented by mixed online and in-person survey methods (e.g. Pratono, 2022; Herlitah, 2025). Online questionnaires were distributed to broaden coverage, while in-person visits conducted by trained surveyors enhanced response rates and data accuracy. Data collection took place between October and December 2025, which yielded 467 valid responses from business organizations across Surabaya City.

The respondent profile reflects the business organizations, in which individuals occupy different positions and owner-managers often combine strategic, managerial and operational responsibilities. Of the respondents, 45% were owner-managers, 15% were managers and the remaining participants were senior staff. Organizational size varied, with 45% employing 1-4 employees, 32% employing 5-19 employees and 23% employing 20-50 employees, while respondents were drawn from diverse sectors, including trading (35%), manufacturing (16%), services (42%) and agriculture (8%). PLS-SEM is appropriate for this exploratory study involving non-normal data and complex relationships in SME contexts (Hair et al. , 2024; see also Widiati et al ., 2025). While limitations related to snowball sampling, the cross-sectional design, and reliance on self-reported measures remain, these are common in SME research and were mitigated through sectoral diversity, variation in organizational roles and established procedural controls.

## Discussion

## Theoretical contributions

While prior research demonstrates that firms derive value from AI by embedding it into organizational knowledge, decision-making routines and resource reconfiguration (Krakowski et al. , 2023; Teece, 2023), this study offers a contextualized extension by modeling AI literacy as a microfoundation of dynamic capabilities within SMEs in Surabaya. Rather than claiming universal generalizability, the findings illustrate how AI literacy operates through individual-level psychological mechanisms in a resource-constrained SME environment. By empirically linking AI literacy to competitive advantage in this specific context, the study supports and refines existing arguments that employees' capacity to interpret AI outputs, recognize limitations and apply AI insights under uncertainty enables innovation-led performance (Brynjolfsson et al ., 2021; Babina et al ., 2024), particularly where formal organizational structures are less developed.

The study further clarifies how AI literacy relates to workplace equality and psychological capital in a developing urban SME setting. Prior literature emphasizes that AI's effects on equality depend on governance, transparency and human oversight rather than technological determinism (Healy et al. , 2019; Danaher, 2024). Building on this perspective, the findings suggest that in the Surabaya SME context, workplace equality emerges not automatically from AI literacy but through socially mediated processes shaped by employees' psychological capital. In this sense, psychological capital functions as an enabling condition that supports confidence, agency, and critical engagement with AI-mediated tasks (Wang et al ., 2022), facilitating voice and bias awareness (Gregory et al ., 2021; Milkau, 2019). These insights should be interpreted as context-bound theoretical refinement rather than universally generalizable effects.

Finally, this study proposes that psychological capital and workplace equality may serve as strategic resources that reinforce competitive advantage within SME ecosystems characterized by limited formal governance mechanisms. While prior research links selfefficacy, optimism, resilience and hope to collaboration and innovation (Luthans et al ., 2007; Gr € ozinger et al ., 2022; Tafvelin and Keisu, 2024), this study integrates these constructs into a single causal structure and tests it in an emerging-market SME context. By positioning competitive advantage as potentially reinforcing workplace equality, the study offers a theoretical reconfiguration that is particularly relevant in contexts where performance legitimacy strengthens inclusive practices. Overall, the contribution lies in framing AIenabled competitive advantage as a human-centered dynamic capability under contextual constraints, rather than presenting a universally applicable model.

## Policy implications

The findings indicate that AI literacy within Surabaya SMEs contributes to performance through effective integration rather than mere adoption. Psychological capital appears to function as a critical enabling mechanism in this setting, where organizational slack and formalized HR systems are often limited. These results suggest that AI implementation in similar SME contexts should be treated as a human-centered organizational transformation rather than solely as a technological upgrade.

From a managerial standpoint, several contextually grounded implications emerge. First, SME managers may benefit from combining AI literacy initiatives with psychological capital development through hybrid training programs that strengthen self-efficacy, optimism, resilience and hope (Luthans et al ., 2007). In smaller firms where roles are fluid, experiential learning and AI-based simulations could be particularly effective. Second, organizations are encouraged to monitor workplace equality before and after AI deployment using validated instruments (e.g. Tafvelin and Keisu, 2024) to assess whether AI-supported processes improve or unintentionally constrain access to participation and influence. Third, the strong relationship between equality and competitive advantage observed in this sample suggests

that inclusive practices may serve as a performance-enhancing mechanism in SME environments, although further replication is needed before broader policy claims are made.

At the policy level, the results are most directly relevant to urban SME ecosystems similar to Surabaya. Indonesian public agencies, such as the Ministry of SMEs, could consider integrated programs that jointly promote AI literacy and the development of psychological capital among SMEs. Rather than focusing exclusively on digital infrastructure or technical upskilling, policy design may benefit from incorporating resilience-oriented interventions that strengthen psychological resources alongside technological adoption (Pratono et al ., 2024). However, broader national or cross-sector generalizations should be approached cautiously until supported by comparative evidence.

## Research limitation and future studies

This study offers valuable insights but also has limitations. The sample includes SMEs in Surabaya City recruited via snowball sampling, limiting generalizability to other settings. Relying on self-reported, perception-based measures may cause recall or social desirability bias, as respondents hold varied roles. The cross-sectional design captures associations at a single point in time, so it doesn't assess the long-term or dynamic effects of AI literacy, psychological capital or workplace equality. The model examines only a subset of constructs, so other influential variables remain unexplored. Future research could use larger, more diverse samples, longitudinal or mixed-method designs, and broaden the model to include more variables across varied settings.

## Conclusion

This study demonstrates that competitive advantage from AI appears to emerge when AI literacy is developed as a human-centered capability and supported by employees' psychological capital, especially in Surabaya SMEs context. The findings suggest that technical proficiency alone is insufficient to produce equitable or performance-enhancing outcomes; supportive leadership, transparent governance and inclusive practices remain central in this specific context. Rather than presenting equality as an automatic byproduct of digitalization, the results indicate that inclusive outcomes depend on intervening human capabilities and organizational conditions. Future research should test this integrated model across sectors, regions and institutional environments to determine its broader applicability.

## Ethical considerations

This study involved a minimal-risk, nonclinical survey of adult participants in their professional roles. Data were collected anonymously, no sensitive or personally identifiable information was obtained and participation was fully voluntary with informed consent. The research involved no intervention, deception or vulnerable populations and adhered to established ethical standards for social science research, including confidentiality and data protection.
