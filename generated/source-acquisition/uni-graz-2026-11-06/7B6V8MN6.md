## Feminist knowledges in the age of AI

## Redi Koobak 1

<!-- image -->

We are in the midst of an accelerating race for global dominance of artificial intelligence (AI) innovation that is widely described as a 'new Cold War', with the United States, Russia, and China positioned as key players. This AI Cold War is clearly not just a matter of competition for technological supremacy, but a global cultural phenomenon shaped by legacies of empire, heteronormative gendered power, and racialised capitalism.

Working recently on an analysis of how national mythology operates within AI governance sharpened my awareness of how narratives, technologies, and power become braided together in ways that make particular futures appear natural and inevitable. That work (Atanasoski and Koobak, forthcoming) examines Estonia's Kratt AI initiative, in which systems for automating government services are named after the kratt - a mythological servant from Estonian folklore, constructed from discarded everyday objects and animated through supernatural means. The kratt promises wealth for its master as long as it is given a consistent stream of tasks; if left idle, it can turn on or even kill its master. Invoking the kratt in this context is telling: it promises tireless efficiency while also carrying the threat of turning on its master, a tension that captures both the appeal and the danger of AI in governance.

A close reading of this initiative as an 'automated myth' shows how policy branding, interfaces, and technological infrastructures do more than deliver services. They organise political imagination, link technological progress to liberal-democratic destiny, and obscure the imperial circuits of capital and expertise that sustain them. Seeing how technofutures are turned into heritage in this way makes it difficult to treat computational systems anywhere - including in feminist academic publishing - as merely technical supports.

This leads us to the question: what might it mean for feminist scholarship to take these systems seriously as part of the 'epistemic wells' it is drawing from?

Feminist  theory  has  long  insisted  that  knowledge  is  never  produced  in  a vacuum. In the present moment, any potential 'vacuum' has been replaced by a dense mesh of computational systems that participate in deciding what is seen, shared, and remembered.

From this vantage point, academic publishing looks less like a neutral channel and more like an infrastructural field. Submission portals, indexing services, citation databases, recommendation algorithms, and language models do not simply organise and circulate scholarship; they actively structure it. They privilege some languages, regions, genres, and modes of writing over others, even as the labour, politics, and inequalities that sustain them remain largely unseen.

Recent critical work on artificial intelligence helps to situate these dynamics within a wider political economy. As Karen Hao (2025) argues, contemporary AI systems are embedded in an emerging technological empire - a global regime of extraction in which data,  labour,  energy,  and  linguistic  resources  are  drawn  disproportionately  from  the world's peripheries to serve a small number of corporate and geopolitical centres. When such systems are folded into academic publishing, they do more than streamline workflows.  They  risk  extending  these  asymmetries  into  the  production  and  circulation  of knowledge itself, amplifying some voices while quietly marginalising others under the banner of efficiency and innovation.

This query into the impact of AI on feminist knowledge production is also connected to a broader question that keeps coming up with intensifying urgency and, at times, deep scepticism: what can feminism do ? Or, to echo Treva Lindsey and Attiya Ahmad's (2024) formulation in a recent preface to Feminist Studies (p. 7): 'What will feminism do?' (emphasis added). As they point out, this provocation resonates widely with many of us committed to feminist scholarship and activism, amid global uprisings around gendered and  racialised  violence,  resurgent  authoritarianisms  and  unfolding  catastrophes  in Palestine, Congo, Sudan, Haiti, Ukraine - and most recently, Venezuela - to name only a few. We note the speed with which the terrains on which feminist struggles are waged are themselves being reorganised. This is a reorganisation that is being shaped by - and is shaping - media systems, data infrastructures, and technologies that determine how violence is documented, how suffering circulates, and how solidarity is made visible or 'disappears'. Therefore, it is not only a moment of multiple acute socio-economic-political crises but also an epistemic emergency, inviting us to revisit our commitments, our methods, our theories, and the possibilities/impossibilities of our shared and uneven field of study and action.

Within this shifting terrain, the European Journal of Women's Studies is not outside these dynamics but part of them. As a transnational feminist journal, EJWS is entangled in the same infrastructures of platforms, metrics, languages, and databases that shape how feminist scholarship travels and takes form. At the same time, the journal has long been a space for feminist scholarship committed to probing how worlds are made and unmade,  how  meaning  is  reworked,  and  how  new  forms  of  knowledge  emerge  in moments of upheaval. These traditions remind us that feminism has never been singular

or settled but has always emerged through contestation, imagination, and the labour of thinking hard and thinking differently.

Rather than offering a programme or a set of prescriptions, these reflections offer questions that will surely preoccupy many of us in our academic work - and just generally in life! - currently and in the foreseeable future. As computational systems become ever more deeply embedded in the ordinary practices of feminist scholarship, they invite us to ask anew what it means to think, write, and act as feminists in this moment. What will feminism do when the infrastructures through which we know and speak are themselves being remade? We remain committed to sustaining a space where such questions can be held open - where the conditions of feminist knowledges can be rendered visible, debated, and re-imagined in the midst of profound political and technological change.
