## Artificial intelligence: a neoliberal tool to better manage social inequalities?

Intelligenza artificiale: Uno strumento neoliberale per gestire meglio le disuguaglianze sociali?

Paul Michael Garrett

School of Political Science and Sociology, University of Galway, Galway, Republic of Ireland

## ABSTRACT

Divided  into  two  main  sections,  and  surveying  critical  literature  often neglected  within  social  work's  academic  discourse,  the  article  discusses AI  and  inequalities  in  a  neoliberal  and  global  context  in  which  notions and  practices  circulating  around  'austerity',  rationing  and  scarcity  are entrenched. First, the focus is on predictive analytics within the spheres of  child  welfare,  social  protection  and  policing.  Here  AI-assisted ways of engaging with 'social problems' shape methods of intervention and are prone  to  replicate  and  deepen  the  discriminatory  approaches  of  the past.  Second,  it  is  maintained  that  development  and  deployment  of  AI is  also  likely  to  extend  forms  of  neo-colonialism  or,  what  some  term, 'digital  colonialism'  in  the  Global  South.  Additional  social  work  research might explore how to counteract some of these structurally  embedded problems.

## SOMMARIO

Suddiviso in due sezioni principali e  passando in rassegna la letteratura critica  spesso  trascurata  nel  discorso  accademico  sul  lavoro  sociale, l'articolo discute l'intelligenza artificiale e le disuguaglianze in un contesto  neoliberista in cui le nozioni e le pratiche che  circolano intorno all'austerità, al razionamento e alla scarsità sono profondamente radicate. Inizialmente, l'attenzione si concentra  sull'analisi predittiva nell'ambito del benessere dei minori, della protezione sociale e dell'attività  di  polizia.  In  questo  ambito,  i  metodi  di  intervento  assistiti dall'IA  per  affrontare  i  'problemi  sociali'  tendono  a  replicare  e,  di  fatto, ad aggravare  gli approcci  discriminatori del passato. Passando  alla seconda  sfera, lo sviluppo e l'implementazione dell'IA rischiano di estendere  forme  di  neocolonialismo  o,  come  alcuni  lo  definiscono,  di 'colonialismo  digitale'  nel  Sud  del  mondo.  In  questo  contesto,  ulteriori ricerche nel campo  del lavoro sociale potrebbero esplorare  come contrastare alcuni dei problemi strutturalmente radicati.

Perhaps, Artificial Intelligence (AI) magnifies the risk of a drift towards dulled functionality within social  work?  AI  has  been  defined  as  'systems  that  display  intelligent  behaviour  by  analysing  their environment  and  taking  actions -with  some  degree  of  autonomy  to  achieve  specific  goals' (European Commission: Directorate-General for Migration and Home Affairs, 2020, p. 1). Thus we can think of  AI  as  any  system  capable  of  doing  things  which  we  would  normally  regard  as  intelligent  in humans. For AI applications today, this typically comes in the form of recognising patterns, making inferences, taking case-by-case decisions or engaging in conversation. Indeed, it is important to recognise AI as an umbrella term, covering various technologies each designed to solve a particular kind of problem (European Commission: Directorate-General for Migration and Home Affairs, 2020. p. 1).

More critically,  and  alluding  to  some  of  themes to  be  addressed  in  this  article,  the  Tricontinental: Institute for Social Research (2021, p. 13) states that AI refers to:

software  that  analyses  and  processes  large  amounts  of  data  with  various  and  complex  computational  mathematical  operations.  The  term  suggests  a  notion  of  neutrality:  though  it  seems  that  there  is  an  autonomous machine 'thinking'  and  making  decisions,  in  reality,  the  software  that  allows  it  to  run  carries  the  bias  of  the entity developing the technology.

Unsurprisingly, the dismal Starmer administration in the UK, in large part attempting to re-heat Blair's  New  Labour  project  and  ever  on  the  lookout  for  a  'big  idea',  has  been  quickly  won  over to  the  notion  that  AI  will  provide  a  fast-track  to  modernisation  and  national  renewal  (Booth, 2025). Although saturated in the ideology of corporate capitalism, the dominant message of his administration  is  that  AI  is,  indeed,  socially  neutral  and  merely  provides  us  with  tools  that  are beneficially transformative.

Here, one of the major problems is the sheer power of the corporate sponsors of AI technologies (Garrett, 2022; see also Hao, 2025; Vincent, 2025). Central is a promotional discourse that animates a constellation of keywords such as AI-assisted 'efficiency' (viewed as unequivocally good and modern) and 'bureaucracy' and 'red tape' (emphatically perceived as bad and old-fashioned) (Garrett, 2018; see  also  Williams,  1983).  In  truth,  the  endgame  appears  to  be  to  for  AI  to  replace  democratic welfare/social states with technologically administered entities shorn of social-democratic imperatives. This is also a context in which expertise, in fields such as social work, may slowly transmogrify into mere data processing.

Not seeking to furnish fresh empirical data, this article is narrowly focused on how the introduction of AI -and, especially, predictive analytics -is taking place at a conjuncture in which neoliberalism is still capital's preferred regime of accumulation. Thus, inequalities and practices circulating around  'austerity',  rationing  and  scarcity  are  deeply  embedded.  Alongside  this  are  institutional orders which are deeply racialised and gendered and this becomes especially clear with regard to the current use of predictive analytics in the spheres of child welfare, social protection and policing. Here,  AI-assisted  ways  of  engaging  with  'social  problems'  are  replicating  and  entrenching  the

## AI and inequalities

Social work is a discipline and field of practice recognising that 'structural barriers contribute to the perpetuation of inequalities, discrimination, exploitation and oppression' (International Federation of  Social  Workers  (IFSW),  2014).  Practitioners  must,  therefore,  must  'challenge'  the  patterning  of social  and  economic  relations  that  solidify  and  consolidate  such  'barriers'  (IFSW,  2018).  Indeed, some supporters of AI assert that, based on mathematical modelling, AI is anti-discriminatory and wholly  attuned  to  the  concerns  of  the  IFSW  as  reflected  in  its  various  policy  documents.  Shortly, we will examine this notion as it relates to predictive analytics given that many 'life-changing interactions between individuals and state agents' are increasingly 'determined by a computer-generated score' based on a 'vast collections of digitized information about individuals and mathematical algorithms that both catalogue their past behaviour and assess their risk of engaging in future conduct' (Roberts, 2019, p. 1695).

In the US and writing in the social work profession's flagship journal, Goldkind (2021, p. 372) correctly  maintains  the  'data  that  powers  outcomes,  and  the  social  systems  in  which  these  tools  are deployed all inevitably reflect existing structural inequalities'. Importantly, although Goldkind may insufficiently  recognise  it,  AI  can  also  provide  neoliberal  governments  with  more  effective  'tools' to manage social misery. That is to say, rather than seeking to eliminate avoidable harm, AI deployment  can  result  in  neoliberal  states  being  better  able  to  organise  the  neglect  of  populations regarded as surplus to capital's  requirements (Garrett,  2019).  Certainly,  introducing  AI  can  appeal to governments as a way to help cut public spending and to further embed retrenchment and 'austerity'. This is because AI's 'core operation of transforming messy complexity into decision boundaries  is  directly  applicable  to  the  inequalities  that  underpin  the  capitalist  system  in  general,  and austerity in particular' (McQuillan, 2022, p. 74). AI can seem, in fact, to achieve a few things simultaneously. On the one hand, it provides a new capacity to predict where 'need' and 'intervention' is 'objectively' warranted. On the other hand, despite the corporate rhetoric of creative 'disruption' (Deloitte, 2020), techno-solutionist 'narratives' tend to pivot on 'maintaining the status quo' and so favour ruling class interests' (Varon &amp; Peña, 2021, p. 5; see also Garrett, 2021b).

Goldkind concludes that AI can provide 'significant possibilities for improving services and the lives  of  staff  and  clients … e.g.  predictive  analytics,  or  the  use  of  data  for  forecasting  service  successes  and  challenges,  might  be  used  to  help  agencies  understand  how  best  to  focus scarce resources '  (Goldkind,  2021,  p.  373,  emphasis  added).  For  example,  a  data  organisation  works  with food pantries to use historical data and machine learning algorithms to 'predict a client's level of dependency on the pantry' (Goldkind, 2021, p. 373). Here what is starkly apparent is that 'scarcity' and  the  need  for  people  to  have  recourse  to  food  pantries  is  presented  as  a  'given',  as  a  mere fact, despite the U.S. being one of the richest and most unequal countries in the world.

Not  unreasonably,  it  might  be  suggested  that  social  workers,  attentive  to  the  IFSW  policies, should not merely aspire to use AI to devise better ways of rationing food for the most impoverished. Rather than more skillfully managing state neglect, a more fitting aim might be to collectively confront an economic system that perpetuates extraordinary levels of food insecurity. Recent data illustrates  the  extent  of  this  social  misery:  8.4  per  cent  (11.2  million)  of  U.S.  households  had  low  food security  in  2023:  5.1  per  cent  (6.8  million)  of  U.S.  households  had very low food security  at  some time during 2023 (Department of Agriculture (USDA), 2025). Attentive to the underlying structural causes of this situation, the IFSW (2021) states 'inequitable global capitalism has to be transformed' so as create an alternative model intent on eradicating 'exploitation' and 'unjust structures that allow for  wealth  concentration  in  the  hands  of  the  few'.  Without  this  overarching  project,  it  is  easy  to foresee how AI might be used to simply administer social hardship.

<!-- image -->

At the level of epistemology, the corporate devisers and vendors of AI software assert that its utilisation can illuminate a 'hidden mathematical order in the world that is superior to our direct experience'  in  that  it  can  identify  'patterns  faster  than  we  can  or,  indeed,  that  escape  us  entirely' (McQuillan,  2022,  p.  48).  According  to  this  reasoning,  we  should  be  easily  persuaded  that  AI  can enhance  and/or  even  occasionally  replace  human  activity  in  a  swathe  of  areas.  At  the  heart  of such endeavours lies a claim that the inputting of data leads to classifications producing predictions about future occurrences. Such an approach has revolutionised global supply chains and logistics of transportation enabling corporations to get products, such as perishable food items, onto shelves in a more efficient and profitable way. Furthermore, predictive analytics is now an important technology across a range of other fields, including those relating to intelligence gathering and the management of migrants (Ozkul, 2023). In Israel's genocidal assaults in Gaza, such technologies have played a highly significant role (Abraham, 2025; see also Garrett, 2025a; 2026). In what follows, however, we will briefly concentrate on the use of predictive analytics in spheres relating to social work.

## Predictive analytics, child welfare and protection services

Child welfare services are increasingly using AI enhanced predictive analytics systems to algorithmically  identify families for attention where there are 'child protection concerns' (Leslie et al., 2020). These developments are driven by a range of additional conjunctural factors, but two may be particularly influential. First, child protection services are often trying to cope with 'austerity' conditions, with an inadequate number of staff and limited resources. This is a situation in which AI enhanced predictive analytics, it often proclaimed, may -as  mentioned earlier -be able to reduce costs by illuminating  how  resources  could  be  used  more  'smartly'  and  'efficiently'  by  better  'targeting' families  for  'early  intervention'.  Second,  the  deaths  of  children  at  the  hands  of  their  parents  and carers is frequently highlighted in the media in a manner that pillories social workers and suggests that they are distracted from their proper safeguarding roles because of an alleged fixation with 'political correctness' and being in thrall to 'woke' ideologies. In such circumstances, predictive analytics is marketed and sold to public authorities as a way of providing a, seemingly, 'scientific' method to 'reform' child protection services (Eubanks, 2018a). Added to the promotional mix is the promise that supposedly 'objective' data can extinguish the charge of racism levelled at child protection services and, more broadly, social work (Dettlaff et al., 2020).

However, studies by critical researchers indicate that AI-aided predictive analytics reinforce the social and economic inequalities that the IFSW state must be challenged. Importantly also, 'socially applied AI has a tendency to punch down: that is, the collateral damage that comes from its statistical fragility ends up hurting the less privileged' (McQuillan, 2022, p. 35). This leads Ricaurte (2019, p. 333) and other critics of such practices to use the term 'algorithmic violence'. Relatedly, the 'values underpinning and shaping' such technologies are 'unavoidably skewed by the fact that there is a diversity  crisis  in  the  artificial  intelligence  sector  across  gender  and  race'  in  that  those  designing systems  tend  to  be  'overwhelmingly  white,  male,  well-off'  and  from  the  Global  North  (United Nations General Assembly, 2019, p. 22). This is not a situation that can be easily remedied by, say, recruiting  a  more  'diverse'  team  of  design  engineers  and  introducing  anti-bias  training.  Although not  necessarily  to  be  scornfully  dismissed,  such  corporate  'quick  fixes'  manifestly  fails  to  address underlying  structural  inequalities  lodged  within  encompassing  hegemonic  apparatuses  (Hoare  &amp; Nowell Smith, 2005).

Presently,  AI  generated approaches within the field of child protection and associated services appear to place emphasis not on judging, but on calculating and relying on the seductive 'evidence of mathematical, purely rational truth' (Arendt, 1972, p. 37). Thus, those advocating for the immediate and widespread use of AI take advantage of the 'aura of science to perpetuate the idea that its abstract mathematical models provide a reliable way of knowing' and are incontestably superior to 'lived  experience'  (McQuillan, 2022, p. 51). However, this reliance on the 'aura' can be questioned because,  whilst  AI  may  emulate  science  by  collecting  data  and  making  models,  AI  processes  are

<!-- image -->

also entirely enmeshed in the social milieu in which it they are situated. Any 'data scientist working to automate issues of a social nature, in effect, is engaged in making moral and ethical decisions' drenched  in  ideology  and  unthinking  'common  sense'  (Birhane,  2021,  pp.  1 -2;  see  also  Garrett, 2024a).

It is striking that even 'proponents of extant predictive analytic tools acknowledge that they may replicate  existing  inequities'  (Glaberson,  2019,  p.  346).  After  'repeated  fitting  to  training  data,  the algorithm  'learns'  to  generalise  its  predictions  for  new  cases.  Thus,  big  data  algorithms  try  to reveal regularities in the data which can be used to make predictions' (McQuillan, 2016, p. 2). Crucially,  however,  there  tends  to  be  an  incapacity  to  adapt  and  respond  to  scenarios  lying  beyond the training data. As McQuillan, observes, with AI:

there are plenty of signs that these insights can be distorted and unreliable. The statistical nature of machine learning  means it  assumes that  the  distribution of  data  on  which  the  algorithm  has  been  trained  covers  the spread of occurrences in the wider world. Any shift in the underlying distribution (new behaviours, unexpected events) can throw a spanner in the works. There have been cases where a self-driving car has collided with a tow truck at the side of the road because its training data didn't include a statistically significant representation of tow trucks (McQuillan, 2022, pp. 27 -28).

More fundamentally, the world is constantly in flux and characterised by relentless movement and change, yet AI applications relating to 'social problems' appear to fail to take account of the possibility  of  a  structural  break  with  what  has  gone  before.  Consequently,  AI  usage  is  likely  to  bolster societal  projects  aiming  to  preserve  existing  power  structures  and  relations  of  power.  Even  when AI systems seem to generate 'new' knowledge this is done in a way that is 'wholly tied to the conditions under which the training data was generated' (McQuillan, 2022, p. 43). This means that such systems are imbued with potentially tremendous and oppressive characteristics. In this sense, AI is best understood 'not as a system that measures and represents the world, but as an apparatus that helps to produce aspects of the world through the specific exclusions' that can be generated and maintained (McQuillan, 2022, p. 112).

This  can  have  profoundly  damaging  consequences  in  child  protection  social  work  given  the intensity  of  surveillance  directed  at  racialised  and  impoverished  communities  (Marovatsanga  &amp; Garrett,  2022).  As  evidenced  in  the  US,  discriminatory  'risk'  assessments  have  also  resulted  in African American children and their families being disproportionately targeted for coercive interventions (Dettlaff et al., 2020; Lash, 2017; Roberts, 2014). Discussing a not dissimilar situation in the UK, relating to the experience of Nigerian parents, Cynthia Okpokiri (2021) refers to their fears of being  caught  'parenting-while-Black'(see  also  Garrett,  2023).  Literature  from  Canada  similarly shows  that  Black  parents  feel  that  their  parenting  practices  are  unfairly  targeted  by  child welfare agencies resulting in the overrepresentation of their children in the care system (Adjei &amp; Minka, 2018). However, racialised -and gendered -notions of 'risk',  derived  from  previous and flawed child welfare practices, uncritically provide the training data or raw material for predictive analytics and so pre-existing patterning of economic and social inequalities, related to state interventions in particular families, are very likely to be reproduced (Redden, 2020; Redden et al., 2020). Glaberson (2019, p. 344) also expresses concerns that AI 'tools tend to project static conditions from  a  family's  past  into  the  future,  without  considering  rehabilitative  actions  taken  or  resiliency-supporting factors that may be present'.

Because of this reductive, technicist epistemology, many parents -having experienced childhood lives characterised by hardship and struggle -are effectively locked into their past. No space is left for transformation because, so goes this reasoning, the maths simply foretells their futures. Hence, these AI  systems are inherently conservative because they do not appear to allow for, as observed, the appearance of the unexpected and a rupture from a pre-existing patterning (Doctorow, 2020). On a more abstract, philosophical level, such systems operate in defiance of Hannah Arendt's assertion that a 'characteristic of human action is that it always begins something new … .We are free to change the world and to start something new in it' (Arendt, 1972, p. 5 original emphasis). Socially applied AI

<!-- image -->

can be perceived, therefore, as 'ultimately a technology of unfreedom because it closes off possible futures' other than those determined by its coders (McQuillan, 2022, p. 82).

After  analysing  several  examples  of  automated  decision-making  systems  in  the  U.S.,  Eubanks (2018a,  p.  9),  concludes  that  the  'cheerleaders  of  the  new  data  regime  rarely  acknowledge  the impacts of digital decision-making on poor and working-class people'. Relatedly, what is occurring with the deployed models is that child neglect is often confused with living in conditions of grinding poverty; in her words, these systems are apt to confuse 'parenting while poor with poor parenting' (Eubanks, 2018b). Nonetheless, Glaberson (2019, p. 331), refers to the fact that child protective services in more than a 'dozen states' were, at the time of her study, using or developing predictive analytic  tools.  She  examines  two  schemes:  the  Allegheny  Family  Screening  Tool  and  the  Eckerd Rapid Safety Feedback tool. One state ended its trial of the latter system citing the tool's 'unreliability'  (Glaberson,  2019,  p.  335).  Reportedly,  'case  workers  using  the  tool  were  'alarmed  and  overwhelmed by alerts as thousands of children were rated as needing urgent protection'' (Glaberson, 2019,  p.  335).  For  instance,  for  'more  than  350  children,  case  workers  received  alerts  saying  the child  had  a  '100  percent  chance  of  death  or  serious  injury  in  the  next  two  years''  (Glaberson, 2019, p. 335).

Clearly, this is a far from 'scientific' and 'objective' endeavour because although 'algorithms often are discussed as 'black boxes" that spit out predictions based on hidden, impenetrable processes, they  actually  are  the  product  of  intentional  design  choices  by  their  creators'  (Glaberson,  2019,  p. 330). One of Glaberson's main concerns is that such systems also produce socially potent 'scoring' systems  that  are  likely  to  result  in  punitive  outcomes  for  many  families.  Moreover,  data  subjects may  develop  'learned  helplessness',  unable  to  'comprehend  the  decisions  that  are  being  made, unable to discuss them meaningfully with others and unable to effectively dispute them' (McQuillan, 2022, p. 61).

## Predictive analytics and social protection systems

The IFSW (2016) advocates for robust systems of social protection, but concerns have been repeatedly aired about the adverse impact of AI on social security systems (Alston &amp; van Veen, 2019). For example, to 'narrow down the pool of social benefits claimants, new and intrusive forms of conditionality are introduced that are mediated by digital infrastructures and data analytics' (McQuillan, 2022,  p.  73).  Thus,  according  to  the  United  Nations  there  is  now  a  'grave  risk  of  stumbling zombie-like into a digital welfare dystopia' (United Nations General Assembly, 2019, p. 2). This is a consequence  of  the  embedding  of  systems  'increasingly  driven  by  digital  data  and  technologies that  are  used  to  automate,  predict,  identify,  surveil,  detect,  target  and  punish'  (United  Nations General Assembly, 2019, p. 2). As a result, the 'digital welfare state often seems to involve various forms  of  rigidity  and  the  robotic  application  of  rules'  (United  Nations  General  Assembly,  2019,  p. 17). Caseworkers are, therefore, often side-lined and silenced within the workflows constructed by corporate software designers (Garrett, 2005). This development is illustrated by the cruel administration of 'universal credit' in the UK (Grover, 2019). There are also countless other examples of AI software  being  used  within  the  sphere  of  housing  and  social  welfare  in  ways  that  penalise  the users of such services (Booth, 2024): here, for example, the SyRI or System Risk Indication in the Netherlands is notoriously prominent and, beyond Europe, the so-called 'robodet' programme in Australia has been subject to widespread criticisms (McQuillan, 2022).

## Predictive analytics and policing

Social workers interact with the police as part of joint-working arrangements frequently enmeshed in carceral approaches to crime and social problems (Garrett, 2004; see also Murray et al., 2024). In the recent past this has become a more controversial area of practice following calls for the 'defunding' of the police following, especially in the U.S., the killing of George Floyd and other minority ethnic

<!-- image -->

people at the hands of serving officers. Relatedly, there have been calls for the 'abolition' of policing and  of  social  work  activities  collaborating  with  a  'care'  to  prison  'pipeline'  for  children  and young people enclaved in marginalised and disenfranchised communities (Hyslop &amp; Pease, 2025; Kim et al., 2024).

Given the focus of the present discussion, it is important to note that AI-generated predictive analytics have been extensively used for many years to purportedly 'target' criminals. Ordinarily rooted in  racialised  and  discriminating  perceptions,  such  hegemonic  approaches  to  predictive  policing deepen  inequalities  given  the  software  merely  identifies  the  'same  people  similar  to  the  ones they  have  been  arresting  all  along'  (Doctorow,  2020).  As  a  consequence  of  this  'overloading  of social  hierarchy  in  the  design  process,  it  should  be  no  surprise  that,  as  with  police  'stop  and search' operations, the generation of algorithmic false positives will both reflect existing social discrimination and act to reinforce it' (McQuillan, 2022, p. 36). Thus, a 'predictive policing system doesn't predict crime, it predicts policing' (Ball in Doctorow, 2020). Nevertheless, such systems are increasing favoured in the U.S and they are also being introduced by, for instance, Javier Milei's neoliberal and authoritarian regime in Argentina (Barber, 2024).

A good deal of this AI software is derived from counter-insurgency and military fields and, mirroring the logics of the occupying army, it is fixated with identifying of so-called 'hot spots': areas of cities which warrant particular attention and a concentration of state forces intent on combatting criminality  and,  more  ambiguously,  on  quelling  other  forms  of  protest  and  disturbance  (Roberts, 2019). For example, the widely adopted Predpol system was integral to so-called 'counterinsurgency' efforts in Iraq, but it was subsequently repurposed to supposedly predict crime in urban areas such as  Los  Angeles  (McQuillan,  2022).  However,  as  Roberts  (2019,  p.  1708)  maintains,  systems  of  AIinformed  predictive  analytics  simply  'package'  inequality  into  'risk  scores'  which  'necessarily reflects  individuals'  privileged  or  disadvantaged  positions'  within  the  wider  social  structure.  Such computerised predictions aspire to identify individuals for 'agencies to regulate from the moment of  birth,  without  any  regard  to  their  actual  responsibility  for  causing  social  harm'  (Roberts,  2019, p. 1713). Indeed, in California, the police were criticised for using predictive technologies to identify so-called 'gangster babies' ( CBS News ,  2016).

Similar systems have been put in place in the UK with Amnesty International (2018) identifying major problems with the Metropolitan Police's so-called Gangs Database set up in 2012 and subsequently  reformulated  and  re-named  in  2024  (BBC  News,  2024,  see  also  Amnesty  International, 2025).  Containing  approximately  4,000  individuals,  at  the  time  of  the  2018  Amnesty  report,  87% of  those  featured  on  the  databased  were  from  Black,  Asian  and  minority  ethnic  backgrounds.  As well as being discriminatory, the database had no process for reviewing, correcting, or deleting outdated information. Neither was there any system in place to even notify individuals that they had been  added  to  the  database  and  so  they  could  contest  their  inclusion  or  have  their  names removed. Information sharing between the police and other government agencies still meant that being  featured  on  the  database  could  adversely  impact  on  an  individual's  interaction  with  social work, housing, education, social welfare and employment services.

As currently deployed in the spheres discussed, AI risks merely embedding 'exclusions under the guise of  solving social problems' (McQuillan, 2022, p. 139). Indeed, uncritical use of this technology would seem to run entirely counter to the equality agenda promoted by the IFSW. Additionally, as we will see, there is also a global inequalities dimension to many of the concerns raised thus far.

## AI, global inequalities and 'digital colonialism'

The IFSW (2014, 2020) is attentive to how the 'legacy of colonialism' impacts on and shapes social work theory and practice (IFSW, 2014, 2020; see also Garrett, 2024b). Alert to the present-day economic power imbalances relating to the Global North and Global South, the IFSW joined other progressive  social  forces  pressing  for  'international  trade  agreements  to  be  socially  just,  fair  and sustainable  as  measured  against  the  World  Fair  Trade  Organisation's  (WFTO)  principles'  (IFSW,

2019). In taking such as position, the IFSW recognised that fairer trade agreements, tested against the  WFTO  benchmarks  are  not  simply  abstract  considerations,  but  'impact  on  all  aspects  of  our lives' on a daily basis with the most adverse consequences faced by the 'millions of the most marginalised and vulnerable peoples of this earth' (IFSW, 2019). Currently, international trade agreements sanctioned by the WTO are 'one of the major reasons why six-sevenths of the world's population continue to live in poverty' (IFSW, 2019).

IFSW awareness of the lack of equity characterising world trade agreements -shaped within the context of deeper, historical practices of colonialism -encourages us to reflect further on the evolution of AI (Césaire , 2000]). Several writers insightfully argue that the most adverse impact of AI may be especially felt in parts of the Global South because, for example, the 'majority of highly paid software development jobs' are 'located in the wealthy Western countries while low paid and often precarious clerical, data entry, assembly and mining work is located' in the Global South (Muldoon &amp; Wui,  2023,  p.  11).  More  pervasively,  the  corporate  power  wielded  by  Big  Tech  and  transnational fossil fuel conglomerates appears to be replicating some of the worst facets of old-style colonialism (Hao, 2025, Ch. 12).

In  the  past,  colonial  powers  exploited  the  sweated  labour  of  Indigenous  populations  and extracted  valuable  raw  materials.  Empires  also  mapped  and  re-ordered  the  terrain  of  colonised lands  re-shaping  the  world  views  of  those  subject  to  colonisation.  However,  Muldoon  and  Wui (2023), drawing on Quijano (2007) and his theorisation of the 'coloniality of power', maintain that some  of  the  practices  formerly  associated  with  the  brutalism  of  empires  are,  in  complex  ways, now mimicked by Big Tech. Importantly, theoretical interpretations such as these illuminate, therefore, how the current practices of Big Tech do not represent such a radical break with the past as some propose (Zuboff, 2019) but can more accurately be understood as a complex intensification of  older  models  of  extraction  rooted  in  capitalism  and  colonialism.  Although  the  vocabulary  of Big  Tech  may  be  very  different  and  its  practices  may  appear  to  be  unlike  those  of  the  past, digging  a  little  deeper  we  can  still  discern  rapacious  approaches  to  the  peoples  of  the  Global South who are, once again, objectified as sources for capital accumulation. Today, in fact, it is possible to identify a new variant of 'corporate colonisation' (Kwet, 2019, p. 6) given the assortment of USbased Big Tech companies colonising digital technology by dominating the following functions:

search engines (Google); web browsers (Google Chrome); smartphone and tablet operating systems (Google Android,  Apple  iOS);  desktop  and  laptop  operating  systems  (Microsoft  Windows);  office  software  (Microsoft Office,  Google G Suite); cloud infrastructure and services (Amazon, Microsoft, Google, IBM); social networking platforms (Facebook, Twitter); transportation (Uber, Lyft); business networking (Microsoft LinkedIn); streaming video  (Google  YouTube,  Netflix,  Hulu);  and  online  advertising  (Google,  Facebook) -among  others  (Kwet, 2019, p. 6)

Central to Kwet's analysis is the understanding that 'digital colonialism' is rooted in the ability of Big Tech to control the 'three core pillars of the digital ecosystem: software (the instructions defining and determining what the computer can actually do), hardware (the physical equipment) and network connectivity  (the  protocols  in  place  enabling  computers  to  connect  and  communicate  with  each other). U.S.  domination of code, and 'other forms of digital architecture' is functioning to denude other countries of sovereignty' (Kwet, 2019, p. 8).

Such control percolates down to the level of individual users because the prevalent coded logic constrains and moulds online experiences. In a more encompassing sense, AI as presently owned and constructed 'reinforces the hegemony of Western values and epistemologies that marginalise non-Western alternatives' (Muldoon &amp; Wui, 2023, p. 13). Whilst not grounded in a crude rejection of  AI  technology,  Abeba Birhane's (2020,  p. 391)  critical  analysis  echoes  Kwet's  work arguing  that in  the  'age  of  algorithms'  domination  may  not  solely  be  exerted  through  brute  physical  force  as in the days of older -yet far from extinguished -forms of colonialism and imperialism (Goldstein &amp;  Stockton,  2026).  Along  the  same  lines,  the  Guatemalan  activist  and  scholar,  Renata  Ávila  Pinto, refers to 'digital colonialism' as the 'deployment of imperial power over a vast number of people,

<!-- image -->

which takes the form of rules, designs, languages, cultures and belief systems serving the interests of dominant powers' (in Muldoon &amp; Wui, 2023, p. 6). She charges that hegemony is no longer organised and sustained by colonising states, but by 'technology empires' sustained by favourable trade agreements and in control  of  'digital  infrastructures,  data  and  the  ownership  of  computational  power' (Ávila Pinto in Muldoon &amp; Wui, 2023, p. 6). Mohamed et al. (2020, p. 665) prefer the concept of 'algorithmic  coloniality'.  More  generally,  the  'decolonial  lens  looks  beyond  the  standard,  immaterial depiction  of  AI  and  draws  attention  to  its  material  infrastructure,  ongoing  energy  consumption, complex geopolitics and the long histories that underpin them' (Muldoon &amp; Wui, 2023, p. 8l;  see also Urquieta &amp; Dib, 2024).

This  global  dimension  to  social  inequalities  extends  to  the  material  sphere  and  the  shifting reconfiguration  of  labour,  given  that  the  work  of  labelling  and  annotating  large  volumes  of  data is  central  to  AI  and  this  is  undertaken  in  'remote  settings,  distributed  across  the  world  using online annotation platforms or within dedicated annotation companies' (Mohamed et al., 2020, p. 668). Such vital tasks are often conducted in geographical zones that afford meagre labour law protections. Mohamed et al. (2020, p. 668) also refer to how corporations located in the metropolitan heartlands often use peripheral territories to 'beta-test' software systems to identify problems that need to be addressed prior to selling  products  to  a  more  esteemed  and  lucrative  customer  base in  the  Global  North.  Not  infrequently,  such  testing  is  undertaken  with  calculated  cynicism:  for example,  supposedly  to  improve  child  welfare  interventions,  the  government  of  Aotearoa/New Zealand  initially  experimented  with  a  predictive  algorithms  system  on  Māori  populations  with  a long  history  of  having  children  removed  from  their  care  because  of  settler  colonialism  and  institutional racism (Keddell, 2015; see also Garrett, 2002). Within the political sphere, Cambridge Analytica (CA) chose to beta-test and develop algorithmic tools for elections in Nigeria and Kenya with the aim of later using its software tools in U.K. and U.S. elections. The two African states were handpicked because they both had 'weaker data protection laws compared to CA's base of operations' in the UK (Mohamed et al., 2020, p. 669).

Aligned,  therefore,  with  the  sentiments  of  those  responsible  for  the AI  Decolonial  Manyfesto (Manyfesto, 2021) this article  recognises that  the  apparently  'irrefutable  'algorithmic  truths''  conjured  by  the  creators  of  AI  can  be  weaponised  to  marginalise  'other  systems  of  knowledge  production  and  other  visions'  which  are  'denied  and  erased'  along  with  'other  peoples'  agency, autonomy' and efforts of contestation (Manyfesto, 2021). It is, therefore, vital for the field of social work to be attuned to some of the issues identified by bringing the profession into conversation with the critical scholarship that is now beginning to interrogate the uses of AI during a period of neoliberal hegemony and expansive digital colonialism.

## Conclusion

Lindgren (2023, p. 1) maintains that 'undoubtedly, scholarship interested in making AI ethical and responsible  is  thriving'  yet  'much  of  the  work  that  is  done  along  these  lines  is  not  critical enough'. This short article, broadly rooted in social work concerns, tends to share this perception. Moreover, one of the emerging and often uncritically interrogated themes, slowly rising to prominence in mainstream discourses, is that the whole AI corporate enterprise, reflected in the fortunes of the currently loss-making OpenAI, may also be producing a 'bubble' that is likely to burst (Milmo, 2025).

Leaving such, not entirely unwarranted speculation aside, this contribution has highlighted how the present political and cultural centrality of AI may pose the threat of social work becoming even more  technicist  and  procedural  (Garrett,  2025b).  More  substantially,  we  have  dwelt  on  how  this rapidly expanding technology risks being deployed to manage, and even, magnify social and economic  inequalities.  Of  prime  concern  is  how  the  purveyors  of  AI  might -using  the  language  of 'efficiency' and 'smart solutions' -aid the neoliberal administration of impoverishment by helping to  better  ration  services.  The  article  explored  how  AI-assisted  predictive  analytics  is  presently

being  used  in  the  areas  of  child  protection,  welfare  systems  and  policing.  Turning  to  the  Global South, it was argued that the way that AI is being developed and used is likely to increase forms of digital colonialism. All these themes warrant, of course, further research and critical interrogation. In this context, empirical investigations of the transitions underway are vital.

Globally, the spread of AI is likely to have profound and uncertain ramifications for social work (Garrett, 2025b). Nevertheless, whilst needing to be reflectively distrustful of facile and uncritically upbeat perspectives, there is a need to be wary of simply accepting the notion that AI will result in catastrophe. Here, the danger is that such thinking may have the 'effect of deterring any engagement in the present' and of rendering us passive, undermining efforts to productively productively shape AI in a way in a way that bestows benefits on all (Aradau &amp; Bunz, 2022, p. 12). For this to occur, however, there needs to be a thorough transformation ushering in an economic and social order that aspires to place people before profit.
