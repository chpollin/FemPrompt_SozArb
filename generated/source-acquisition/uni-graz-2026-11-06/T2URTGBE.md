## Using ChatGPT for Qualitative Social Media Analysis: Insights for Cross-Cultural Social Work

Michin Hong Indiana University Grace Yi California State University Fullerton

ABSTRACT Objectives: This study explored the feasibility of using ChatGPT for thematic analysis of qualitative social media data in a cross-cultural context. We evaluated ChatGPT ' s coding quality and considered the ethical implications of its use in social work research. Method: We randomly selected 30 social media posts from a larger dataset of 869 Korean-language posts about dementia. Posts were made in 2025 -2023 in MissyUSA, a large Korean American online community. We conducted a comparative analysis of human and ChatGPT coding across three domains: linguistic and cultural sensitivity, coding consistency, and depth of interpretation. Ethical considerations were then reviewed. Results: ChatGPT showed strong linguistic and cultural sensitivity, successfully translating informal Korean language posts into English. However, human researchers with expert knowledge and understanding of cultural context were better able to capture nuanced and layered meanings. Inconsistencies appeared in ChatGPT ' s coding depending on which researcher entered the prompt, possibly in /uniFB02 uenced by prior interactions with the AI. Although ChatGPT sometimes offered deeper insights, it also risked overinterpretation and occasional hallucination. Two main ethical concerns emerged: (a) data privacy and con /uniFB01 dentiality and (b) bias and positionality. Conclusions: ChatGPT may be a useful supplemental tool for qualitative research with language minorities. Nonetheless, its limitations and ethical risks require careful human oversight.

## Application of Arti /uniFB01 cial Intelligence in Social Work

There is growing interest in the application of analytic or generative AI technologies in social work. Current literature highlights AI use in child welfare services, particularly in needs assessment, case management, and decision-making support. For example, the Allegheny Family Screening Tool uses machine learning to analyze hundreds of administrative data variables, estimate risk, and support case prioritization. By integrating behavioral health and criminal justice records, it assigns greater weight to cases involving infants and young children than those involving adolescents (Kawakami et al., 2022). Similarly, risk assessment tools have used arti/uniFB01 cial neural networks and natural language processing to predict child abuse and neglect using public data sources (Ciobanu, 2023).

AI is also being explored in clinical and educational contexts. One study identi/uniFB01 ed bene /uniFB01 ts and challenges of using OpenAI ' s Assistant API and the GPT-4 model in a chatbot integration to deliver narrative therapy through a WhatsApp-based platform (Chan et al., 2025). In social work education, AI technologies have contributed to enhancing pedagogy in regions like Zimbabwe through tools such as translation services, intelligent search, virtual simulations, and adaptive learning platforms (Revesai et al., 2024).

Despite these advancements, practitioners often report negative experiences and signi /uniFB01 cant resistance to AI integration (Wassal et al., 2024). While recognizing AI ' s potential to complement practice, they express concerns about its lack of transparency and technical limitations (Kawakami et al., 2022). As Reamer (2023) suggested, without a comprehensive framework for professional accountability and client welfare, the active incorporation of AI technologies into social work practice and research is likely to remain stalled.

## Large Language Models in Qualitative Research

Although limited, the emerging literature on AI tools in qualitative research can be categorized into two primary areas of focus. One line of research examines the conceptual potential of LLMs to enhance qualitative research. These studies often position LLMs as novel tools that can reduce the cognitive and logistical demands associated with qualitative data analysis, including transcription, coding, and thematic development. Given the rigor, iterative nature, and subjectivity of thematic analysis, AI tools are viewed as promising supports that may improve ef /uniFB01 ciency and consistency across stages of data analysis (Wachinger et al., 2024). The most common suggestions for their use in qualitative research fall into two areas: (a) assisting in the generation of preliminary codes (V. V. Lee et al., 2024; Nguyen-Trung, 2025; Patton et al., 2023; Turobov et al., 2024) and (b) reviewing or validating codes to support consistency and mitigate researcher subjectivity during coding (V. V. Lee et al., 2024; Y. Yang &amp; Ma, 2025).

A second line of research evaluates the feasibility of AI models through case studies or small-scale comparisons with traditional human coding. For example, Ş en et al. (2023) conducted a case study assessing ChatGPT4 ' s performance in thematic analysis and concluded that the AI model could serve as a supplemental tool, aiding but not replacing human judgment. Comparative studies have highlighted both the strengths and limitations of AI tools in this context: Although AI tools can support validation and improve ef /uniFB01 cacy (Ferrag &amp; Bentousi, 2024; V. Although they noted similarities between ChatGPT4-generated and human-coded themes, their /uniFB01 ndings emphasized the AI models ' limited capacity for contextual interpretation.

## Cross-Cultural Social Work Research

Social work emphasizes cultural competence as an essential professional skill set (National Association of Social Work, 2026). In this sense, cross-cultural research

is a critical pillar of social work, providing the necessary evidence to address persistent health and social disparities among marginalized populations. In the United States, research involving participants with limited English pro /uniFB01 ciency presents signi /uniFB01 cant methodological challenges, particularly during data collection and analysis. Because such studies are often conducted in participants ' native languages rather than English, researchers must take additional steps to ensure data accuracy and validity. These steps typically include transcribing the spoken language, translating the transcription into English, and validating the translated content with bilingual/bicultural experts from diverse cultural backgrounds (Clark et al., 2017). Although these steps are necessary to preserve data integrity, they are exceptionally time consuming and resource intensive, often creating barriers that prevent researchers from engaging with language-minority populations. This exclusion inadvertently reinforces the invisibility of culturally speci /uniFB01 c needs within social work literature and practice.

In this context, generative AI tools may have promising potential. Such tools are increasingly recognized as valuable assets for enhancing cross-cultural communication, particularly through advanced capabilities in language translation and contextual understanding. Unlike earlier rule-based machine translation systems that rely largely on word-for-word substitution (Monti et al., 2011), generative AI models such as ChatGPT or Google ' s Gemini can produce more contextually accurate and culturally sensitive translation by accounting for tone, idiomatic expressions, and conversational intent (Fu &amp; Liu, 2024). Beyond translation, these AI tools can also facilitate intercultural dialogue, helping users navigate complex social cues, etiquette, and culturally speci /uniFB01 c communication styles (Tahara, 2024). As such, generative AI can potentially support cross-cultural research by making data more accessible across languages and helping researchers to capture deeper layers of meaning embedded in culturally rich expressions.

Despite the increasing attention given to generative AI tools in social work practice and research, existing literature is limited in several ways. First, cross-cultural empirical research on AI use in social work remains scarce despite the profession ' s emphasis on cultural responsiveness and ethical engagement with vulnerable populations. Second, most prior studies on AI tools in qualitative research focus on Englishlanguage data, raising concerns about their cross-cultural applicability. Lastly, little research has examined the use of generative AI tools for analyzing large-scale, unstructured data such as social media content, despite growing interest in this data source for understanding community experiences, needs, and disparities.

## Current Study

To address this gap, the present study explores the feasibility of using a generative AI LLM, ChatGPT, for thematic analysis of qualitative social media data within a cross-cultural context. We selected ChatGPT for its widespread popularity and accessibility to social work researchers, practitioners, and students, who often face

barriers to adopting costly, specialized qualitative software (John &amp; Johnson, 2000). Unlike traditional qualitative data analysis tools such as NVivo or ATLAS.ti, which typically require some level of methodological and software-speci /uniFB01 c training and familiarity with structured coding procedures, ChatGPT ' s conversational interface supports a more inclusive approach to research in resource-limited settings (Patton et al., 2023). Its advanced multilingual capabilities also offer a unique opportunity to bridge the language and cultural gap in social work research by facilitating the analysis of culturally rich, non-English data (Fu &amp; Liu, 2024). This study speci /uniFB01 cally focuses on social media posts written in Korean by Korean Americans. It is guided by the following two research questions:

1. What is the quality of coding generated by ChatGPT for qualitative data in a cross-cultural context?
2. What ethical and practical considerations arise when using generative AI for qualitative data analysis?

## Method

## Study Sample

Data were from MissyUSA, a large online community primarily serving Korean American married women. This forum is widely used for information exchange and mutual support among this ethnic group, and posts are predominantly written in Korean. For this study, data were collected from the publicly accessible ' Life Q&amp;A ' section, which does not require membership. Weretrieved posts made fromJanuary 2023 to February 2023 containing the keywords ' dementia, ' ' Alzheimer ' s, ' and ' senility ' in Korean. Data collection occurred in March -April 2023. After screening and removing duplicate or irrelevant posts, a total of 869 posts (146 main posts and 726 replies) were included. We consulted with the California State University, Fullerton, Institutional Review Board, which advised that review was unnecessary for this project because the data used were limited to publicly available, anonymous social media posts.

To facilitate AI-based coding, 30 main posts and 106 associated replies were randomly selected using a random number generator. This sample was chosen to allow for a detailed, line-by-line comparison between human and AI coding while ensuring thematic saturation. Analysis of these 30 units revealed no new themes or codes, indicating that saturation had been reached (Guest et al., 2006; Hennink &amp; Kaiser, 2022). Therefore, no additional data were selected, as the existing sample provided suf /uniFB01 cient depth to address our research questions. All posts were written in Korean. On average, each post received 3.5 replies (range: 0 -11).

## Procedures

Weused a comparative approach to address Research Question 1, analyzing humangenerated codes alongside those produced by ChatGPT. This approach allowed for a

structured assessment of ChatGPT ' s capacity to perform qualitative analysis in a linguistically and culturally nuanced context.

The comparative analysis was conducted in /uniFB01 ve steps, adapted from Braun and Clarke (2006, 2019). First, two bilingual and bicultural researchers independently performed open coding of the Korean data in English. This direct coding preserved linguistic and cultural nuances by eliminating the need for translation. Second, the researchers reviewed and compared their open codes through a collaborative consensus-building process. Rather than calculating a quantitative reliability coef /uniFB01 -cient, the team engaged in iterative discussions to identify and resolve discrepancies in interpretation. This process involved line-by-line comparisons, critical re /uniFB02 ection on cultural and linguistic nuances, and repeated cross-checking of the emerging codes. Coding disagreements were discussed until a shared conceptual agreement was reached on all codes and themes. This re /uniFB02 ective, consensus-based approach ensured that the /uniFB01 ndings were grounded in a uni /uniFB01 ed interpretation of the data (Braun &amp;Clarke, 2019), re /uniFB02 ecting both the researchers ' expertise and the culturally speci /uniFB01 c contexts of the posts.

Third, the same set of Korean-language posts was input into ChatGPT-3.5 (free version) using two standardized prompts: ' Provide the main themes in English ' and/or ' Provide the key summaries in English. ' To re /uniFB02 ect the exploratory nature of the study, only these identical prompts were used, without providing any additional contextual instructions. With this strategy, we anticipated that the ChatGPT coding outputs would be consistent across researchers. Fourth, each researcher independently reviewed the ChatGPT outputs. Finally, in the /uniFB01 fth step, we conducted a comparative thematic analysis to examine similarities and differences between human and ChatGPT-generated codes.

Guided by prior research on quality standards in qualitative research (e.g., Nowell et al., 2017; O ' Conner &amp; Joffe, 2020), the comparative analysis focused on three key domains: (a) linguistic and cultural sensitivity, (b) coding consistency, and (c) depth of interpretation. Linguistic and cultural sensitivity refers to the extent to which the AIgenerated codes captured the linguistic nuances and cultural meanings embedded in the Korean-language data. In cross-cultural qualitative research, sensitivity to language and cultural context is essential for valid interpretation (Squires, 2009; Van Nes et al., 2010). Coding consistency evaluates the extent to which ChatGPT generated comparable codes for each researcher when identical prompts were entered using the same version of the tool. This criterion is derived from the concept of inter-coder agreement (O ' Connor &amp; Joffe, 2020) and is particularly relevant given ChatGPT ' s generative variability, which can yield different outputs depending on user interaction history (M. Lee et al., 2025; X. Yang et al., 2022).

Depth of interpretation assesses the extent to which ChatGPT-generated themes re /uniFB02 ect nuanced and meaningful insights rather than super /uniFB01 cial summaries. Highquality qualitative analysis requires attention to underlying meaning, contextual

complexity, and subject-matter familiarities (Kessler et al., 2022; Lim, 2025). Given ChatGPT ' s lack of lived experiences or domain expertise, we evaluated whether its outputs could approximate the interpretive depth expected in rigorous qualitative research. Together, these three domains provide a structural framework for evaluating the strengths and limitations of the AI-assisted qualitative coding.

To address Research Question 2, the two researchers re /uniFB02 ected on the research process, outcomes, and ethical and practical considerations during and after data coding and analysis.

## Research Question 2: Ethical and Practical Considerations Data Privacy and Con /uniFB01 dentiality

The integration of generative AI tools into qualitative research heightens concerns regarding privacy and con /uniFB01 dentiality. This is particularly relevant to social work, where studies often involve deeply personal narratives and require stringent ethical standards. In the present study, data were from a publicly accessible forum visible to anyone, regardless of membership status. To minimize potential ethical concerns, we obtained approval for data use from the website administrator. All posts were shared anonymously with the public, and we were unable to trace any data back to individual users. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that although the dataset lacks direct personal identi /uniFB01 ers, it may still contain potentially sensitive content, such as geographic references (e.g., state or county names), mentions of health conditions, and relational or familial contexts of the users. In typical qualitative social work research, which often relies on in-depth interviews and close observation of underrepresented populations, standards for data privacy and con /uniFB01 dentiality must be even higher.

When using AI for qualitative data analysis, another unresolved question concerns whether and how data entered into AI systems are stored and used, either to train models or to generate future outputs tailored to speci /uniFB01 c users. Data uploaded for transcription or coding may reside on AI company servers without the study participants ' knowledge or consent, and the use of voice or video recordings could exacerbate these concerns because they contain identi /uniFB01 able features that are more dif /uniFB01 cult to fully deidentify than text-based data. Even when data are deidenti /uniFB01 ed, researchers lose control once information is input into AI systems, and generative models may reproduce elements of the training data in unpredictable ways, posing serious implications for participant privacy and con /uniFB01 dentiality.

## Bias and Positionality

Generative AI tools produce outputs based on both user input and their underlying training data, which introduces a risk of producing misleading interpretations of participants ' experiences, intentions, or behaviors. In qualitative research, it is widely expected that researchers disclose their positionality, including their background, identity, beliefs, and standpoint, as these factors in /uniFB02 uence all stages of the research process. Re /uniFB02 exive acknowledgment helps clarify how researchers ' perspectives may shape interpretations of the data and suggestions, which can enhance transparency and trustworthiness (Holmes, 2020). In this study, the research team consists of two middle-aged Korean American female researchers who were trained

in gerontology and social work in the United States. Both researchers are bilingual and have lived substantial portions of their lives in both Korea and the United States; they bring bicultural and bilingual sensitivity to the study of aging, including policy and service contexts in both countries. This positioning allows them to analyze language nuances and system-level barriers in care for older adults in the United States and South Korea.

In contrast, generative AI models such as ChatGPT lack /uniFB01 xed identities. Their outputs depend on training data and the user ' s prompts, and they cannot re /uniFB02 ect their own assumptions or acknowledge biases. The concept of AI ' positionality ' is therefore inherently ambiguous, as outputs are shaped by probabilistic language patterns re /uniFB02 ecting preexisting training data and the framing of user instructions written in prompts. Without contextual awareness or subjective insight, AI tools can produce generic or biased analyses, especially when used for theme development in qualitative research. Even when users assign ChatGPT a speci /uniFB01 c role or persona through prompting, control over the outputs ' unintended bias is limited.

## Discussion

Our study examined the feasibility of using the generative AI tool ChatGPT for qualitative social media analysis involving a language minority population. By comparing human and ChatGPT-generated coding across linguistic and cultural sensitivity, coding consistency, and depth of interpretation, we found that both approaches identi/uniFB01 ed similar themes in longer, more detailed posts. ChatGPT often produced more expansive outputs; however, its performance declined for short, highly contextual or nuanced posts, sometimes generating inaccurate, irrelevant, or overinterpreted themes. Although ChatGPT demonstrated strong translation capabilities, even in the presence of informal language, typographical errors, and colloquialisms, human researchers -drawingontopicalknowledgeandculturalexpertise -werebetterable to capture layered meanings and contextual subtleties. Variability in ChatGPT outputs across identical prompts from the two researchers further suggests instability in coding consistency, potentially in /uniFB02 uenced by interaction history or probabilistic generation.

Two central ethical considerations emerged: (a) data privacy and con /uniFB01 dentiality and (b) bias and positionality. Although privacy risks were minimized in this study duetotheuseofpubliclyaccessible data, these concerns remain especially salient in social work research that involves direct participant engagement. Researchers can enhance protections by rigorously deidentifying data prior to AI input, generalizing sensitive geographic or relational references, and using platform settings that prevent uploaded data from being retained for model training (Reamer, 2023). Informed consent procedures should explicitly disclose how AI tools will be used and how data maybestored or processed (Goldkind et al., 2024), reinforcing social work ' s ethical commitments to transparency, participant protection, and social justice.

Generative AI also raises concerns related to algorithmic bias. AI outputs re /uniFB02 ect probabilistic patterns in training data and can reproduce historical inequities or culturally dominant assumptions (Garkisch &amp; Goldkind, 2025; Joseph, 2025; Kapur et al., 2025). Although AI systems cannot hold positionality or re /uniFB02 exive awareness, they may serve as an external analytic tool to highlight alternative interpretations or dissenting themes, which can help researchers identify and check their own biases (Morgan, 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). However, outputs must be critically evaluated by human researchers, as AI suggestions are not inherently neutral and may reinforce systematic biases if unexamined.

AI ' s utility has been rapidly increasing and varies across the qualitative research spectrum. However, given the operational characteristics of generative AI, it is best suited for realist or postpositivist approaches, such as content analysis, descriptive coding, or discourse analysis, where identifying patterns, keywords, or trends is the goal. For constructivist or phenomenological research, which heavily relies on the co-construction of meaning and re /uniFB02 exivity, AI ' s capabilities are limited. Researchers should integrate AI outputs critically, using them as a supplement to rather than a replacement for human judgment.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we used ChatGPT-3.5, which was the most accessible version at the time of the study but is now considered outdated. Newer models, such as GPT-4, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5, or Gemini demonstrate signi /uniFB01 cantly improved capabilities in translation, summarization, and linguistic nuance. Consequently, the speci /uniFB01 c technical inaccuracies observed in ChatGPT-3.5 may not fully generalize to these more advanced systems. Second, specialized qualitative software, such as ATLAS.ti, has recently incorporated AI-assisted features with enhanced analytic structure and data security (Williamson et al., 2025). Therefore, our /uniFB01 ndings maynotfullyre /uniFB02 ect the capabilities of these newer, domain-speci /uniFB01 c platforms. Third, the restricted prompt design may have limited analytic depth, as AI outputs are highly sensitive to prompt structure. More iterative or adaptive prompting strategies may yield richer results. Fourth, this study focused exclusively on Korean-language data; /uniFB01 ndings may not generalize to other languages, particularly given Korean ' s reliance on implicit meaning and honori /uniFB01 cs (Brown, 2015). Finally, although the sample of 30 posts was suf /uniFB01 cient to achieve thematic saturation and enable detailed line-byline comparison, it may have limited the identi /uniFB01 cation of rarer themes or highly nuancedexpressions. Larger or purposively sampled datasets could extend these /uniFB01 ndings.

## Implications

Despite these limitations, this study offers valuable and timely implications for social work research and education. Most importantly, it provides a baseline for understanding the use of AI-assisted tools in cross-cultural qualitative research, highlighting

both their potential and limitations. Because generative AI technologies are rapidly evolving, periodic replication of similar studies using newer models is essential for assessing how technical advancements in /uniFB02 uence qualitative analytic processes and ethical risks. Establishing such baseline studies allows the /uniFB01 eld to track changes over time rather than assuming that improved model performance automatically resolves interpretive, cultural, or ethical challenges.

Second, generative AI tools such as ChatGPT could be best used as supplements rather than sole sources of analysis. Although they offer clear ef /uniFB01 ciency bene /uniFB01 ts, reducing time spent on labor-intensive analysis even with large datasets, they demonstrate notable quality limitations. AI may overanalyze content or misinterpret meaning when faced with short, nuanced posts or long threads, re /uniFB02 ecting its limited capacity for contextual understanding. Moreover, our /uniFB01 ndings (e.g., Quote 6, online Table S1) indicate that the AI models may exhibit ' hallucination ' during data analysis. Hallucination refers to AI-generated outputs that include fabricated, exaggerated, or inferential content not grounded in the original data, arising from model architecture and training limitations (Sun et al., 2024). In this study, hallucinations were most evident when ChatGPT overinterpreted short or context-dependent posts, introduced themes not explicitly present, or assigned disproportionate weight to illustrative examples.

These tendencies underscore that generative AI tools are most effective when used to support rather than replace human judgment. Consistent with prior research, AI can assist in developing preliminary coding schemes, re /uniFB01 ning categories, or validating analytic decisions by cross-checking human interpretations (V. V. Lee et al., 2024; Nguyen-Trung, 2025; Patton et al., 2023; Turobov et al., 2024; Y. Yang &amp; Ma, 2025). However, human researchers must retain full responsibility for evaluating AI outputs, identifying hallucinations, and ensuring that interpretations remain grounded in participants ' expressed meanings.

AI tools can also serve as additional coders when provided with carefully designed prompts. In qualitative research, multiple coders are recommended to enhance objectivity (Church et al., 2019). However, this is not always feasible, especially when working with language minority populations. Given their demonstrated linguistic and cultural sensitivity, AI tools could serve as supplemental coders. To maximize reliability, researchers should iteratively re /uniFB01 ne prompts, compare AI-generated coding with human coding on a subset of the data, and report intercoder reliability across all coder combinations. Importantly, researchers should prede /uniFB01 ne how AI tools will be used, documenting procedures in study protocols and reporting procedures clearly in both the methods and results sections.

This study also has important implications for social work education. Faculty teaching research methods should introduce AI tools not solely as ef /uniFB01 ciency boosters but as subjects of critical ethical inquiry. Students must learn to design rigorous prompts, perform human-in-the-loop veri /uniFB01 cation (i.e., systematic human review of

AI-generated output) to mitigate hallucinations, and navigate the ethical complexities of algorithmic bias (Ricciardelli et al., 2026). Integrating AI-assisted data analysis into the curriculum can foster digital literacy and prepare the next generation of social work researchers to use AI tools responsibly and effectively (Ahn et al., 2025). This approach ensures that practitioners remain the primary interpreters of data, using AI to support and enhance professional judgment rather than replace it.

Institutional review boards (IRBs) in universities and other institutions conducting human-subject research must urgently develop clear policies and guidelines for studies that use AI models as data preparation or analysis tools. For example, researchers may need to disclose AI use in IRB applications. For studies involving direct data collection, the inclusion of AI tools should be addressed in informed consent documents, enabling participants to make fully informed decisions about how their data may be treated, analyzed, translated, and utilized.

More active academic discourse within the social work /uniFB01 eld is needed to fully leverage this rapidly advancing technology. Future research should extend this study byreplicating the analytic procedures with newer generative AI models and conducting direct cross-model comparisons to examine whether improvements in linguistic accuracy also translate into greater interpretive depth and ethical reliability. Such studies would help distinguish model-speci /uniFB01 c limitations from structural challenges inherent in AI-assisted qualitative analysis. In addition, comparative studies across different languages could provide critical insights into how AI tools handle cultural and linguistic variations. Likewise, research that develops and tests guidelines for effective prompt design would strengthen the rigor and transparency of AI-assisted qualitative analysis. The social work research community should convene to discuss ethical standards for AI use with vulnerable populations while exploring innovative strategies to leverage these tools to advance the /uniFB01 eld ' s knowledge base.
