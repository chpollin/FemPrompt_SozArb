## Metacognitive AI literacy: going beyond the AI skills gap agenda

<!-- image -->

Hanne Shapiro a ,  Manuel Souto-Otero b and  Richard Watermeyer c

<!-- image -->

a Centre for Higher Education Transformations, School of Education, University of Bristol, Bristol, UK; b School of Education, University of Bristol, Bristol, UK; c Centre for Higher Education Transformations, School of Education, University of Bristol, Bristol, UK

## ABSTRACT

While  use  of  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  proliferates  across  educational, professional,  and  civic  domains,  conventional  AI  literacy  frameworks remain  anchored  in  functional  skills  acquisition  and  fail  to  address  the fundamental epistemological challenges posed by probabilistic, opaque algorithmic systems. This article argues that AI literacy must be reconceptualised  as  a  metacognitive  social  practice  that  transcends individual  competencies  to  encompass  collective  capacity  for  critical engagement with AI design, deployment, and governance. We examine how the probabilistic  nature  and  epistemic  opacity  of  contemporary  AI systems  -  particularly  large  language  models  -  render  traditional  rulebased digital literacy paradigms obsolete. Drawing on case studies from higher education institutions and the Nordic financial sector, we illustrate how participatory co-design processes and experimental pedagogical spaces can cultivate metacognitive awareness and democratic  agency  in  AI-mediated  environments.  Our  analysis  reveals that efficacious AI literacy development requires institutional transformation:  universities  must  evolve  beyond  skills  transmission  to become sites of collective intelligence where learners interrogate algorithmic power structures and mobilise alternative AI futures. Framing AI literacy as a critical social practice positions higher education  as  essential infrastructure for civic  resilience,  demanding fundamental reconfiguration of university missions, governance structures, and pedagogical approaches to anchor AI literacy as a public good rather than a private asset.

## 1. Introduction

As artificial intelligence (AI) rapidly permeates education, work, and civic life (Chatterji et al. 2025; Gallup 2025; Souto-Otero and Brown 2024), uncertainty persists not only about how AI should be used, but also about what it means to be literate in relation to systems that increasingly mediate decision-making, knowledge production, and social participation. In policy circles, AI is increasingly presented as a strategic lever for national competitiveness, with AI skills framed as the critical means of ensuring workforce readiness. Initiatives such as America's AI Action Plan 2025 (United States Government 2025), the UK's AI Opportunities Action Plan 2025 (UK Government 2025), and Singapore's AI for the Public Good (Smart Nation Singapore 2023) illustrate how AI literacy is tied to ambitions of economic growth, digital inclusion, and democratic resilience.

## KEYWORDS

Generative AI and cognition; AI literacy; metacognition; higher education; participatory design; algorithmic governance; critical  pedagogy

<!-- image -->

AI literacy is commonly understood as 'the ability to understand, use, monitor, and critically reflect on AI applications without necessarily being able to develop AI models themselves' (Laupichler et al. 2022, 1; Long, Blunt, and Magerko 2021), although AI technical development capabilities  are  also  introduced  in  AI  competency  frameworks  (Chee,  Ahn,  and  Lee  2025).  However, existing conceptualisations remain fragmented and underdeveloped within educational research, often privileging functional and technical dimensions over broader epistemic and pedagogical concerns (Ng et al. 2021). Digital literacy frameworks prescribe the ability to 'access, manage, understand, integrate, communicate, evaluate, manage safety, problem solve, create information' through digital technologies (UNESCO 2018). This is not far apart from definitions of digital literacy, which focus on the ability to understand and use information in multiple digital formats (Gilster 1997), and  to  critically  analyse  that  information,  as  well  as  digital  technologies  themselves  and  their specific applications (Pangrazio, Godhe, and Ledesma 2020). The operational, social, and evaluative knowledge and skills necessary to develop informed, critical, and engaged users and producers are also  part  of  most  definitions  of  media  literacy  and  social  media  literacy  (Livingstone  2013,  1; Polanco-Levicán and Salvo-Garrido 2022; Potter 2022).

Two main critiques have been raised in relation to the definition of these different types of literacy. Wuyckens, Landry, and Fastrez (2022) highlighted overlaps between them. They also note that  definitions  for  each  of  these  terms  have  proliferated,  which  are  somewhat  different  in  their specific  constitutive  elements  (e.g.,  specific  skills  that  they  include).  This  has  led  to  a  good  deal of  confusion.  Second, Bawden (2008) has argued, digital literacy should not be reduced to a set of functional skills and competences, underlining the importance of the socio-cultural context of technology to understand literacy.

In this article, we argue that the characteristics of the technology also need to be considered when defining what being literate is in relation to that technology or set of technologies. Building on this argument, we show that mainstream definitions of digital literacy do not sufficiently capture the radical  changes  brought  about  by  the  development  of  AI  technologies.  More  specifically,  we argue that existing AI literacy frameworks, while advancing foundational competencies, have little to say about the importance of metacognitive awareness and the governance models of AI usage. A central  associated  thesis,  linked  to  Bawden's  arguments,  is  that  AI  literacy  depends  on  environments that enable users to engage as co-designers of AI solutions, thereby shaping their practices.

A lack of clarity on the changes brought about by AI literacy is problematic in several respects. For learners and workers, it increases the risk of over-reliance on opaque systems, deskilling, and reduced capacity to contest algorithmic decisions that shape key social processes such as assessment, recruitment, or service access. For educators, it translates into fragmented and limiting curricular responses - often tool training or integrity management. Finally, for policymakers, it risks directing public investment towards short-term workforce readiness rather than longer-term institutional capability and democratic resilience. In this sense, lack of clarity about AI literacy is not merely a pedagogical problem but a governance problem that redistributes risks downward to individuals while leaving wider structural issues insufficiently examined.

A shift  in  our  understanding  of  AI  literacy  as  more  than  an  individual  skillset  has  profound implications for the role of higher education in developing it. This role needs to go beyond skill development towards the cultivation of participatory and pedagogical spaces where learners experiment with co-designing alternative AI solutions and governance models. By embedding dialogic, reflexive, and interdisciplinary practices, higher education can develop metacognitive AI literacy, not only as a mode of critical reflection, but also as a civic practice that enables individuals and communities to shape the trajectories of AI - see Section 2.

The article is based on a narrative literature review and a largely conceptual approach. Rather than seek exhaustive coverage through linear systematic review protocols, the methodology used to  review  the  literature  was  iterative,  interpretive,  and  integrative  (Juntunen  and  Lehenkari 2021). Building both on the researchers' previous knowledge and targeted searches, the article synthesises diverse strands of scholarship on digital literacy, AI literacy, and AI education, to generate

new conceptual and theoretical  insights  (Baumeister  and  Leary  1997).  Searches  were  conducted across two major academic databases ( Scopus and Google Scholar ) using keywords related to artificial intelligence, digital literacy, education, and human-AI interaction. Following abstract and main text review, sources were selected primarily based on relevance and conceptual contribution, rather than  strict  inclusion  criteria,  consistent  with  the  interpretive  nature  of  narrative  reviews.  The selected  literature  was  thematically  analysed  to  identify  recurring  definitions,  frameworks,  and debates surrounding AI literacy. This approach is consistent with traditions in critical scholarship within educational technology, where the aim is to advance theoretical perspectives and provoke new  lines  of  inquiry  rather  than  to  test  predefined  hypotheses. In the remainder of the article, we present an argument for a different conceptualisation of AI literacy and discuss the implications that this has for skills development. We then illustrate alternative approaches to the development of AI literacy in practice, using case studies from higher and adult education institutions, before presenting our conclusions.

## 2. What is different about AI literacy: probabilistic reasoning and epistemic opacity

In its initial use towards the end of the nineteenth century, literacy related to the ability to decode and  encode  printed  text,  which  was  considered  increasingly  necessary  (Williams  1983).  By  the 1970s, there was a broadening of the meaning of the term, as it came to encompass the background knowledge  and  skills  necessary  to  properly  understand,  not  just  decode,  texts.  From  the  1980s onwards, the etymology of literacy shifted again to embrace a qualification of authority over epistemic domains and the understanding of the language of different subject areas - e.g., being mathematically or scientifically literate  (cf.  Stordy  2015).  This  preceded  the  articulation  of  'computer literacy',  understood  as  the  ability  to  use  computers  and  software  effectively.  By  the  late  1990s, and associated with the digital massification of knowledge, the term 'digital literacy' rapidly established (Gilster 1997).

A technicist  understanding of literacy  prevailed until  the  emergence of  'new literacy  studies', which argued for literacy as social practice (Street 2003). Challenging reductive and functionalist prescriptions, a view of literacy - organised around the theorisations of educational philosopher Paulo Freire - as a political, ethical, and emancipatory practice (cf. Freire 1970; Freire and Macedo 1987) emerged. Literacy was related to understanding texts, but also their context (reading the word and the world, including its power relations and structures of oppression), to enable transformative action. An ensuring theory of multiliteracies (cf. Selber 2004), further exceeded purely technical designations  by  proposing  literacy  to  as  a  critical  and  sociopolitically  aware  practice.  Focusing on multiliteracies and digital literacies, Selber added attention to how technologies structure action and meaning and argued that literacy is linked to the ability to produce digital texts and systems, but also to understanding how they structure power and action (rhetorical literacy). These foundational models situate AI literacy as necessarily composite and differentiated by functional, critical, and rhetorical components, yet nonetheless neglect a metacognitive dimension.

AI literacy cannot be treated as a straightforward extension of earlier digital and media literacies grounded in interaction with rule-based systems. Earlier AI relied on explicit 'if - then' or fuzzy logic rules, whereas contemporary machine learning models infer patterns from data and operate through  deep  neural  architectures  that  function  as  opaque  'black  boxes'  (Zysman  and  Nitzberg 2020).  This  opacity  erodes  users'  capacity  to  form  reliable  mental  models  of  system  behaviour, whether in low-stakes contexts such as recommendation systems or in high-stakes domains such as  hiring  or  credit  scoring  (Crawford  2021).  Generative  AI  systems,  including  large  language models, further complicate epistemic relations by producing outputs that may contain inaccuracies

<!-- image -->

or fabrications that are difficult for users to detect (Adel and Alani 2025; Bender et al. 2021). The probabilistic nature of these systems means that identical inputs may yield different outputs, and results often lack traceable explanations even to their designers. These characteristics fundamentally challenge  literacy  models  premised  on  understanding  how  tools  work  in  order  to  use  them correctly.

These epistemic challenges are amplified by the rapid diffusion of generative AI into everyday educational and workplace practices (Bick, Blandin, and Deming 2024). Epistemic opacity intensifies the risks associated with cognitive offloading, where users defer reasoning to AI systems without  sustained  evaluation.  Reliance  on  AI  for  routine  cognitive  tasks  may  erode  independent judgement  unless  individuals  develop  strategies  to  interrogate  outputs  and  regulate  when  and how reliance on machine-generated results is appropriate (Gerlich 2025). AI literacy can therefore not be conceptualised as stable mastery of tools but must involve ongoing inquiry, experimentation, and reflective calibration of human judgement in human-AI interaction, a defining feature of metacognitive AI literacy, which involves the capacity to monitor how AI reshapes reasoning, belief formation, and communication (Hutler et al. 2025). Kojukhov, Marom, and Levin (2025) argued that AI education itself must be understood as a metacognitive endeavour, requiring learners to continuously  evaluate  when  to  trust,  verify,  or  override  algorithmic  outputs  rather  than  treating them as deterministic truths. Educational and workplace environments must therefore treat AI literacy  not  only  as  reflective  critique  but  also  as  an  acting  competence,  since  reflection  without responsibility  risks  the  degradation  of  meaningful  engagement  (Haipeter  et  al.  2024;  Velander, Otero, and Milrad 2024).

Most  current  AI  literacy  frameworks  remain  anchored  in  skill-  and  knowledge-based  taxonomies. For example, Chiu et al. (2024, 2025) organise AI literacy across multiple foundational literacies (mathematical, data, ethical, and technical). While this approach systematises domains of competence,  it  continues  to  privilege  individual  proficiency  over  collective  and  participatory engagement with AI design and deployment. Laupichler et al. (2022) document fragmented and inconsistent  definitions  of  AI  literacy  across  higher  education  curricula,  while  Velander,  Otero, and Milrad (2024) showed that dominant discourses frame literacy primarily as functional adoption, neglecting civic and organisational dimensions. Many critical AI literacy frameworks extend digital  literacy  models  by  emphasising  ethical  awareness  and  risk  mitigation,  frequently  with  a reminder to 'use AI responsibly' (Biagini 2025).

Against this backdrop, and in light of the epistemic shifts introduced by probabilistic and opaque  AI  systems  (Bender  et  al.  2021;  Chiu  et  al.  2024;  Crawford  2021),  we  conceptualise  AI  literacy  as  comprising  three  analytically  distinct  but  interrelated  dimensions  (Table  1).  Building on  Selber's  (2004)  distinction  between  functional,  critical,  and  rhetorical  literacies,  we  differentiate  between  functional,  critical,  rhetorical,  and  metacognitive  AI  literacies.  Functional  AI  literacy  refers  to  the  capacity  to  operate  AI  tools  effectively  and  to  manage  basic  risks  such  as data protection and regulatory compliance. Critical AI literacy involves the ability to interrogate the socio-technical implications of AI, including bias, opacity, and institutional deployment contexts.  Rhetorical  literacy  relates  to  the  ability  to  recognise  technologies  and  texts  as  persuasive constructions,  to  interrogate  the  interests  and  values  they  embody,  and  act  as  a  critical  agent in  shaping  their  use  and  design.

Metacognitive  AI  literacy,  which  we  argue  is  insufficiently  theorised  in  existing  frameworks, refers to  the capacity to recognise epistemic uncertainty, monitor and regulate one's reliance on AI outputs, and reflect on how interaction (human-AI; human-human) shapes judgement, agency, and decision-making (Azevedo 2005; Tankelevitch et al. 2024; Winne and Hadwin 1998). It also entails the ability to participate in shaping alternative AI practices and governance arrangements, recognising that  cognitive  regulation  increasingly  depends  on  collective  and  organisational  conditions rather than solely individual competence (Bender et al. 2021; Crawford 2021).

In contrast, participatory design foregrounds questions of power and purpose, such as who benefits from AI, who shapes it, or what values are encoded in its design.

## 3. AI literacy, agency, and the capacity to co-design in the age of AI

Design choices constrain or enable metacognitive AI literacy practices. But metacognitive AI literacy requires changes at the organisational level too because it is a collective or civic practice. Thus, as noted in the previous section, metacognitive regulation in AI contexts cannot remain a purely internal cognitive  process  but  becomes  enacted  through  organisational  arrangements  that  enable  collective reflection,  contestation, and redesign. It requires embedding participatory processes where users be they learners or workers - can individually and collectively interrogate outputs, test alternative scenarios, and influence the trajectories of AI implementation. This is not a straightforward task in a  context  where  leaders  in  large  companies  are  placing  enormous  trust  in  AI.  A  recent  survey  of 1500  IT  and  business  executives  in  Australia,  France,  Germany,  Japan,  the  UK,  and  the  USA found that around 9 out of 10 managers in Australia and the UK would trust AI agents to act on behalf of their organisation in a crisis such as an outage or security incident (Wakefield Research 2025). Concurrently, the higher education sector is experimenting with AI to accelerate the processing of large numbers of student applications to make offers within minutes (Newal 2025). But when systems are epistemically  opaque,  users  can  overestimate  reliability  and  under-check  errors  -  especially  under time  pressure  (a  crisis)  or  high  workload  (Abubakar,  Jeilani,  and  Yusuf  2025;  Karamuk  2025). Work  in  education/research  contexts  shows  over-reliance  can  also  degrade  critical  thinking  and decision  quality  -  for  example,  when  AI  guidance  is  too  simplistic,  wrong,  or  hallucinated  (Passi and Vorvoreanu 2022; Zhai, Wibowo, and Li 2024; Kosmyna et al. 2025).

AI literacy is thus not only an individual capability but an organisational achievement. Organisational environments that provide users with autonomy and enable them to build competence create  the  conditions  for  developing  metacognitive  awareness  (Kawakami  et  al.  2023).  They strengthen their ability to critically engage with but also reshape AI systems, rather than rely on them passively (Chen and Wang 2025). This provides strong theoretical support for designing education and workplaces where reflective practices, feedback mechanisms, and participatory governance  actively  nurture  metacognition.  This  reflective  capacity  is  developed  through  participatory practices where users are invited to co-design, experiment with, and adapt, AI systems in context (Hingle and Johri 2025).

Participation in AI co-design processes holds the potential to position users as active agents in technological change rather than as passive recipients. This type of agency is crucial for developing what we term 'AI agential identity' - understood as a durable sense of competence, legitimacy, and responsibility in shaping human-AI collaboration rather than merely adapting to it (Reissner and Armitage-Chan  2024;  Soulami,  Benchekroun,  and  Galiulina  2024).  Co-design  of  AI  requires changes  in  organisational  design  to  create  environments  deliberately  designed  to  provide  users

<!-- image -->

with opportunities to interrogate system outputs, reflect on decision rationales, and recalibrate their own cognitive and use strategies. This can require initiatives such as time banks for the collective auditing of AI deployment and outputs, and the possibility to opt out of certain tools or uses of AI. Such  environments  also  necessitate  institutionalised  reflection  points,  dialogical  practices,  and iterative feedback loops that systematically incorporate users' insights into the continuous refinement of systems - these conditions are widely recognised in organisational research as essential for  sustaining  productivity  and  fostering  innovation  (Brynjolfsson,  Li,  and  Raymond  2023; Souto-Otero,  Brown  and  Freebody  2023).  At  the  same  time,  they  constitute  arenas  in  which struggles over job design, autonomy, and the recognition of skills are actively negotiated (Acemoglu, Autor, and Johnson 2023). The central challenge is therefore not just equipping users with technical  competences,  but  reconfiguring  education  and  work  environments  so  that  users'  practical expertise  and  critical  agency  shape  how  AI  systems  are  introduced,  used,  and  governed.  This  is not just a cognitive requirement but a condition for maintaining agency in AI-intensive environments, including at work (Tankelevitch et al. 2024).

Environments  that  support  epistemic  resilience  and  critical  voice  help  to  ensure  that  users remain functionally competent in AI but also recognised stakeholders in the negotiation of AI's role within education and labour processes. Co-design participation and user voice develop critical metacognitive skills that are essential for shaping the future directions of AI adoption. When users participate in AI design, they develop a deeper understanding of system capabilities and limitations, enabling  more  sophisticated  strategies  for  human-AI  task  allocation  and  collaboration.  This implies that AI literacy cannot be separated from an individual's lived reality and reframes AI literacy as more than knowing how to use AI tools - it is about empowering people to critically understand, question, and shape AI's role in their lives and labour.

The participatory  design  tradition,  established  by  Scandinavian  researchers  such  as  Bjerknes, Ehn,  and  Kyng  (1987),  demonstrated  that  effective  technology  integration  requires  active  user involvement  in  design  processes.  Successful  systems  emerge  from  'common  efforts  of  skilled, experienced users and design professionals', combining practical domain knowledge with technical expertise (Ehn 1988). Studies by MIT's Institute for Work and Employment Research (Kochan et al. 2024) also show that implementation models excluding frontline participation often generate mistrust and under-utilisation. Additionally, exclusion misses critical on-the-ground knowledge about where algorithms may fail or how jobs could be enriched through AI assistance.

Participatory approaches - examples of which include co-design workshops and joint oversight committees - are shown to lead to higher adoption and safer integration. The absence of such participatory models is thus not simply a human resource (HR) matter but a governance issue that shapes organisational capability AI (Haipeter et al. 2024). The realisation of value through AI at work, for example,  requires  significant  complementary  investments  in  work  redesign,  leadership  practices, and  change  management.  This  is  referred  to  as  the  dynamic  'Productivity  J-Curve'  (Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson 2021), where gains from AI emerge only after organisations adjust processes, roles, and governance mechanisms. This means that a technically skilled workforce may still be unable - or unwilling - to use AI effectively if organisational design lags behind. Resistance to AI use may not stem from a lack of skill but from contesting visions embedded in AI systems. AI is not neutral: it encodes choices about efficiency versus autonomy, surveillance versus trust, and personalisation versus privacy (Suchman 1987; Winner 2014). Taken together, this evidence suggests that framing the challenge  as  a  one-sided  'skills  gap'  obscures  the  capability  conditions  required  to  use  AI  in  ways that are empowering, legitimate, and productive (Shapiro 2025; Souto-Otero and Brown 2024). AI governance, user voice, and organisational cultures are thus central elements in discussions around AI  literacy  and  AI  use.  Yet  systematic  studies  of  organisational  AI  implementation  processes  and their outcomes remain scarce, underscoring the need for deeper inquiry in this field.

Participatory design and practices include a wide range of approaches and traditions where participant  roles  vary  from  testers  or  informants  to  designers,  and  where  power  relations  on  who frames  and  decides  on  the  terms  of  participation  cannot  be  ignored  (Robertson  and  Simonsen

<!-- image -->

2012; Vargas et al. 2025). Debates at the intersection of participatory design in AI literacy centre on whether participation functions as a mechanism of empowerment (democratising agency to question, reshape, or resist (e.g., non-adoption of AI systems)), or as a means of facilitating AI adoption (improving usability and acceptance without questioning goals and constraints). The release of beta versions of GPTs illustrates a paradox in participatory approaches, as what is framed as 'co-construction'  with  non-expert  users  often  blurs  the  line  between  inclusive  and  extractive  practices. On the one hand, beta testing mobilises a wide range of users to stress-test systems in everyday contexts,  generating  feedback  that  improves  contextual  appropriateness  while  fostering  a  sense  of involvement in shaping emerging technologies (Bødker and Kyng 2018; Ehn 2008). On the other hand,  it  effectively  outsources  systems  testing  to  unpaid  labour,  where  user  interactions  supply training signals, proof-of-concept validation, and early market development - without explicit recognition of their value (Irani 2015; Zuboff 2019).

## 4. Implications for pedagogy: competence, epistemology, and critical agency

For AI literacy, this duality is significant: beta environments may enhance users' intuitive grasp of system  affordances  and  limitations,  but  they  also  risk  naturalising  a  role  in  which  citizens  and workers  participate  as  data  sources  rather  than  as  empowered  co-designers.  This  tension  foregrounds  the  need  for  critical  AI  literacy  that  makes  visible  the  boundary  between  participatory design and the commodification of tacit human knowledge (Pangrazio and Sefton-Green 2021). Central to participatory practice as a learning environment is that it offers opportunities to collaboratively experiment with, critically reflect on, and redesign practice, thus equipping users to question AI and to decide how they interact with it, on what terms, and with what implications.

In practice, AI literacy develops not through discrete skills training but through sustained engagement with AI systems in authentic disciplinary and occupational tasks, where uncertainty, error, and value trade-offs are made visible and discussable (Deranty and Corbin 2024; Watermeyer, Shapiro, and Chen 2023). In higher education, studies show that fluent generative outputs increase risks of over-reliance and reduced epistemic vigilance unless pedagogical designs explicitly scaffold verification, source critique, and reflective calibration of trust (Brown, Sillence, and Branley-Bell 2025; Kosmyna et  al.  2025;  Zhai,  Wibowo,  and  Li  2024).  Lifelong  learning  contexts  further  require  literacy practices that support adaptive judgment across changing tools rather than mastery of specific applications, reinforcing AI literacy as a dynamic capability rather than a static competence (Chiu 2025; Tofiq and Latif 2025). In workplaces, AI literacy is shaped by organisational workflows, performance pressures, and power asymmetries, making collective practices - such as peer review of AI outputs, escalation  protocols,  and  participatory  system  design  -  critical  to  safe  and  productive  adoption (Faraj, Pachidi, and Sayegh 2018; Loaiza and Rigobon 2025). Case evidence from healthcare, financial, and legal services indicates that learning-led AI strategies combining technical training with ethical deliberation and task redesign hold the potential to support augmentation rather than substitution of  professional  judgment  (Loaiza  and  Rigobon  2025;  Maschke,  Nelson,  and  Simek  2026).  Across domains, effective  AI  literacy  therefore  depends  less  on  individual  proficiency  and  more  on  institutional  learning  architectures  that  embed  reflection,  feedback,  and  governance  into  everyday human-AI interaction (Biagini 2025; Yan, Husted, and Fath 2026).

AI literacy demands continuous experimentation and iteration, viewing technologies as evolving processes shaped by human judgement rather than fixed systems to master (Buxton 2007). If AI literacy is conceived not as a fixed skill set but as a reflective, evolving capability, the implications extend well beyond the boundaries of individual workplaces. Such literacy must be cultivated in the formative environments where professional identities, epistemic habits, and civic orientations are shaped. This places higher education at the centre of the discussion (Watermeyer et al. 2024). Universities are not only sites of advanced training; they are also civic institutions where future professionals  learn  to  navigate  uncertainty,  negotiate  values,  and  integrate  emerging  technologies into  broader  social  and  ethical  frameworks  (Watermeyer,  Shapiro,  and  Chen  2023).  To  embed

<!-- image -->

AI literacy meaningfully, then, requires rethinking higher education towards deeper commitments to epistemic responsibility.

If AI literacy requires metacognitive reflection, HEIs need to enable learners to critically 'read' the socio-technical implications of AI in work and society, and to exercise agency in reshaping how these technologies are designed and deployed. This broader orientation is echoed in recent AI literacy scholarship. Selwyn (2022, 2024) argued that digital and AI literacy agendas are overly instrumental,  tied  to  employability  and  adoption,  while  neglecting  critical  digital  citizenship,  power asymmetries, and the political economy of technology. Atenas et al. (2023) emphasised open and participatory literacies that resist enclosure by commercial platforms, while D'Ignazio and Bhargava (2020) frame data and AI literacy as feminist practices. Choi et al. (2025) similarly underline that AI literacy in both secondary and higher education must explicitly integrate ethics and power, rather than remain at the level of tool proficiency.

To embed AI literacy meaningfully, therefore, requires more than simply adding AI courses or tools  into  existing  curricula.  Learning  environments  should  be  designed  to  enable  learners  to develop the ability to monitor and adjust their strategies in response to uncertain or opaque results, fostering scientific scepticism rather than the narrow functional proficiency typical of earlier digital literacy models (Bender et al. 2021; Crawford 2021). Reframed through this lens, acquiring AI literacy becomes an emancipatory practice - one that enables learners to engage with AI critically and creatively,  within  diverse  disciplinary  and  societal  contexts.  For  example,  in  the  social  sciences, coursework  could  require  students  not  only  to  analyse  the  deployment  of  AI  tools  in  domains such as welfare administration or credit scoring, but also to interrogate their socio-technical implications for rights and equity. Beyond analysis, students might then collaborate with at-risk groups or community organisations to co-design alternative AI solutions, thereby operationalising metacognitive AI literacy as both a reflective and civic practice. Critical experiential pedagogical strategies thus have a key role to play in this kind of work.

Yet this can be challenging. As Giroux (2014) highlights, neoliberal funding regimes have shifted universities towards measurable outputs and market efficiencies, eroding conditions for democratic and critical pedagogy. Marginson (2016) described this as the globalisation of an 'efficiency logic' in higher education, where critically oriented teaching is marginalised. Analyses of the 'platform university' illustrate how teaching and research have been re-infrastructured by platforms, normalising surveillance-evaluation hybrids that crowd out critical and participatory pedagogies (Carrigan and Jordan 2022; Komljenovic et al. 2025; Pangrazio et al. 2022; Williamson 2024; Williamson, Macgilchrist, and Potter 2023). The situation is compounded by the use of AI as a stealth technology that is  integrated into day-to-day technologies such as search engines and recommendation systems, acting beneath the surface and shaping decisions, information flows, and experiences without being visible. This invisibility means that users may not question how it works, what data it uses, or what biases it carries (Watermeyer et al. 2024).

Taken together,  this  literature  strengthens  the  argument  that  unless  governance  and  funding shift from scale to depth, universities will reproduce shallow, market-conforming versions of 'AI literacy', rather than cultivate the metacognitive capacities needed. Yet, the structural reliance on cost-efficient  and  scalable  teaching  models  that  many  universities  operate  under  works  against the very dialogical, reflective, and collaborative pedagogies required for critical AI literacy. Resolving this tension requires rethinking not only curriculum design but also the structural conditions under which universities operate and organise teaching and learning (Komljenovic et al. 2025).

## 5. AI literacy in practice

This section presents examples of real-life experiences of institutions dealing with AI literacy in higher education and adult learning, to illustrate current alternative approaches to the development of metacognitive AI literacy. The cases illustrate how AI literacy can be cultivated across pedagogical  design  (supporting  learners  in  recognising  uncertainty,  analysing  errors,  and  reflecting  on

<!-- image -->

the ethical and practical consequences of human-AI interaction), organisational governance (giving  users  a  voice  in  deployment  decisions  and  accountability  mechanisms),  and  socio-technical experimentation (capturing opportunities for iterative testing and redesign of AI-supported practices).  Together, these domains operationalise metacognitive AI literacy as an institutional capability rather than an individual trait.

Various universities,  across  very  different  national  and  institutional  contexts,  have  developed interdisciplinary project-based curriculum resting on collaboration with external stakeholders on authentic socio-technical challenges around AI, such as Aalborg University's Engineering Faculty, Denmark, or Aalto University, Finland. But there are examples in very contrasting contexts, such as  from  Strathmore  University  in  Kenya.  Strathmore's  involvement  in  the  Global  Alliance  for Ethics and Impacts of AI (GAEIA) has engaged almost 200 postgraduate students across the Global North and South, framing AI literacy as socio-technical - connecting disciplines from anthropology to policy. Through challenge-based collaboration with fintechs and edtechs, AI is situated within  diverse  geopolitical  and  cultural  contexts,  exposing  how  AI's  benefits  and  risks  are unevenly distributed between emerging and advanced economies. This approach reframes AI literacy as a collective, context-aware practice. Northeastern University has built its post-2025 strategy around President Joseph Aoun's vision of a 'robot-proof' university, embedding his concept of Humanics -  an  interdisciplinary  model  combining  technological,  data,  and  human  literacies. This strategy is backed by bottom-up innovation funding, enabling faculty, students, and external partners  to  co-develop  initiatives  that  cut  across  disciplinary  boundaries  (Aoun  2024).  Stanford has invested in sandbox environments, where faculty and students can experiment with AI tools in  controlled  conditions,  balancing  innovation  with  data  security  and  ethical  safeguards.

Together, these approaches illustrate how institutions can encourage cross-boundary collaboration and experimentation to avoid shallow forms of AI literacy solely aligned to market logics.  Yet  they  also  bring  to  light  enduring  tensions  within  the  prevailing  model  of  knowledge construction in higher education. Risk of work overload, faculty caution caused by lack of time for  technological  upskilling,  and  stigma  attached  to  AI,  which  disincentivises  faculty  from  getting  involved  with  it.  This  underscores  the  challenge  of  ensuring  that  AI  enhances  rather  than undermines the cultivation of disciplinary judgment (Balalle and Pannilage 2025; Peoples 2025). While education for AI literacy should be co-constructed - grounded in dialogue, reflection, and lived  experience  -  universities  often  remain  structurally  bound  to  a  funding  model  that  favours large lecture-based instruction (Dilek, Baran, and Aleman 2025; Watermeyer, Shapiro, and Chen 2023). While pedagogical strategies that render AI visible as a socio-technical system and enable learners  to  interrogate  and  contest  AI  logics  in  genuine  settings  are  critical  to  building  AI  literacy as a civic practice, these require significant transformations in the higher education sector.

There  are  also  examples  in  the  adult  continuing  training  sector.  Shapiro  (2025)  reports  on experiences  in  the  use  of  AI  in  the  Danish  financial  services  sector  and  documents  the  use  of exploratory  value-driven  models  that  have  treated  their  implementation  as  a  facilitated  learning process. A Nordic bank's implementation of agentic AI exemplifies critical AI literacy as a social practice. A cross-disciplinary team mapped customer journeys to identify automation opportunities while preserving human judgment in advisory practices, focusing on hyper-personalisation. It used contextual data to reframe conversations around life goals rather than products. Internal hackathons  generated  use  cases  while  surfacing  critical  questions  about  ethics  and  workflows.  This approach  positioned  co-design  as  a  vehicle  for  metacognition.  Through  experimentation  cycles, error analysis, and reflective dialogue, staff developed collective routines for interrogating system behaviour  and  adapting  practices.  'Model  moments'  and  feedback  loops  created  shared  spaces for externalising reasoning and co-developing strategies for AI interaction. The pilot was embedded within a learning ecosystem featuring online courses on AI theory, prompting, GDPR, and design thinking. This integration of experimentation with structured learning opportunities demonstrates how organisations can transform implementation into collective capacity-building that questions rather than merely adopts algorithmic systems.

## 6. Conclusions

Traditional digital and emerging AI literacy frameworks do not appropriately accommodate changes brought about by AI as a probabilistic reasoning tool. AI literacy is not just an extension of digital literacy; it represents a paradigm shift. Learners must be equipped to navigate probabilistic systems and epistemological opacity, continuously question outcomes, and consider the broader impacts of AI - competencies that were marginal in prior frameworks but now need to take centre stage.

Our analysis underscores that the impact of AI on work and learning is not technologically predetermined, but contingent on human decisions in design, deployment, and governance. The prevailing narratives often oscillate between utopian promises and dystopian fears, a dichotomy that oversimplifies  a  more  complex  reality  (Nurski  2025).  A  more  critical  perspective  highlights  the need for deliberate strategies to align AI with human ends rather than treating technology as an autonomous force beyond our influence. In this sense, AI literacy is not only a technical or even educational challenge but a governance problem.

Framing AI literacy as a critical social practice transforms universities across three dimensions. Epistemically, it positions AI literacy beyond functional skills towards interrogating how algorithmic systems reshape knowledge production (Crawford 2021). Civically, it cultivates ethical agency to contest power asymmetries embedded in computational infrastructures (Eubanks 2018). Institutionally, it enables universities to reclaim legitimacy as custodians of collective intelligence rather than vendors of digital credentials (Williamson 2019). This integrated framework - linking metacognitive  awareness,  socio-technical  understanding,  and  democratic  participation  -  positions higher education to anchor AI literacy as a public good, not just a private asset.

If the conception of AI literacy we have outlined is to be meaningfully advanced, embedding it within higher education is a challenge but also an opportunity, as it would require that universities be reimagined as civic and epistemic institutions in an AI-mediated society. This is not a straightforward task, as it requires a fundamental shift in mission, governance, and funding, in a sector constrained by commodification and market competition, which promotes standardisation rather than innovation (Stanley 2020). What passes for innovation is too often confined to questions of delivery, while  the  deeper,  transformative  purposes  of  education  are  neglected.  Regulatory  pressures  and competitive mimicry further entrench resistance to meaningful change (Watermeyer et al. 2021; Watermeyer, Shapiro, and Chen 2023). Against this poverty of imagination, Staley calls for speculative,  transformative  innovation:  a  willingness  to  design  alternative  models  of  the  university capable of cultivating civic purpose and epistemic responsibility in the age of AI. Connell (2019) extends  this  argument  by  advancing  a  renewed  public-good  mission  for  higher  education,  one that re-centres democratic resilience and civic capacity against the drift towards commercial logics. These diagnoses underscore the urgency of rethinking higher education if it is to anchor the civic conception of AI literacy we propose. In this view, higher education becomes not merely a site of skills transmission but a key arena where society negotiates what kinds of AI futures are possible and desirable. The strategic task ahead is to lay the groundwork for the reimagining of universities as  institutions  that  actively  shape,  rather  than  merely  adapt  to,  the  AI-intensive  world.  Without such transformation, AI literacy risks becoming another credentialised competence disconnected from the lived realities of algorithmic governance.
