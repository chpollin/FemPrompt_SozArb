## 14 Social Work Ethics and Generative AI

Michael Balkow

## Introduction

Social work is a profession that works with people, in their homes, in hospitals with physical or mental health difficulties, seeing children in schools, and connecting  with  community  and  support  networks  in  times  when  protection,  support,  advocacy,  or  empowerment  is  needed.  Technology  provides  an  interface between this human connection, from social worker to service user, to record, document, assess, and plan. These written social work records are vital but ultimately time-  consuming, often starting off as handwritten notes to be expanded, edited, and inputted into systems and databases. Another key element of social work is the ability to think, to have the time and space to critically reflect upon work undertaken, whether through informal peer-to-peer discussion, or with more formal supervision routes, all of which require time, and critical thinking, often in fraught, emotionally charged contexts. It is perhaps unsurprising then that when technological advancement appears, which makes promises to increase productivity or reduce time-consuming administration tasks; that professions such as social work will take notice. Therefore, this chapter examines the use of generative AI in social work, the possibilities and potential, whilst paying attention to the risks, and the alteration of ethics of non-human entities when considering regulation. The scale and speed of technological advancements in AI can move at a rapid and unpredictably fast pace. The world of publishing and academic writing, with its need for diligent research, time for reflection and careful consideration, a peer review process, editing, and all the other seen and unseen facets of academia, makes AI a difficult topic to write about. What is written today may not be true tomorrow, so readers are advised to consider contexts. This is also a fundamental issue with AI, in that technology companies will be able to outrun anyone's abilities - whether organisations, the public, or governments - to carefully scrutinise or even regulate AI's usage and proliferation into our modern world. Social problems in society  will  endure,  and  social  workers  will  remain  to  help  solve these problems. However, what does change is the society around us, and social work must be malleable in a time of what Ioakimidis and Maglajlic (2025) refer to as 'fluid certainties.'

## Generative Social Work

Generative AI is now gaining traction in social work practice in the United Kingdom, currently in the form of local authority trials, as 28 councils in England are using or testing an AI tool to produce case notes from visits and assessments. This has been developed by AI company Beam, alongside social workers, to record meetings and emails, with the AI then providing the user with a summary and suggested actions for inclusion in case notes based on council-agreed prompts (Koutsounia, 2024). Beam's website promises that you can 'Hire Self-Learning AI Agents to Run Your Operations,' boasting that their services are 'Used by Fortune 500 companies and scale-ups, the  platform  automates  workflows with an advanced AI agent platform to reduce operational cost and create leverage' (Beam AI, 2025). Advertised on Microsoft's website is an article about their AI assistant Copilot and its usage in Barnsley, England where the anonymous 'Sam', a social worker enthuses:

It's been really useful for writing up calls, tidying up notes or doing minutes after a meeting and making all that a lot quicker … It's really nice having Copilot as a sort of personal secretary … It means I'm not having to concentrate quite as much on scribbling things down, so it frees up brain space to really focus - if I'm chairing a meeting, for example.

(Dawson, 2024)

The article then promotes further benefits and applications: 'The AI tool can quickly reorganise visit notes into accurate records … This saves social workers hours of analysis, so they can prioritise any urgent interventions. It can also quickly summarise large files when social workers take on a new case, presenting key information and major risk factors', followed by a rejoinder by Sam that: 'Given the importance of what we do, I do need to keep an oversight on it … But it makes a really useful start on things' (Dawson, 2024). The tone between Sam (the social worker), and the writer of the article, someone who works for Microsoft AI, bears some contradictions to the approaches. Sam is pleased to have a virtual secretary, to free up the space to really think and focus, whilst the author sees analysis of written case records as cumbersome, time-consuming and in need of AI to tidy up and organise these - presumably messy or disorganised - records, records that of course represent people's lives. Sam appears to value time to think about casework, whereas the author sees the reading of case records as unnecessary if AI can do this. We could argue that having AI transcribe a one-hour interprofessional core group meeting, as opposed to the social worker doing this, is of clear benefit to busy workers. However, critical questions arise from this: what could be lost as well as gained with this newfound efficiency? Turning notes from the one-hour meeting, into coherent minutes to share with professionals, may take time but could allow further insight, reflection and analysis, creating a more comprehensive understanding of the case. It could also follow that if extremely complex cases can be condensed and neatly summarised by AI, then there may be a temptation for managers to over-allocate workers or allocate these complex cases to less experienced workers.

Lillis, Leedham and Twiner (2020a, 2020b), drawing on their three-year ethnographically  oriented  study  exploring  contemporary  professional  social  work writing, focused on a key concern - the amount of time taken up with writing, or 'paperwork.' This research framed social work as a 'writing-intense' profession, borrowing from Brandt who defines this as a profession where 30% of time is spent on writing (Brandt, 2005). The problem of paperwork distancing social workers from the service users and communities, has never been fully addressed or solved, although there have been some solutions proposed to make social work written recording part of its educational and professional standards (Balkow and Lillis, 2019). The AI tool also hints towards being more than a mere minute-taking secretary, into something that makes 'suggestions' possibly becoming a tool that will contribute to social work assessments. This is arguably a huge leap for social workers into an unknown future - the use of non-humans in decision-making which has potentially significant implications for the profession.

## AI Futures

In 1997, Kreuger wrote a journal article titled: 'The End of Social Work', listing three factors that would lead to the demise of the profession in the next century. He defined one of these as the rise of 'Hypertechnologies', imagining a world where virtual headsets allow users to escape into enhanced realities, preferable to or improved upon their existing ones. Although today virtual reality headsets are not as ubiquitous as Kreuger anticipated, many young people spend several hours in the virtual space of social media, scrolling, clicking and liking hyper-real representations of self. Social media is an area of technology concurrent with the rise of the internet, where it can be debated the extent to which this harms the lives of young people, with psychologists like Jonathan Haidt proposing regulation and recommendations to minimise potential harm to children (Haidt, 2024). This is comparable in ways to the rise of generative AI. Just as it would be difficult to envisage a world where social media is totally removed from the lives of young people, we may soon arrive at a similar situation with AI in human-led workplaces.

It is easy to read the article today, with still at least seven decades until the end of  social  work,  with  an  air  of  cynicism  as  some  predictions  appear  far-fetched. However, the thought experiment is often a key part of literature in the development of AI and evaluating its societal impact, as exaggerated futures where AI becomes more intelligent that humans can be safely explored in the confines and safety of a book's pages (see Tegmark, 2017). The article displays some prescience with the prediction that counselling will be delivered by electronic software generated counsellors, seen in the rise of AI-powered mental health chatbots, replacing the face-to-face therapeutic relationship with an on-demand, readily available counsellor, of which multiple examples now exist (Woebot Health, 2025).

Kreuger also envisages how 'These developments will affect professional life … a vast array of new technologies will supersede human service skills as clients negotiate solutions in totally electronic personal territories' (Kreuger, 1997, p. 20).

The Covid-19 pandemic brought this to the fore in many workplaces including social work, as face-to-face visits and meetings, became wholesale conducted in the virtual spaces of Zoom, Teams or similar platforms. The residual effects of this being that many online meetings have remained, as with hybrid working models of work between home and the office (Ottmann and Noble, 2024;   Ferguson et al., 2022). Kreuger's future social work is painted with gloomy pessimism, ignoring some of the strides technology can make in enhancing people's lives. Yet, by examining critical and negative perspectives of social work futures, we can interrogate ambitious or dubious claims and propose new possibilities. For example, another more recent article of a similar profession-apocalyptic tone is damning of social works' foundational knowledge base: 'With a divided focus, we are less grounded in evidence-based interpersonal interventions than psychology, less rigorous and sophisticated than sociology, less nimble and agile than genuine community movements, and less efficient than the algorithms that have come to dominate welfare bureaucracies' (Maylea, 2021, p. 778). Again, the tone sides with pessimism, discounting the value of social work having a fluid and eclectic theory base to draw upon, the possibilities of creativity and inclusion of varied knowledge, rather than a rigid and exclusionary approach. Yet this critique could present a possibility of AI being used to improve theoretical rigour in the profession, with generative AI quickly connecting research to practice. Evidence-based practice (EBP) can be defined  as  'the  conscientious,  explicit,  and  judicious  use  of  current  best  evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients' (Hannes, 2020, p. 1). This approach is dominant in the health professions and does have more difficulties when applied to mostly non-clinical professions like social work, as critiqued rigorously by Webb (2001). The evidence base from which EBP draws upon is to be found in research papers, the implication being that practitioners must be up to date with research at all times, utilising and then applying it to practice situations. Keeping professional knowledge current is vital but consumes time, drawing practitioners away from service users. As generative AI is trained on specific types of data that aligns with its purpose, an AI social work tool could be trained on huge volumes of social work literature, allowing practitioners to type in case scenarios, with generative AI then linking relevant literature, summarising studies and recommending outcomes based on findings. This process has already taken hold in the domain of health research, whereby every major consumer technology corporation, from Google to Apple, to Facebook, Amazon, Microsoft and IBM, has moved into the health and biomedical sector, referred to as the 'Googlization of health research' (Sharon, 2018). These companies hold the advantages of large-scale and broad reach, being skilled at handling large amounts of data, often for the purposes of selling this data to advertisers. Therefore, these companies have a utility here but may impart values of profit into health research (Filippo, 2024).

A similar but more refined approach is currently being used by the ethical philosopher Peter Singer, and his AI chatbot:

Peter Singer AI is an advanced chatbot that facilitates meaningful ethical discussions. Drawing from my extensive body of work, it provides thoughtful

responses and poses challenging questions to encourage users to reflect on their experiences and beliefs. Employing the Socratic method, this AI guides users through a process of critical thinking and self-examination.

(Singer, 2024)

Users can propose ethical questions and the chatbot will emulate his responses. As Elliott (2022) identifies, chatbot's sound increasingly human-like, due to Natural Language Processing (NLP) which encompasses AI technologies related to the analysis, interpretation and the generation of language. However, NLP could be subject to bias either through the programming methods, or inherent bias contained within the data. For example, a study by Wilson, Hoffman and Morgenstern (2020), cited  in  (Kaplan,  2021,  p.  27),  illustrated  that  several  decision-support  systems applied by judges may be racially biased, based upon past rulings; and self-driving cars better detected lighter skin than darker tones, as their algorithms were trained using pictures among which were few people of colour. We have here a quite literal danger for people of certain races, if the technology is unable to correct for systemic racism within data. Therefore, before AI is even placed into the sphere of social work, questions would need to be asked about where the AI training data has been obtained from, does it reflect prevailing attitudes, norms, and therefore discrimination.

## Generative AI Social Work Assessments

Anecdotally,  from  personal  experience  when  having  attended  training  sessions on how to integrate AI into the UK University curriculum, I am met with fatalist-type phrases in relation to AI such as: 'this stuff is going nowhere'. This is often a response to concerns around generative AI being used to plagiarise in student assignments, or concerns that all applications of AI and their subsequent outcomes, may not be entirely positive, such as effecting students' ability to write well academically and think critically. Generative AI gives us the ability to outsource our thinking, helping with idea generation, proofreading and critique of written work, functioning here like a critical friend or supervisor. Therefore, it would also be relevant to rephrase statements like these into: 'this stuff is going everywhere', AI being on every mobile phone, laptop, powering internet searches, in every workplace - every hospital, police station, court, and even placed into chips implanted in human brains. Case in point, Neuralink a firm founded by Elon Musk, which is developing plug in chips in human skulls to increase cognitive abilities and memory to 'save the human race' against AI (Dubber et al., 2021,  p.  35). AI  here  being  both  the  problem  and  the  solution,  an  existential threat to humanity, yet one that can be surmounted with the fusion between soft brain matter, hard technology and ever more intelligent AI. While at this point AI remains fairly submissive and has not yet taken over the human population in some apocalyptic-style scenario, we should critically question which parts of society will benefit from AI to assist with human tasks, and to what extent AI should be everywhere or nowhere.

With this in consideration, I will explore the use of generative AI in social work assessments. Definitions of assessment have common themes such as ascertaining levels of risk and determining need (Milner et al., 2020). Bolton (2018) broadens the definition to include ethics and morals: 'Assessment is a complex moral and ethical act' (Bolton, 2018, p. 205). This considers assessment as more than just the sum of its parts - the mere gathering of data, written records, carefully organised and analysed with an appropriate outcome - but rather an act that incorporates philosophical concepts such as morals and ethics.

In a detailed book on social work assessments, Parker (2025) sees the enquiry following the tragic death of Victoria Climbie as an important point in the history of assessment in social work. Written by Lord Laming in 2003 (Laming, 2003), the report recommended action that social workers must undertake during the process of assessment including: 'speak alone to the child; see and speak with carers; visit the  child's  accommodation; seek and consider the views of other professionals' (Parker, 2025). These recommendations, which retrospectively can seem obvious and self-evident were not strictly adhered to at the time, the resulting outcome being the horrific torture and murder of an eight-year-old girl in the United Kingdom. We can note, that generative AI, in its current forms would not be able to undertake any of the tasks recommended by the review. This would require input from the social worker who would gather this primary data, generative AI could perceivably then assist with the written aspect of these assessments.

In addition to assessment definitions being broad, the steer has also altered historically. Munro (2008, 2020) discusses child and family assessments evolving to encompass risk: 'The language of risk is relatively new to child protection work. In childcare textbooks from the 1950s or 1960s, there was no reference to risk assessments' (Munro, 2008, p. 58). The term risk assessment can therefore be seen as rooted in prediction. If the risks to a child are identified then harm could feasibly be predicted, logically following that future harms could be prevented. Serious case reviews such as these lay bare the private family life and the individual suffering of children, exposing these to the arena of the public sphere. This often generates outcry, horror and a need for retribution against the perpetrators, frequently overshadowed and directed towards the professionals involved. A pertinent example in the UK being the case of Peter Connelly, or 'Baby P' as he was posthumously known (Jones, 2014).  Government scrutiny  and  enquiry  frequently  follow  with recommendations, and the need to fix practice errors could take a technological turn. Webb (2006) defines technology that enables social workers to do their job, as technologies of care. Predating AI, he lists these to include email and computerised case files, seeing them as tools but also entities that can be critically scrutinised:

Technologies of care are concerned with the governance of uncertainty - they are rational schemas for organising social work and developing procedures for how it is carried out and delivered. In this sense they are programmatic since  they  are  based  on  sets  of  calculating  prescriptions  through  which care-based interventions are organised, arranged and regulated.

(Webb, 2006, p. 141)

Webb further argues that care management 'tends to be overly preoccupied with the assessment of need and risk and the matching of available services for service users, rather than relationship building or therapeutic direct work' (Webb, 2006, p.  142). Technology here designed to assist, moves social work into a different direction,  focusing  on  the  collation  and  calculation  of  data  to  predict  risk,  then subsequently allocate services to meet need, rather than focusing on the individual relationship between social worker and service user. These technologies are still  present,  but  may become increasingly enhanced or augmented by the addition  of  generative AI.  For  example,  case  files  can  be  quickly  summarised  into shortened distillations of the most pertinent issues, such as trawling all associated notes regarding a parent's mental health history, and collating chronologies into a shortened summarised narrative. Chronologies are often a key part of social work assessments and can be critical in determining historical patterns, risk factors and providing vital links between disparate pieces of information. Dyke (2023) discusses that in practice, chronologies are often seen as a bureaucratic chore, rather than a vital part of understanding service users' history, recommending that they are  the  first  thing  a  social  worker  should  complete  for  an  assessment.  He  also argues they have 'moral value' (Dyke, 2023, p. 33) reminding us that the service users' story matters.

To date, assessment in social work is quite rightly viewed through the lens of human-decision-making. Munro (2020, p. 27) discusses two defined methods of understanding the world as 'intuitive and analytic'. Intuition can be viewed as mysterious as it tends to happen at speed, an unconscious process that automatically occurs as a response to perceptions, which can then be articulated and justified by language. Drawing here from Nobel Prize-winning psychologist Daniel Kahneman (2011), he discusses an analogy of intuitive decision-making when firefighters entered a house with a kitchen on fire. Soon after entering the room, the commander shouted, without realising where this came from, to get out just before the floor collapsed. This was due to the fire being below rather than in the kitchen as originally thought. The firefighter discussed the decision being prompted by a 'sixth sense of danger' (Kahneman, 2011, p. 11). Psychologists now understand more about these types  of  intuitive  decisions  and  there  is  less  mystery  involved.  Seeing  multiple similar situations over the course of his professional career will have contributed to a knowledge base the ability to seek similarities in patterns and situations. The need for firefighters to make quick intuitive decisions is vital, not having the luxury of time to weigh up, assess and analyse situations. However, we can apply similar principles to cases of child or adult protection in social work that require immediate responses. Generative AI's ability to read huge amounts of text, concisely summarising the key points could be applied to social work databases. These contain documentation collated by professionals and could be read by AI, then if also trained on relevant research could provide evidence-based responses and suggestions for outcomes. The speed at which this could happen would be impressive in comparison to human standards, however what is gained in speed and efficiency may be lost when information is glossed over in a cursory manner. For example, this could lead to what Ornellas et al. (2020), refer to as deprofessionalisation, a key

feature of neoliberalism which leads to reducing professional discretion, deskilling social  work  and  diminishing  professional  autonomy and identity. Further warnings  about  generic  tick-box  approaches  have  been  made  by  Dustin  (2007),  and the McDonaldization of social work, with efficiency, replication and productivity becoming the main measures of success, replacing core moral and ethical values of the profession.

## Ethics and Generative AI Social Work

Banks (2021, p. 31) states that social work can be viewed as a 'human services' profession, an occupation that focuses on human relationships and the multiple complexities that arise therein. She identifies the relationship between social worker and service user as one of unequal power, the social worker holding more power than the service user, often the power to substantially intervene in that person's private family domain. If social work begins to use non-human entities in assessment and therefore decision-making, this could be viewed as a fundamental shift in the dynamics of the profession. This could distance social work from human elements in favour of AI-powered outcomes. To consider how generative AI may influence social work practice, and any subsequent alterations required to regulations and the ethics of the profession, we can examine the impact technology has had on social work historically. Writing  in  2008,  Parton,  critically  examined  the  use  of  computer databases within social work, something which is now ubiquitous in statutory social work. He argued that this created an environment where the key focus of  activity,  became  the  gathering,  sharing  and  monitoring  of  information  about individuals, together with the accounting for their own decisions and interventions, and those of the other associated professionals and agencies (Parton, 2008). The advantages of social care databases can be multiple, they allow information to be confidentially stored, easily and quickly accessed, then shared with other professionals. This allows continuity and adaptability of the workforce, in that if a social worker leaves post before the conclusion of their work, then another can take over the case, reading case notes and files to then continue to build upon the previous worker's role. However, Parton argues that:

Rather than be concerned with presenting a picture of the subject, as, previously, social work increasingly acts to take subjects apart and then reassembles them according to the requirements of the database. Practitioners are required to produce dispersed and fragmented identities made up of a series of characteristics and pieces of information which are easy to input/output and compare. In the process, the embodied subject is in danger of disappearing and we are left with a variety of surface information … Such a development begs the question as to how much direct contact and interaction needs to take place between the social worker and the client if the primary concern becomes gathering, inputting and analysing information.

(Parton, 2008, p. 263)

The focus of social work becomes less on the quality of social interaction with the service user, the human encounter, than on the amount of data that can be inputted into the database, signifying that work has been undertaken. In the example of  the  social  worker  leaving,  this  may  be  devastating  for  the  service  user  who has taken time to establish trust with the worker, relaying painful details of their lives - fragmented and then be pieced back together. Yet, this is of no consequence to the database, which can continue to be inputted by the next worker - feeding the machine. Parton (2008) goes on to argue that this gives rise to a database culture within social work, moving away from narrative approaches where knowledge is constructed mainly from the face-to-face encounter between social worker and service user, to information and data collection, fitting the service users' biographical history into the narrow and defined parameters of the social care database, ultimately leading to the erosion of the social within social work. However, unlike the database, which is essentially a virtual space that holds data, generative AI can read at speed, rapidly creating summaries of data. In the same way that internet searches often now have an AI summary at the very beginning, generative AI could quickly read hundreds of case records relating to a service user, then summarise these into a narrative. The database with the active elements of AI then becomes something less inert - a mere storage warehouse of information, morphing into something that can think through data, mirroring the way a social worker would read case files, building a picture of the service users' life, to analyse patterns, identify risks, and needs. The time saving potential to social workers here is significant. Questions would however arise as to whether this time gained, would be converted to prioritising time spent with service users, versus working on administrative tasks, or whether this means quicker less diligent social work, as cases can be rushed through and completed at a much faster rate. Garrett, for example, warns us that: 'Clearly, there is a kernel of truth in the perception that social work is becoming more onerous and less fulfilling. The working day is expanded, and technology is, seemingly, deployed more to entrench the alienation of practitioners than to create possibilities for better service provision and personal growth' (Garrett, 2024, p. 124). Here technology is restricting rather than liberating the worker. The ability of generative AI to create space for social workers to reconnect with service users, away from the enduring administrative office tasks, may also create more focus and surveillance of populations as their data is gathered, stored and used by AI.

When we consider generative AI's role in social work practice, specifically if it takes a more active role in the assessment and decision-making process, then we may need to completely reconsider the ethical standards of the profession. Banks (2012) summarises that most of the literature on social work ethics has focused on lists of principles about how the social worker ought to treat the individual service user,  often  underpinned  by  one  basic  principle  as  'respect  for  persons'  (Banks, 2012, p. 42). The philosopher Immanuel Kant articulated this concept of the categorical imperative as: 'So act that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means' (Kant 1785, cited in Kerstein, 2024). Fundamental here is the idea of a

person, someone who has their own individual desires; ends which should not be subsumed by others for their own means to an end. This is comparative with the power that social workers can hold over others, and the need to protect persons from exploitation or abuse. The categorical imperative is not entirely perfect as an ethical standpoint, and there are situations whereby social workers would need to act for the common good. For example, a social work assessment that summarises a child should be removed from their parents, to protect them from future abuse, may go against the desires of that child and their parents, overriding their own ends for a specific means. However, present here are human persons and not robots, theoretical principles and guidelines of human-on-human behaviour. Therefore, when AI becomes involved in decision-making, social work ethics becomes blurred and problematic.

Benossi and Bernecker (2022) theoretically analyse robot ethics through a Kantian perspective. They argue that AI lacks the two key components required for morally relevant decision-making, which are an emotional inner life and freedom of will, clarifying that robots are not free, rather determined by preprogrammed algorithms. Then if AI lacks the core features both cognitively and ethically to make moral decisions, we can argue against their involvement in decision-making in social work, to risk diluting the ethical principles of this human profession. This would relegate generative AI usage to the function of an administrative assistant, leaving decision-making - which can be complex, emotionally challenging, mirroring the complexity of the problems to solve, amend or ameliorate - firmly in the hands of humans.

## Conclusion

Responses towards AI can fall into different categories, from the sceptical to the optimistic, the fatalistic to the resistant. We are dealing with a phenomenon that is largely unencountered, the terrain is unknown and often the use of thought experiments, or guesswork is required to shape and predict AI futures. By analysing some of these responses we can move towards creating a critical framework to challenge assumptions, address risks and predictions of future workspaces. Failure to do this could result in retrospective action to reclaim areas of human practices, in areas that have become saturated with AI.

Elliott (2022, p. 10) states that, 'AI as both ubiquitous and invisible,' yet social work must not ignore or blindly accept its usage. The British Association of Social Workers (BASW) has issued a statement on the use of generative AI in social work, concluding that 'In light of current limitations, the report finds that the use of generative AI tools in care assessment and planning would currently be inappropriate without direct human oversight' (BASW, 2025, p. 11). This is a welcome warning but also vague in its stipulation. We do not know which human will have oversight and how direct this will be. Whether these will be managers, administrative workers, or people within the AI industry, the so-called Big Tech - the technology giants that dominate and control the market, often referred to by the acronyms GAFAM which refers to US-based companies and includes Google (Alphabet),

Apple,  Facebook  (Meta), Amazon,  and  Microsoft  (Verdegem,  2024)  -  remains undetermined. The speed at which these companies develop if involved in social work, could overwhelm the professions' ability to regulate and define the ethical parameters of the work they undertake. However, if social work remains true to human-based ethics in all aspects of decision-making, then we can retain the most important elements - the human in this human profession.
