## ABSTRACT

For four decades, discourses on digital divides have shaped engagement with societal transformation processes in the context of digitality. With the rapid development of AI technologies, these disparities are manifesting in an emerging 'AI Divide' that not only reproduces existing social inequalities but potentially amplifies them. This article analyses the interactions between AI development, digital inequality and social participation, and argues, by way of example, against technological solutionism and techno-  ableism, which reduce complex social challenges to technically solvable problems. Instead, the necessity of a critical integration of AI safety and AI alignment with inclusive educational processes is elaborated. Central to this is the question of how transformative education can enable people not merely to adapt technically, but to actively participate in shaping an inclusive, AI-  influenced society. The article proposes anchoring inclusion as a continuous moment of reflection in the development and implementation of AI, and outlines pedagogical enabling spaces that can promote both 'Inclusive AI' and 'AI for Inclusion'.

## 1   |   Introduction

For  four  decades  now,  discourses  on  digital  divides  and  disparities  have  shaped  the  engagement  with  societal  transformation  processes  in  the  horizon  of  digitization  and  digitality (Hargittai  2021;  van  Dijk  2005,  2020).  During  this  period, perspectives on digital divides and disparities have repeatedly crystallized around the evolving structures, dynamics and processes of society, not least in relation to the ongoing advancement of media developments (Mjøs 2023). These have often been described  along  the  lines  of  discourses  on  digital  inequality (Hargittai 2021) and the digital divide (van Dijk 2020). These discourses  refer  to  different  perspectives  in  describing  the digital  within  contemporary  social  transformation  processes: Stalder (2018) encompasses under the term 'digitality'-which will  be  preferred  in  the  following  text-contemporary  digital  phenomena  from  a  sociological  viewpoint,  such  as  algorithms,  communal  dynamics  and  referentiality.  By  contrast,

Krotz  (2017),  from  a  media  and  communication  studies  perspective,  analyzes  media  change  as  a  profound  mediatisation process,  visible  in  the  growing  diversity  of  media  forms,  the ubiquity and increasing interconnectivity of media, an accelerated pace of innovation and intensified datafication.

In recent years, new entanglements between digital media technologies  and  society  have  emerged  (and  continue  to  emerge) along the lines  of  AI  technologies  and  applications.  These,  in turn,  touch  upon  questions  of  inequality,  privilege  and  disadvantage, as well as the (im-  )possibilities of social belonging and participation-in this sense, of inclusion.

Artificial  Intelligence  (AI)  has  developed  into  an  umbrella term for various technologies and research fields aimed at replicating  human abilities  such  as  learning,  logical  reasoning, problem-  solving, perception and decision-  making. the  field  has  progressed  enormously  in  recent  decades.  As early as 1959, Arthur Samuel defined machine learning as a 'field of study that gives computers the ability to learn without being explicitly programmed' (Samuel 1959, 210). While classical  software  development  specifies  each  program  step in detail, machine learning systems can learn from examples and independently detect patterns. In recent years, generative AI systems in particular have attracted considerable attention. These include systems capable of producing texts, images, videos, or music. At the same time, the next stage of development is already taking shape: Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), which  does  not  describe  a  single  technological  innovation but rather a stepwise process toward systems that can reach at  least  human-  level ability across a wide range of cognitive tasks (Morris et al. 2024).

In the context of these technological developments, it is often argued that AI holds the key to solving societal challenges-from the  climate  crisis  to  issues  of  social  inequality  and  inclusion. This  stance  can  be  described  as  'technological  solutionism' (Morozov  2014)  and  deserves  critical  consideration  for  several reasons. The critique is not directed against technological approaches  per  se  but  against  a  superficial  form  of  problemsolving.  Particularly  in  the  context  of  inclusion,  this  issue  becomes  apparent,  for  instance,  in  the  form  of  technological ableism (Shew 2022), which reduces complex societal barriers and exclusion mechanisms to problems assumed to be solvable solely through technology.

Three aspects are central here: First, enabling social belonging and participation in the sense of inclusion, as well as addressing forms of digital inequality, requires not only technological but also social, political and economic strategies based on a deeper  understanding  of  the  societal  structures  and  mechanisms of inclusion and exclusion. Second, a focus on supposed technological  'silver  bullets'  can  distract  from  necessary structural  changes  (Kruschel  and  Merz-  Atalik  2023),  that is,  from  the  political,  social  and  institutional  reforms  truly needed to make inclusion possible. Third, technological solutions can themselves generate new, unforeseen social problems which then need to be addressed. Particularly problematic is the  emerging reinforcement of digital inequality, which can be referred to as the 'AI divide' (Bubeck et al. 2023; Carter et al. 2020). While AI systems on the one hand hold potential to reduce barriers to participation, their development and application at the same time reproduce problematic structures of power and domination.

The mere provision of AI technologies and applications without the  simultaneous consideration of the respective social conditions is not sufficient to enable social belonging and participation. Instead, from the perspective of inclusion-understood as social belonging and participation-there arises the necessity of analysing and reflecting upon the living conditions and situations of social actors, the structures, dynamics and processes of society, as well as their interdependencies.

Against  this  backdrop,  this  contribution  argues  for  an  intertwining  of  discourses  on  AI  development  and  use  with  the (im-  )possibilities  of  social  belonging  and  participation  in  the sense  of  inclusion.  It  becomes  evident  that  regarding  digital participation and inclusion, a purely technological 'quick fix' is inadequate. Rather, what is required is a fundamental social transformation that comprises not only technological but also social,  political  and  educational  dimensions.  The  following discussion will thus shed light on the interrelations between AI development, social transformation and questions of social belonging and participation in the sense of inclusion. Both the potentials and risks of AI systems for social inclusion and exclusion will be analysed, and perspectives for a critically reflective shaping of these transformation processes developed. At their core, these touch upon questions of AI safety, AI alignment and transformative education. Fundamentally, in view of the advancing development of AI technologies and their deepening permeation of society, education must be reoriented in order to address the manifestations of digital inequality already produced or yet to be produced by AI technologies, together with the connected forms of social exclusion.

## 2   |   Methodology

This article employs a hermeneutic literature review approach to analyse the intersection of AI safety, digital inequality and transformative  education.  The  analysis  is  based  on  a  transdisciplinary  selection  of  literature,  integrating  perspectives from media pedagogy, sociology of technology and disability studies.

Literature was selected based on relevance to three core thematic clusters: (1) Digital and AI inequality (e.g., 'AI Divide', 'Technoableism'), (2) AI Safety and Alignment and (3) Transformative learning  theories.  The  analysis  follows  a  hermeneutic  circle approach, interpreting specific technological phenomena (such as large language models) through the lens of established sociological and educational theories (such as the digital divide and inclusion).

The paper operationalizes 'Inclusion' not merely as technical access,  but  as  social  participation  and  co-  determination.  'AI Safety' is analysed beyond technical robustness to include the prevention of social stratification.

## 3   |   Digital Inequality and the Erosion of Social Participation

Societal transformation processes carry the risk of producing social inequalities, perpetuating existing social inequalities or entrenching them (Dörre et al. 2019). This is particularly evident with regard to digital media technologies in discourses on digital inequality (DiMaggio and Hargittai 2001), the digital divide (van Dijk 2005, 2020), media (education) and social inequality (Niesyto 2009), as well as media (education) and inclusion (Bosse et al. 2019). The entanglement of social and digital inequality manifests itself essentially along the unequal possibilities  of  social  belonging  and participation (Kronauer 2010, 24). This results in unequal opportunities for interaction and agency among people, which express themselves in the form of advantageous and disadvantageous living conditions and life chances (Kronauer 2013, 19-21). Inclusion, understood as a guiding principle of societal transformation, aims at dissolving barriers to belonging and participation and the accompanying social  and  digital  inequalities  (Kronauer 2013, 21; Clayton and Macdonald  2013).  Here  it  becomes  clear  that  discourses  on participation are always closely tied to questions of overcoming inequalities and to the entanglement of social and digital inequalities (Mogge-  Grotjahn 2022, 20). Discourses on participation thereby touch upon both socio-  political and social-  legal dimensions  as  well  as  societal  questions  of  value  (MoggeGrotjahn  2022,  18-19).  In  the  socio-  political  and  social-  legal context, the central question is how a societal framework must be designed to realize the right to self-  determination as well as to co-  determination of general living conditions-for example,  through  welfare  state  services  and  institutional  security systems. In terms of values and value orientations, participation as a human right (e.g., UN CRPD 2006) is concerned with shaping  societal  and  political  framework  conditions  in  such a way that all people can shape their lives autonomously, live self-  determinedly,  obtain  comprehensive  access  and  participate in decision-  making processes (Mogge-  Grotjahn 2022, 22). Against this background, in societies where social belonging and participation (or where social exclusion) are closely tied to access to and engagement with digital media technologies, the necessity arises for constant reflection on society and societal transformation within the horizon of digitality along the guiding principle of inclusion (see Table 1).

Social  belonging  and  participation,  as  inclusion  in  contemporary  societies,  refer  particularly  to  gainful  employment (primarily  as  the  foundation  of  (sur)vival  in  modern  societies),  citizenship  rights  (personal,  political,  social;  primarily as the source of the polity), as well as integration into reciprocal social relationships (primarily as a source of solidarity) (Kronauer 2013, 18).

In this context, the perspective of inclusion is associated with an analysis, reflection and addressing of the social structures and dynamics of exclusion of people, toward the transformation of societal conditions-'put pointedly: inclusion into socially exclusionary conditions cannot be the goal, but only the overcoming of such conditions' (Kronauer 2013, 24). Along the figure of the 'simultaneity of 'inside' and 'outside'' (Kronauer 2010, 44), a constellation that is characteristic of the relationship between inclusion and exclusion in modern societies, exclusion is to be understood as marginalization within society (Kronauer 2010, 41).

Understanding  exclusion  as  a  'particular  form  of being inside' appears all the more necessary today, as people in highly developed capitalist societies are still far more (…) embedded in the welfare state and have developed common standards across social class boundaries for what a cultural subsistence minimum for social participation entails. Even in experiences of  social  exclusion,  the  simultaneity  of  'inside'  and 'outside',  the  confrontation  with  and  identification with  socially  shared  expectations,  constitutes  the particular acuity. (Kronauer 2010, 44-45).

While welfare state measures formally secure social belonging and participation, they frequently presuppose existing relations of exclusion without questioning them. Precisely people in precarious  life  situations  thereby  experience  a  tension  between formal  inclusion  and  factual  exclusion-that  simultaneity  of 'inside'  and  'outside'  that  Kronauer  describes  as  the  characteristic of modern exclusion. In the context of gainful employment, exclusion manifests itself as marginalization in the labour market up to permanent loss of employment-while simultaneously being bound to socially shared ways of life beyond gainful employment as well. This is likewise accompanied by a loss of social recognition and possibilities for action, but also of the capitalist  guiding principle of participation in monetary profit maximization (Kronauer 2010).

Fundamentally, with regard to the relationship between inclusion and exclusion in modern, capitalistically shaped societies, the following becomes evident:

Inclusion on the basis of social rights  remains (…) under capitalist-  market economy conditions always (sic!) endangered and fragile. The precarious connection  between  market  dependency  and  social rights  represents,  so  to  speak,  the  'Achilles  heel'  of social inclusion. (Kronauer 2010, 34).

Interesting  in  this  context  is  the  question  of  what  social  belonging and participation in the sense of inclusion refers to-is

it  about  belonging  to  and  participation  in  a  capitalist  society (order) with its uncertainties regarding social rights (e.g., welfare state arrangements) (Schluchter and Böhmer 2024)?

## 3.1   |   On the Relationship Between Digital Inequality and Digital Divide

Digital media technologies both open up and limit possibilities for  social  belonging  and  participation  (van  Dijk  2005,  2020; McMahon et al. 2023). Various connections arise  here  to  discourses  on  digital  inequality  ('digital  inequality';  Mossberger et al. 2003; Dobransky &amp; Hargittai 2006) and associated discussions concerning the digital divide (Hargittai and Hsieh 2013) or digital inclusion ('digital inclusion', Ragnedda and Mutsvairo 2018).

Digital  inequality  encompasses  not  only  differing  opportunities  for  access  to  digital  media  technologies  (first-  level  digital divide), but also differences in their use (second-  level digital divide) (DiMaggio and Hargittai 2001; van Dijk 2005, 2020). The concept of the digital divide is expanded by the idea of the zerolevel digital divide (van Dijk 2005, 2020), which emphasizes the significance of structural features of digital media technologies (such as software/apps and the internet itself) for their use-and thereby the inequalities connected to them (ibid.). All three perspectives on the digital divide point to different forms of unequal access  to  and  use  of  digital  technologies  (ibid.).  This  demonstrates interactions between pre-  existing social inequalities and digital inequalities:

'… digital  inequality  can  refer  both  to  how  existing social inequalities influence the adoption and use of digital technologies as well as how differential use of the Internet itself may influence social stratification' (Hargittai and Hsieh 2013, 141).

In societies where media are highly significant, both perspectives can observe that '… (u)nequal access to digital technologies is about unequal participation in society' (van Dijk 2005, 15).  The  spectrum  of  digital  inequality  extends  across  several dimensions (DiMaggio and Hargittai 2001, 8-10): Inequality in technical apparatus, inequality in autonomy of use, inequality in skill, inequality in the availability of social support, inequality in variation of use.

As  digital  media  technologies  increasingly  permeate  societies and  their  significance  for  social  belonging  and  participation grows,  risks  arise  both  of  the  entrenchment  of  existing  inequalities and the emergence of 'new' (previously unobserved) inequalities. This growing importance of digital media technologies in societies is connected to societal power and domination structures, making the design of digital media technologies, as well as societal transformation processes in the context of digitality, a contested (discursive) field (Kellner and Share 2019, 8).

In  view  of  this,  the  manner  in  which  social  transformation processes are designed (and digital media technologies are socially implemented) becomes a focal point for equality/inequality  (and  thus  privilege/disadvantage). Accordingly, against the background  of  the  dynamics  of  societal  transformation  processes, it is necessary to consider the specific barriers and design spaces regarding digital media technologies in order to explore possibilities for addressing digital as well as social inequalities.

Based on the premise that digital media technologies are never neutral but instead follow the interests of different social actors in their conception and use (Kellner and Share 2019, 8), inclusion becomes a (potential) orientation for the further development of society, accompanying social transformation processes in  the  horizon  of  digitality  (Schluchter  2016).  The  focus  is  on shaping social change in order to address existing digital as well as social inequalities, so that belonging and participation for all may become possible,  along  the  lines  of  the  idea  of  inclusion (ibid.). Linked to this are issues in the context of AI safety and AI alignment (Bengio et al. 2025; Russell 2022). AI safety refers to measures to ensure that AI systems are safe and reliable, while AI alignment ensures that AI systems act in accordance with human values and goals. If the societal phenomena associated with digital inequality remain unattended, the increasing diffusion of digital media technologies across societal sectors leads to growing divides and a loss of opportunities for belonging and participation (Schluchter 2023).

## 3.2   |   AI Divide as an Intensification of Digital Inequality and Digital Division

The increasing use of AI technologies and applications in societies and the accompanying influences on societies entails the risk of an 'AI Divide' (Carter et al. 2020). Alongside the concept of the AI Divide (Carter et al. 2020), further terms such as 'algorithmic knowledge gap' (Cotter and Reisdorf 2020) or 'algorithmic awareness gap' (Gran et al. 2021) can be found, which describe  individual  and  structural  inequalities  with  regard  to AI technologies and applications. Here, manifold connections to the previously outlined discourses on digital inequality and the  digital  divide  (DiMaggio  and  Hargittai  2001)  become  evident,  as  well  as  to  discourses  on  digital  inclusion  (Ragnedda and Mutsvairo 2018). Particularly with regard to the close entanglement of structural social inequalities and digital inequalities,  including  AI  technologies  and  applications  (Cotter  and Reisdorf  2020;  Gran  et  al.  2021;  Lutz  2019;  Ragnedda  2020; Zarouali et al. 2021).

Fundamentally,  discourses  on  the  AI  Divide  can  be  regarded as a sub-  field of discourses on digital inequality and the digital divide:

AI-  related inequalities about access to AI (the first level digital divide), the ability to use AI (the second level digital divide) and the outcomes of AI engagement (the third level digital divide). (Carter et al. 2020, 259).

All three perspectives capture different forms of unequal access and  usage  possibilities  of  digital  media  and  AI  technologies. Closely connected to this are questions of individual perceptions, beliefs  and  attitudes  with  regard  to  AI,  which  must  be  taken into account in the description and addressing of inequality formations within the horizon of AI technologies and applications (Carter  et  al.  2020).  Current  discourses  on  AI  demonstrate  that

(existing) social and digital inequalities (in the sense of privileges and disadvantages) are largely reproduced in engagement with AI technologies and applications (Elena-  Bucea et al. 2021; Mubarak et al. 2020; Park and Humphry 2019). Systematic empirical studies on AI-  related disparities are thus far scarcely available (Wang et al. 2024; Cotter and Reisdorf 2020; Gran et al. 2021).

In  the  long  run,  the  AI  divide  can  accelerate  other  socioeconomic problems and may impact the sustainability of society. (Wang et al. 2024, 6).

In this context, it becomes evident that users' AI-  related knowledge,  skills  and  attitudes  with  regard  to  engagement  with AI technologies and  applications vary considerably (Gran et al. 2021; Zarouali et al. 2021). For instance, difficulties in recognizing AI systems (Dogruel et al. 2020; Gran et al. 2021), in understanding algorithmic decision-  making processes (DeVito et  al.  2017;  Eslami  et  al.  2016;  Gruber  and  Hargittai  2023),  as well  as  in  critically  evaluating  AI-  curated  online  information (Shin 2022) can be observed.

The increasing proliferation of AI applications intensifies individual  challenges  in  the  domain  of  knowledge,  competencies and skills in dealing with AI (Wong and Kindarji 2023; Wang et  al.  2024).  Here,  for  example,  the  connection  becomes  evident between AI competencies and advantageous engagement with AI technologies and applications in the sense of maximum self-  determination (Ragnedda 2020) and possibilities for social participation (Patel and Patel 2020) as a response to data-  driven manipulation,  manifestations  of  AI  bias  and  exploitation  of power imbalances (Eubanks 2017; Mohamed et al. 2020). This perspective requires an expansion of existing digital divide discourses to include the question of which societal actors can understand, shape or control AI (Wang et al. 2024). This results in the necessity of intensified engagement with the intersections of individual knowledge stocks, competencies and skills, social living conditions and situations, with societal relations of power and domination.

A  deeper  examination  of  the  discourse  on  the  AI  Divide reveals,  however,  that  this  involves  not  merely  a  perpetuation  of  the  classical  digital  divide,  but  rather  a  complex double  division  (or  'intensified  digital  divide,'  according  to Hendawy 2024). On the one hand, along the perspectives of the (zero-  , first- and second-  level) digital divide, unequal access  to  AI  technologies  and  applications  as  well  as  unequal usage  possibilities,  objectives  and  outcomes  exist.  On  the other hand, however, the AI systems themselves are permeated  by  inequality-  relevant  structures:  for  example,  training data reproduce societal biases, while at the same time, algorithmic decision-  making processes are often opaque and follow normative assumptions that systematically disadvantage or exclude certain groups. Insofar, the AI Divide concerns not exclusively digital participation in the classical sense, but also power over and within the systems themselves. This double dimension  of  digital  inequality-in  access  to  AI  and  in  AI itself-points to new challenges for education, research and society in dealing with AI.

Since AI technologies and applications in particular, corresponding  to  digital  media  technologies  in  general,  are  increasingly being linked with everyday functions and professional requirements, here too exists the risk that existing social and digital inequalities  are  intensified  and  new  forms  of  inequality  are produced (Mandapuram et al. 2018)-not least described in discourses on the AI Divide:

As  we  navigate the complexities of this digital transformation, it's crucial not just to celebrate advancements  but  also  to  critically  examine  who might  be  left  behind.  (…)  This  is  not  just  about equitable access to technology but about empowering all individuals with the knowledge and skills to thrive in an AI-  driven world. (Hendawy 2024, n.p.).

This underscores the urgency of targeted support by AI designers, political decision-  makers and educators for disadvantaged user groups. The aim should be to open up equal opportunities for all users to acquire AI competencies that enable participation  in  the  culture  of  digitality  and  empower  critical  reflection  on  inequalities  in  AI  systems.  Otherwise,  AI  threatens not  only  to  reproduce  social  disparities  but  to  generate  new mechanisms of exclusion-with the consequence of an 'intensified division,' where unequal access meets unequal system structures.  In  this  context,  with  the  progressive  integration of  AI  technologies  and  applications  into  various  domains of  life,  there  exists  the  risk  that  digital  divisions  increase in complexity. In what follows, AI bias, techno-  solutionism and techno-  ableism are exemplarily presented as phenomena of an AI Divide:

## 3.2.1 | AI Bias(Es) and Diversity

Technological  progress  in  the  field  of  AI  is  frequently  portrayed as a neutral process or one conducive to inclusion. The notion  of  an  objective  or  neutral  AI  is  a  technological  illusion that rests on the assumption that algorithms themselves contain no values or biases. In reality, however, AI systems emerge  within  a  social  and  cultural  context  in  which  they not only reproduce existing structures of discrimination but often  intensify  them.  Research  has  demonstrated  that  algorithmic systems frequently process data that reflect existing societal hierarchies, particularly with regard to the structural categories  of  race,  gender  and  disability  (Baker  et  al.  2025; Buolamwini and Gebru 2018; Noble 2018). This leads to AI models  not  only  inscribing  normative  conceptions  of  what counts  as  'normal'  or  'deviant',  but  also  actively  producing mechanisms of exclusion. Yet the inherent mechanisms with which AI systems are trained are not free from bias, but rather reproduce existing social hierarchies and normative conceptions of normality (Shew and Earle 2024; Walgenbach 2023). In this context, techno-  solutionism and techno-  ableism combine into a problematic ideological  position:  while  solutionism  stylizes  AI  as  a  universal  answer  to  societal  challenges,  techno-  ableism  promotes  an  implicit  conception  of disability as a deficit that can be 'overcome' through technology (Shew 2020; Bieling and Joost 2018). The term 'technoableism' thus advances the notion that people with disabilities must be 'repaired'  (Shew  2020).  Techno-  ableism  thereby  reduces the consideration of disability to deficits that are to be

eliminated  or  compensated  through  technological  interventions.  Against  this  background,  the  perspective  on  the  real needs,  experiences  and  decision-  making  freedoms  of  people with disabilities is ignored.

## 3.2.2 | Techno-  Solutionism

Techno-  solutionism  regards  technology  as  the  preferred  or even sole means for solving social problems (Harrasser 2013). With regard to disability, this leads to a reduction to functional adaptation: AI-  based assistive technologies are conceived not as  tools  for  autonomy and diversity, but as means for establishing normative capability (Shew 2022). The underlying designs rarely proceed from the perspectives of disabled people, but rather consolidate ableist structures by regarding technology as a 'repair mechanism' for deviations from the 'normal' body or behaviour (Bieling and Joost 2018; Stöhr 2023). The assumption that technology is an inherently progressive force leads to social problems being treated as primarily technical challenges. In doing so, it is overlooked that technologies do not  emerge  in  a  societal  vacuum  but  always  operate  within particular  power  structures  (Winner  1986;  Mühlhoff  2023). In practice, it  becomes evident that technological 'solutions' to  social  problems  often  perpetuate  existing  inequalities  or generate  new  mechanisms  of  exclusion.  For  instance,  AIsupported  assistance  systems  for  people  with  disabilities are  frequently  developed  without  their  active  participation, whereby the specific needs of users are inadequately considered (Shew 2020). The consequence is that adaptive technologies do not contribute to self-  determination, but rather create a binary relationship of 'useful' and 'useless' technology that structurally anchors ableism.

## 3.2.3 | Techno-  Solutionism and Ableism

While ableism historically emerges from social constructions of disability as deficit, techno-  solutionism offers a modern continuation of this mode of thinking by promising technical solutions  for  societal  barriers  without  questioning  these  barriers themselves  (Shew  2020).  Empirical  studies  demonstrate  that AI technologies are often developed with a normative design bias  that  privileges  certain  abilities  and  excludes  alternative forms  of  use  (Walgenbach  2023).  This  manifests  particularly in digital learning platforms that are oriented toward homogeneous user groups and thus maintain barriers for, for example, neurodiverse or motorically impaired persons (Schmidl 2022). A  particularly  problematic  example  of  the  linkage  between techno-  solutionism and ableism is the domain of 'smart' health and surveillance technologies. Many of these systems are based on  the  medical  model  of  disability  and  focus  on  measuring, classifying and-where possible-correcting functional deviations (Hamraie and Fritsch 2019). Thus wearable sensors or AIsupported  diagnostic  tools  are  frequently  developed  with  the aim of identifying disabilities early and treating them 'therapeutically', rather than engaging with the social and structural conditions that first make disability an obstacle. This logic is deeply  rooted  in  the  historical  traditions  of  'eugenics',  which regard disability as a 'problem' requiring technical correction (Clare 2017).

## 3.2.4 | Techno-  Ableism

The  term  techno-  ableism  describes  the  interplay  of  technosolutionism  and  ableism  by  showing  that  many  technological innovations for disability do not originate from affected persons themselves  but  are  defined  by  external  developers  (Shew  and Earle 2024). This leads to an intensification of existing power relations: Who determines which adaptation is deemed necessary? Who decides which impairment must be 'remedied' through AI? Critical  perspectives  such  as  the  concept  of  Crip  Technoscience (Shew  2022)  or  the  parahuman  perspective  (Harrasser  2020) offer  alternative  models  in  which  technology  is  understood  not as a repair instrument but as a flexible, inclusive design medium for  different  needs.  As  a  counter-  model  to  techno-  ableism,  Crip Technoscience demands a reorientation of innovation processes in which the expertise of disabled people stands at the centre. This concept  not  only  critiques  existing  technological  development practice but also demonstrates that technology is always embedded in social and political contexts and therefore cannot be designed 'neutrally'. In practice, this means that disabled people are not merely regarded as consumers or beta-  testers of technology, but as active co-  designers who decide on the orientation, objectives  and  functionality  of  technological  developments  (Hamraie and Fritsch 2019).

The essential principles of Crip Technoscience are:

1.  Centring people with disabilities as knowers and designers: The development of technologies must occur in a collaborative process in which disabled people are recognized as experts on their own needs.
2.  Recognition  of  inclusion  as  productive  friction:  Rather than  comprehending  inclusion  as  'seamless'  integration, Crip Technoscience emphasizes that friction is necessary to question existing norms and hierarchies.
3.  Interdependence as political technology: Disability should not be eliminated through technological 'optimization', but rather utilized as a starting point for new forms of social and technical design of social coexistence.
4.  Commitment  to  disability  justice:  Technological  innovations  must  be  embedded  in  a  more  comprehensive societal transformation that opposes mechanisms of exclusion and advocates for the recognition of diverse bodies and abilities.

The close linkage of AI bias, techno-  ableism and solutionism, following the argumentative orientation of the article, poses a  double challenge for education systems: On the one hand, the AI Divide is potentiated as educational platforms, adaptive learning technologies or algorithmic assessment systems perpetuate  existing  inequalities  (Walgenbach  2023).  On  the other  hand,  the  one-  sided  fixation  on  digital  competencies leads  to  a  reductive  conception  of  inclusion  that  reduces  itself to adaptability rather than questioning structural barriers (Stöhr 2023). An expanded concept of inclusion must therefore consider the interplay of technological possibilities and societal power relations in order to actually make digital media usable for  equitable  participation.  An  inclusion-  oriented  education policy must therefore aim not only to eliminate technological

barriers but also to bring about structural changes in the education system itself. This requires a departure from a purely competence-  oriented  understanding  of  education  toward  a perspective that recognizes the diversity of human forms of learning and communication.

Potential  risks  of  technological  solutionism  and  ableism  become exemplarily evident in findings from research on 'frontier reasoning models' (Baker et al. 2025). Such AI systems learn to solve complex tasks by means of natural language (chain-  ofthought). While monitoring their internal reasoning processes through another language model enables the identification  of problematic behaviour, direct optimization-that is, for example, 'punishing' undesired 'thoughts'-can lead to undesired side effects or the seeking of shortcuts. For AI systems, finding such shortcuts means that they do not learn the intended capabilities. They learn to circumvent the assessment system rather than developing genuine competencies. This is reflected in the examples presented by Baker et al. (2025), where the models do not learn to actually implement robust software solutions, but merely how to superficially pass tests. Thus, it becomes exemplarily evident in the discourse on techno-  ableism: when AI systems are designed to ostensibly fulfil inclusive standards, this frequently occurs merely at a superficial level. The underlying societal assumptions, such as achievement-  oriented conceptions of normality, remain unchanged and are rather obscured than actually  transformed.  In  practice,  this  means  that  instead  of genuinely  overcoming  structural  barriers  and  discrimination through participatory and transformative approaches, existing power relations are perpetuated. AI systems thus suggest an apparently  'technical  solution'  to  social  problems  (solutionism), while ableist and normative paradigms of 'correct' behaviour or functioning continue to persist.

This problematic underscores the necessity of examining more closely  both  the  theoretical  foundations  of  solutionism  and  of techno-  ableism, as well as-as will be discussed in the later section on AI safety and AI alignment-considering the danger of a 'lock-  in'  of  values  and  societal  structures,  whereby problematic  societal  presuppositions  could  be  permanently  inscribed into technological infrastructures through AI systems. Through this,  discriminatory  patterns  or  exclusionary  practices  could be permanently consolidated rather than being critically questioned or gradually dismantled.

## 4   |   AI Safety and Alignment

## 4.1   |   Foundations for Inclusive and Equitable Participation

The previous discussion of the interactions between AI development, digital  inequality  and  social  participation  shows  that technological  change  can  not  only  reproduce,  but  potentially reinforce, existing power structures. Therefore, it is worthwhile to consider the long-  term social and ethical implications of AI systems, as these also present new and familiar challenges for educational processes: On one hand, AI systems offer the potential  to  overcome  historical  inequalities,  but  on  the  other, they  carry  the  serious  risk  of  entrenching  them  permanently through  algorithmic  decision-  making  pathways  and  systems.

These reflections, from an inclusive perspective, lead to the central concepts of AI Safety and AI Alignment. AI Safety (Amodei et al. 2016; Bengio et al. 2025) concerns the development of AI systems that act safely and reliably. From the perspective of inclusion and social participation, this must engage not only with technical safety, but also with the avoidance of systems that reinforce or create new social inequalities (Varsik and Vosberg 2024, 37). AI Alignment (Leike and Sutskever 2023), in turn, focuses on ensuring that AI systems are consistent with existing human values and intentions (Leike and Sutskever 2023).

A particular issue arises from the persistence of digital structures.  Of  notable  importance  is  the  possibility  that  certain values, power relations and social structures could become permanently fixed through AI systems (MacAskill 2023, 83), which could have potentially irreversible consequences for social participation and communal life as a whole. This irreversibility is conceivable  because  AI  systems,  unlike  biological  cycles,  can potentially  operate  indefinitely  without  undergoing  the  kind of reflective, mutating, or rejuvenating processes characteristic of humans. If left unaddressed, this could cause values, prejudices and power structures inscribed during early development phases of AI systems to become entrenched and reproduced over long periods of time, even as society produces new values and norms (MacAskill 2023).

These considerations are increasingly urgent in light of current social developments and research in AI. Notably, the expected timeframe for systems with capabilities referred to as Artificial General Intelligence (AGI, see introduction for definition) has shortened markedly: Whereas initial estimates before the release of ChatGPT in 2022 projected a timeline of around 80 years, recent assessments suggest development could occur within less than  a  decade.  Some  researchers  refer  to  this  acceleration  as a  'compressed 21st century,' reflecting how rapid technological and societal changes induced by AI place new demands on human adaptability (MacAskill 2022; Amodei 2024).

This state of constant transformation presents a challenge to societal adaptability. Wilson (2009) captures this pointedly: 'The real problem of humanity is the following: we have paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions, and god-  like technology.' This formulation identifies three core areas of challenge in the sociotechnical  context  (Stalder  2018)  of  AI  development:  First,  the emotional-  psychological dimension, with human response patterns shaped by evolution unable to keep pace with technological change; second, the institutional inertia of social structures; and third, the exponentially increasing complexity and capabilities of AI systems.

When sections of the population are already exposed to structural  disadvantage,  further  barriers  to  transformational  processes arise. Social and  digital inequalities reinforce one another, as disadvantaged groups are less likely to have access to new technologies, face limited opportunities to develop relevant skills and are often excluded from shaping these technologies. The risk of an intensifying AI Divide becomes particularly clear in this context. Where technological development and its steering mechanisms are shaped primarily by privileged groups, there is a danger of locking in values and structures that perpetuate existing social conditions. ## 4.2   |   Technical Perspective

Foundational work on the so-  called 'Value Learning Problem' (Soares  2016,  3)  has  already  indicated  that  even  superintelligent systems capable of fully understanding human intentions would not necessarily act in accordance with these intentions. The technical challenge of correctly anchoring human values in AI systems therefore requires particular approaches to indirect value  representation.  It  may  thus  be  necessary  to  develop  AI safety and alignment mechanisms that do not operate through the inscription of fixed value systems, but rather create flexible, adaptive structures that enable and promote societal negotiation processes.  This  requires  a  critical  analysis  of  existing  power structures  and  the  deliberate  development  of  AI  systems  oriented toward equal opportunities, participation and social justice, without immutably inscribing certain values. AI must not passively adopt existing norms and power relations, but should work toward more just social conditions in the spirit of inclusion while considering societal developmental processes.

## 4.3   |   Societal and Educational Perspective

From the societal and educational-  theoretical perspective, educational  processes  must  simultaneously be designed to enable people to participate in the outlined technological developments and  to  actively  engage  with  rapid  change.  The  existential  dimension of this task becomes evident in light of the fact that the development of AI systems potentially implements irreversible societal  decisions  that  touch  upon  fundamental  questions  of human dignity, autonomy and justice.

The discrepancy described by Wilson (2009) between biological conditioning,  institutional  inertia  and  technological  development also raises the question of how educational processes can be designed in view of this existential challenge. Toffler's concept of transience proves particularly relevant in this context. In 'Future Shock,' Toffler formulated a thesis in 1970 that remains current today:

It  is  no  longer  sufficient […] to understand the past. It is not even enough […] to understand the present, for the here-  and-  now environment will soon vanish. [One] must learn to anticipate the directions and rate of  change.  […]  Tomorrow's  illiterate  will  not  be  the [one]  who  can't  read;  [it]  will  be  the  [one]  who  has not  learned  how  to  learn.  (Toffler  2022,  424-437; emphasis added by authors).

He thereby refers to the effects of accelerated change processes and  the  necessity  of  educational  processes  and  the  significance of didactics based less on the transmission of bodies of knowledge than on dynamic process competencies. While, for example,  Klafki  (1993)  addresses  societal  transformation  processes primarily through content and themes with his concept of epoch-  typical key problems, Toffler's approach focuses more precisely on the capacity for adaptation in a world of permanent transience and acceleration (Toffler 2022, 20), which represents a perspective that appears central to us for dealing with changes triggered by AI. The particular relevance of Toffler's approach lies in the fact that it not only describes the external dynamics of constant changes but also captures their psychological effects on individuals who must navigate a world without permanence.

Transformative learning theory (Mezirow 1995) offers valuable starting  points  here.  Education  is  understood  as  a  process  of changing relationships to self and world, triggered by perturbations/irritations (Maturana and Varela 2024, 24) that can lead to new forms of thinking and action.

The crucial question is thus how educational processes can be designed that open people to change, productively irritate them and stimulate new thinking without overwhelming or manipulating  them.  The  irritations  triggered  by  AI  technologies  at various societal levels can already be understood as educational occasions  that  call  existing  understandings  of  self  and  world into question.

Education therefore plays a key role in inclusive AI alignment structured by societal negotiation processes, not only by initiating technical AI competencies but also by enabling people to engage in critical reflection and active co-  design. This involves empowering  particularly  disadvantaged  groups  to  participate in shaping the values of AI systems. Otherwise, there is a risk that  certain  social  groups  will  be  permanently  excluded  from or  remain  excluded  from  participation  in  AI-  shaped  societies. This  requires  critical  awareness  of  existing and emerging power structures, psychological barriers and historical inequalities, as well as educational concepts that enable people not only to adapt but to actively co-  shape them. ## 5   |   Conclusio

This article makes clear that inclusion in the context of AI cannot be reduced to merely technical-  instrumental aspects. While it  is  indeed important to reduce existing participation barriers through  accessible  interfaces,  adaptive  systems,  or  assistive AI applications, what is crucial is that inclusion-in a broader understanding-requires  more  than  optimizing  technologies. Instead, it calls for engaging with the structures, power relations and cultural interpretive patterns that shape how and why AI is implemented at all.

In  this  context,  education  can  become  a  space  where  people are not only enabled to competently interact with AI, but more profoundly, are empowered to reflect critically on their self- and world-  relations in order to collectively decide how social coexistence should be shaped in the future. From the perspective of transformative learning (Mezirow 1995), such transformation of self- and world-  relations can be triggered by experiences of crisis  that,  as  stimulating moments, provoke reflection processes through  the  irritation  or  discrepancy  between  prior  expectations and new, contradictory experiences-in which previously held  interpretive  frameworks,  so-  called  'frames  of  reference' (Taylor  2017,  17),  or  habitus  (Bourdieu  1987)  are  called  into question.

AI in particular creates a variety of situations in which such a divergence between expectations and reality can be experienced. AI  may  significantly  disrupt  established  notions  of  creativity, work, intelligence, or human capabilities, leading to disorienting dilemmas (Autenrieth 2025). If these moments of irritation are  pedagogically guided, they open up potential for transformative learning processes: Subjects question and expand their existing  interpretive  repertoires  and,  in  the  process,  gain  the capacity  to  respond  in  new  ways  to  technological  and  social change processes.

However,  transformative  education  goes  beyond  acquiring knowledge about AI. It also seeks to foster reflection on one's own fundamental assumptions, attitudes and values, and supports people in converting uncertainty into productive engagement  and  in  questioning  existing  premises  (Mezirow  1995, 46)-for  example, which  understanding  of technological progress they have adopted, which implicit norms about intelligence have been internalized, or which notions of participation have so far remained unexamined. Only such phases of critical reflection and discourse lay the foundation for adequately engaging with issues of inclusion and justice in the context of AI.

For such transformation to succeed, specific, didactically structured  educational  settings  are  required.  Mezirow  (1996)  emphasizes that educational processes aimed at profound change cannot be stimulated by cognitive perception alone. Reflection spaces are needed in which learners can articulate irritations, experience a diversity of perspectives and experiment with new ways of acting in a safe environment (Autenrieth 2025):

-  (Media-  )pedagogical  spaces  of  possibility  serve  as  starting points for linking empowerment and resilience with educational practices in the context of AI systems, digital

media  technologies  and  inclusion.  They  should  provide children, adolescents and adults with suitable conditions, stimuli, and impulses that encourage independent or collaborative  reflection  about  the  connections  between  AI systems  and  inclusion.  These  spaces  enable  reflection on  potential  value  orientations  and  allow  the  development  of  individual  as  well  as  political  action  strategies (Schluchter 2020).

-  Spaces of participation (Mayrberger 2020) can be designed so  that  learners  become  co-  constructors  of  joint  learning  processes.  Instead  of  ready-  made  instructions,  they are  given  freedom  to  contribute  their  own  questions  and experiences
-  'Safe  enough  spaces'  (Singer-  Brodowski  et  al.  2022)  are spaces where learners, on the one hand, find sufficient support to allow self-  disclosure, and on the other, experience enough challenge  to  critically  examine  existing  attitudes. Through a carefully balanced combination of security and demand,  the  potential  for  transformative  processes  can fully unfold.

Inclusion  as  a  reference  point  of  transformative  education thus  opens  up  the  possibility  (and  necessity)  for  educational processes to encourage not only responses to AI technologies and  applications  (and  their  social  implementation),  but  also to  develop  shared  perspectives  on  the  development  and  use of AI. These perspectives should be grounded at the (ethicalphilosophical)  core  of  AI  systems  and  applications  (such  as addressing  AI  biases  and/or  techno-  ableism).  In  this  way, inclusion-regarding  digital  media  technologies  and  AI  systems-becomes an ongoing and indispensable moment of reflection  for  the  development  (Inclusive  AI)  and  the  societal implementation  and  use  of  AI  technologies  and  applications (AI for Inclusion).
