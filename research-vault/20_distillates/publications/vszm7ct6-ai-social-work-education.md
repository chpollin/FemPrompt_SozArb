# Distillate: Artificial intelligence in social work education

The article develops a prospective account of how artificial intelligence could affect social work education. It treats possible institutional and pedagogical changes as matters for critical professional deliberation. It does not evaluate an educational intervention.

## Core statements

- The authors use anticipated changes in higher education to argue that social work curricula must reconsider the knowledge and skills needed for digital working life. ^s1
  > "AI will also have several implications for students, including a reconsideration of what sorts of skills and knowledge should be taught and developed in the academy to prepare students for digital working lives." (Accepted Manuscript, abstract)

- The article connects the professional relevance of artificial intelligence to social work's knowledge of marginalisation and material consequences. ^s2
  > "social work can draw on knowledge of material impacts of AI on those who could become invisible or marginalised, to have a key influence in its development." (Accepted Manuscript, section on social work education)

- The authors identify critical thinking, judgement, appraisal and creativity as educational emphases in a setting where automated systems may handle more routine content and assessment functions. ^s3
  > "Social work pedagogy will include a stronger focus on critical thinking, judgment, decisionmaking, appraisal, creativity, greater integration between classroom and field pedagogies, and less about content or conceptual knowledge" (Accepted Manuscript, section on social work education)

- The concluding thought experiment leaves the acceptable extent of automation open for professional debate. ^s4
  > "Are there aspects of social work and social work education that are non-negotiable? What should be retained and fought for?" (Accepted Manuscript, conclusion)

## Research question

The article asks how anticipated uses of artificial intelligence in higher education intersect with the ethical and relational commitments of social work education. Its discussion covers what educators should teach, how institutions might organise learning and which professional qualities require protection.

## Method and study scope

The article is a prospective conceptual discussion based on literature about artificial intelligence, higher education, professional ethics and social work. It surveys possible applications in student administration, tutoring, assessment, simulation and research. A concluding thought experiment extends these developments into a deliberately speculative future educational setting.

## Main arguments

The article rejects a purely technical treatment of artificial intelligence in education. Automated student profiling, personalised tutoring and assessment promise institutional benefits, but their purposes and embedded interests require scrutiny. The authors connect this concern to the risk that data-driven systems reproduce racial, gendered and structural inequalities.

Social work contributes relational accountability, ethical reasoning and attention to marginalised groups. The article therefore presents engagement with artificial intelligence as part of professional education, while keeping human relationships, social justice and critical judgement central to curricular decisions.

The discussion of simulation and automated teaching remains conditional. Virtual environments may expand controlled practice opportunities, and automated systems may perform routine educational tasks. The paper does not establish that these uses improve learning or justify replacing field education and direct interaction.
