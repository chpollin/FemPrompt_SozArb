# Distillate: Visual disinformation and digital equity

## Core statements

A mini review uses narrative synthesis and an intersectional framework to examine how generative image systems, platform amplification, moderation and unequal access to verification create different risks across social positions.

- The synthesis identifies compounded exposure among racialised, low-income and Global South communities. ^s1
  > "communities of colour, low-income populations, and individuals in the Global South face compounded risks" (Abstract)

- The review treats its vulnerability matrix as a synthesis rather than original empirical evidence. ^s2
  > "These categories are analytical constructs that organize insights from  multiple  empirical  studies,  theoretical  frameworks,  and policy analyses rather than representing original empirical findings." (Section 4.2)

- The governance proposal gives priority to groups facing the greatest harms. ^s3
  > "platform policies, detection systems, and moderation practices be designed explicitly to protect the most vulnerable populations rather than to optimise for the majority user experience." (Section 5.1)

- Its educational proposal requires community participation and local knowledge. ^s4
  > "Effective interventions must be co-designed with target communities, incorporating local knowledge systems and leveraging existing community  networks  for  peer  education" (Section 5.2)

## Research question and method

The review asks how AI-generated and AI-amplified visual disinformation produces unequal harms through intersecting differences in race, ethnicity, socioeconomic position, geography and digital literacy, and which interventions could address these inequities. It identifies itself as a mini review using narrative synthesis. Systematic searches of Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, arXiv and SSRN were conducted between October and December 2025. The search combined terms for generative AI, deepfakes, visual disinformation, algorithmic bias, marginalised communities, digital equity and intersectionality.

The review prioritised peer-reviewed studies, institutional reports and policy documents published from 2023 through 2025, while retaining foundational theory. Inclusion required accessible English-language work on AI-generated visual content, differential social impacts, platform governance, algorithmic systems or digital equity. Approximately 50 sources were retained across communication studies, critical algorithm studies, computer science, sociology and policy research. Thematic analysis organised the synthesis into four domains, AI production mechanisms, intersectional vulnerability frameworks, platform algorithmic governance and equity-centred interventions. Intersectional theory and social identity theory supply the conceptual frame.

## Main findings

In the production domain, the review links synthetic-media harms to biased training data and to generative systems that reproduce racialised and gendered stereotypes. Platform recommender systems then amplify emotionally engaging material and target it through demographic profiles. The claimed harm therefore arises through the relation between content generation and distribution rather than through image generation alone.

The vulnerability synthesis distinguishes several mechanisms. Communities of colour face stereotyped synthetic depictions and poorer performance from verification systems trained on unrepresentative data. Lower-income groups have less access to high-resolution devices, paid verification tools and media-literacy provision, while economic precarity also restricts time for verification. Global South and non-English-speaking communities face infrastructure and bandwidth limits, fewer authoritative debunking resources and safeguards designed around Western users and languages. These mechanisms can overlap within the same person or community.

In the platform-governance domain, engagement optimisation can prioritise dominant narratives and create what the review calls algorithmic redlining in information quality. Automated moderation can both overmoderate legitimate minority speech and remove disinformation targeting marginalised communities more slowly. The paper presents the vulnerability matrix as a conceptual organisation of patterns across prior work, not as a dataset or a new measurement of prevalence.

The intervention synthesis proposes demographic algorithmic-impact assessment, participation by affected communities in system oversight and equitable allocation of moderation resources. It pairs these governance measures with culturally responsive media literacy that explains synthetic-media generation, amplification and demographic targeting while using local knowledge and community networks. The stated research gaps concern intersectional and Global South evidence, longitudinal change, comparative evaluation of interventions, grassroots resistance and the political economy of AI development.

## Limitations

The article reports a narrative synthesis rather than a new population study, platform audit or meta-analysis. Its corpus is approximate, restricted to accessible English-language sources and weighted toward recent work from 2023 through 2025. The source does not report duplicate screening, a formal quality appraisal, study-level extraction or a reproducible flow diagram. Its cross-disciplinary evidence includes empirical studies, theory, institutional reports and policy documents, so the synthesis does not support pooled prevalence or causal estimates. Several claims about platform exposure and enforcement are presented at review level and depend on the quality and comparability of the cited literature. The vulnerability matrix is conceptual rather than empirical. Linearised tables were available in the bound representation, while figures and visual layout were not independently inspected during preparation.
