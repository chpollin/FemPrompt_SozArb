# Distillate: Algorithmic decision-making in social work practice and pedagogy: confronting the competency/critique dilemma

The article argues that social-work education must combine technical competence with critical analysis of algorithmic decision-making. Case studies illustrate why algorithmic tools should support interpretation rather than replace professional judgement and relational practice.

## Core statements

- Social-work education faces a dual task of teaching technical competence and the capacity to critique the social framing and effects of algorithmic systems. ^s1
  > "Here we use the concept of 'digital literacy' in its fullest sense to include technical competency and critical understanding of the technical implications and social  form  of  such  digital  tools  as  they  contribute  to  making  and remaking our world." (10.1080/02615479.2023.2195425, Introduction)
- Algorithmic decision-making can reproduce structural inequality and can also expose professional bias when used transparently and reflexively. ^s2
  > "while  there  is  no  assurance  that  ADM  will  remove  practices  of  racism,  disablism, heterosexism, and misogyny in the allocation of resources or assessment of risk (in fact it  can  introduce  new  structural  biases),  used  reflexively  it  can  draw  attention  to  such biases." (10.1080/02615479.2023.2195425, Algorithmic decision-making in practice?)
- The proposed pedagogy uses cases to examine assumptions, intersectional context, intended and unintended consequences, and forms of relational engagement. ^s3
- Professional education should prepare social workers to influence, constrain or refuse algorithmic processes when those processes harm people receiving support. ^s4
  > "digital literacy and awareness of opportunities and risks associated with ADM." (10.1080/02615479.2023.2195425, Conclusion)

## Research question and method

The article asks how social-work education can prepare practitioners for algorithmic decision-making while retaining critical interpretation, relational engagement and attention to unequal power. It develops two paired tensions, presence and mediation as well as technique and critique. The method is a conceptual analysis using case studies of the Allegheny Family Screening Tool, COMPAS, the Australian National Disability Insurance Scheme, HEALER and Chicago's Heat List. The cases illustrate practice and pedagogy. They are not a systematic comparison or a new empirical evaluation.

## Main findings

Algorithmic decision systems can regularise decisions and reveal patterns that practitioners may miss, but they can also turn historical disparities into opaque risk classifications, narrow professional discretion and recenter decisions away from clients and practitioners. Their consequences depend on data, institutional purpose, implementation and the continuing role of human interpretation.

The authors propose a technical-critical pedagogy. Students should understand how systems work and examine their assumptions, affected intersectional groups, intended outcomes, human-rights implications, unintended consequences and relationship to face-to-face practice. Digital literacy therefore includes the capacity to use tools, criticise their framing and challenge their deployment.

## Limitations

The article is a conceptual and pedagogical argument based on selected cases. It does not systematically identify deployments, measure their prevalence or compare their effects through a common empirical design. Some cases cross health, policing, corrections and welfare rather than social-work practice alone. The authors also caution against treating algorithmic use as universal.
