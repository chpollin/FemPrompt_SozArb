# Distillate: Evaluating gender bias in large language models in long-term care

The study tests whether changing gender references in otherwise equivalent long-term-care case notes changes summaries generated by four language models. It combines sentiment, thematic inclusion and word-frequency analyses to make gender-related output differences interpretable for care practice.

## Core statements

- Counterfactual pairs allow the study to attribute differences in summaries to the represented gender while holding the underlying care record constant. ^s1
- Models differed materially, with one recent model showing no detected gender differences and another producing the clearest differences in tone, topic inclusion and language. ^s2
  > "Llama 3 showed no gender-based differences across any metrics. Gemma displayed the most significant gender-based differences." (10.1186/s12911-025-03118-0, abstract)
- Underemphasis of women's health needs could affect professional impressions and service allocation in needs-based care. ^s3
  > "If women's health issues are underemphasised, this may lead to gender-based disparities in service receipt." (10.1186/s12911-025-03118-0, abstract)
- The proposed evaluation is designed to expose both the presence and the form of bias rather than reduce the comparison to one scalar score. ^s4
  > "The  framework  for  assessing  counterfactual fairness in LLM outputs is designed to be reproducible, interpretable,  and  applicable  across  domains,  with  all code available on GitHub." (10.1186/s12911-025-03118-0, Generalisibility)

## Research question and method

The study asks whether current open-source language models produce gender-based differences in summaries of long-term-care notes, whether differences concern topic inclusion or linguistic framing, and what they could mean for care practice. It uses pseudonymised adult-social-care records from one English local authority. After excluding unsuitable or short texts, the author creates male and female counterfactual versions and generates summaries with Llama 3, Gemma, T5 and BART under several output lengths. Bias is assessed through validated sentiment measures, thematic term counts and document-level word-frequency models, with robustness checks and correction for multiple comparisons.

## Main findings

The models did not behave uniformly. The paper reports no detected gender differences for Llama 3, some variation for the benchmark models and the strongest disparities for Gemma. In Gemma summaries, men's physical and mental health needs were more directly and frequently emphasised, while women's needs were more often expressed indirectly or omitted.

Because long-term-care allocation depends on assessed need, these differences may influence urgency judgements or the amount of support offered. The study does not demonstrate an observed allocation effect. It provides a reproducible framework for testing whether generated records differ across counterfactual gender versions and for examining how those differences appear in language.

## Assessment relevance

`Generative_KI`, `Gender`, `Bias_Ungleichheit` and `Fairness` are central because the study evaluates gender bias in generated care summaries through counterfactual fairness. `Soziale_Arbeit` is relevant through adult social-care records, care assessment and service-allocation implications. The study compares men and women but does not address broader gender identities, intersectional diversity, prompting as an intervention, AI literacy or a feminist method.

## Limitations

The records come from one local authority and one service domain. Inputs are shorter than full case histories, and cases with gender-specific circumstances were removed to support counterfactual comparison. Model outputs are stochastic, many word-level tests were conducted and available sentiment models were not trained specifically on care documentation. The methods may miss subtler framing or discourse differences, and the study does not observe downstream allocation decisions.
