# Distillate: Worker discretion and racial disparities in child-welfare screening

## Core statements

- In one county child-welfare intake system, the study compares a hypothetical automated policy based on the Allegheny Family Screening Tool with actual worker decisions assisted by the tool. ^s1
- For the studied post-deployment period, the hypothetical algorithm-only policy produced a 20 percent Black-white screen-in-rate disparity, while actual worker-assisted decisions produced 9 percent. ^s2
- Interviews and contextual inquiry suggest that workers used contextual information, compensated for perceived model limitations and sometimes collaborated across roles. ^s3
- The quantitative comparison does not establish that introducing the tool caused the observed change from the earlier period. ^s4

> "workers reduced the disparity in screen-in rate between Black and white children from 20% to 9%." (bound Version, source text)

> "Without first understanding workers' objectives when making predictions and decisions, such accuracy comparisons may essentially be evaluating workers' performance on a game that they are not playing." (bound Version, source text)

## Method and scope

The mixed-methods study analyzes discretionary referrals before and after deployment of version 1 of the tool. After preprocessing, the quantitative data contain 31,025 child-referral entries before deployment and 51,750 after deployment. The analysis compares actual worker-assisted decisions with a counterfactual algorithm-only rule that screens in scores from 15 to 20. The qualitative component combines contextual inquiry and semi-structured interviews with nine call-screen caseworkers and four supervisors in 2021, followed by thematic analysis with open coding and affinity mapping. The study evaluates screen-in-rate disparity and several proxy-based accuracy measures, and includes sensitivity analyses across thresholds.

## Assessment relevance

The study directly supports `KI_Sonstige`, `Soziale_Arbeit`, `Bias_Ungleichheit` and `Fairness`. It concerns algorithm-assisted child-welfare practice and examines racial disparity, proxy outcomes, discretion and organizational collaboration. It does not study generative AI, prompting, gender or feminist methods.

## Limitations

The quantitative observations cover 2016 to 2018, whereas the qualitative fieldwork occurred in 2021 after policy and model changes. Corrected retrospective scores sometimes differ from the scores workers actually saw. The algorithm-only decisions are counterfactual and depend on a chosen threshold. Re-referral and placement are imperfect proxies for the immediate safety judgments workers described. The study does not distinguish warranted from unwarranted disparity and cannot attribute the pre-to-post change to the tool because there was no randomization and other policies changed. Its findings concern one county, one workflow and one tool version. Figures were not independently inspected during preparation.
