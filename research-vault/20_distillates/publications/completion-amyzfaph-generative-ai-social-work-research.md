# Distillate: Recommendations for social work researchers and journal editors on the use of generative AI and large language models

The article develops preliminary guidance for responsible use of generative AI and large language models across social-work research. It treats these systems as potentially useful for writing and analysis and as sources of privacy, bias, attribution, reproducibility and quality problems.

## Core statements

- Researchers remain accountable for text and analysis produced with generative AI and must inspect and verify those outputs. ^s1
  > "Researchers must use great caution when deploying generative AI technologies, meticulously examining, verifying, and taking accountability for the text and analyses produced by these instruments." (10.1086/726021, abstract)
- Responsible research use requires knowledge of a tool's construction, training data, limits and potential biases. ^s2
  > "This knowledge is essential for recognizing AI tools ' limitations and potential biases and making informed decisions about which tools are best suited for a particular research project." (10.1086/726021, Construction and Limitations of Artificial Intelligence Tools)
- Reproducible reporting should identify the tasks, prompts, tool names and versions involved in AI-assisted analysis. ^s3
  > "If researchers employ LLMs for tasks such as information extraction, classi /uniFB01 cation, or clustering, the prompts used should be detailed, along with the names and version numbers of the tools." (10.1086/726021, Large Language Models in Analysis and Interpretation)
- Editorial governance must cover research quality, ethics, transparency and accountability rather than authorship disclosure alone. ^s4
  > "Likewise, journal editors will need to implement quality control procedures and ethical standards to guide and evaluate the use of these technologies in social work research." (10.1086/726021, abstract)

## Research question and method

The article asks how generative AI and large language models could affect social-work research and which responsibilities should govern their use by researchers, editors and reviewers. It is a conceptual and normative contribution. The authors organise opportunities and problems through a disruptive and disrupting framework, review early policies from scientific journals and publishers, and derive recommendations for data collection, analysis, interpretation and dissemination. The article does not report a systematic review or a new empirical evaluation of an AI system.

## Main findings

Large language models may assist literature searching, writing, qualitative coding, statistical work, programming and dissemination. The same uses can expose confidential data, obscure ownership and attribution, reproduce bias, generate false references, weaken analysis through automation and complicate reproducibility when models or outputs change.

The proposed responsibilities require researchers to understand the systems they use, obtain informed consent where AI processes human-subject data, protect data, document prompts and model versions, verify outputs and assume responsibility for results. Editors and reviewers should require adequate methods reporting, check scientific quality and assess privacy, consent and bias. The authors present these recommendations as a starting point that needs revision as technologies and disciplinary practice change.
