# Distillate: Critical digital literacy for social work education in a time of artificial intelligence

The article develops a conceptual framework for critical digital literacy in social work education. It joins ethical and algorithmic accountability, pedagogical change, and decolonial and social-justice perspectives, then illustrates how generated images can become material for classroom critique.

## Core statements

- Digital literacy is framed as critical engagement with the social and political effects of technology rather than technical proficiency alone. ^s1
  > "digital literacy becomes essential-not simply as technical competence, but as a deeper capacity to understand, question, and navigate the technologies shaping social work." (10.1080/02615479.2026.2660786)

- The proposed framework treats AI as historically and politically situated. ^s2
  > "Understanding AI not merely as a tool but as a socio-technical formation-rooted in" (10.1080/02615479.2026.2660786)

- The image exercise is explicitly illustrative rather than an exhaustive bias analysis. ^s3
  > "The Table 2 below shows commands given in the AI systems and Table 3 shows the images generated on the date the commands where given." (10.1080/02615479.2026.2660786)

- The teaching questions direct attention to presence, absence, marginalization, misrepresentation, and stereotypes. ^s4
  > "Who or what is missing, marginalized, or misrepresented?" (10.1080/02615479.2026.2660786)

## Research question and method

The article asks how digital literacy in social work education can engage critically with artificial intelligence while remaining grounded in decolonial, ethical, and pedagogical principles. It uses an integrative literature review covering peer-reviewed articles, conceptual essays, and policy reports published from 2020 through 2025. Searches in Web of Science and Google Scholar yielded 60 sources. Title and abstract screening, inclusion criteria, and close reading reduced these to 22 sources. Deductive and inductive thematic coding produced three themes, ethical concerns and algorithmic bias, implications for social work pedagogy and practice, and decolonial perspectives.

## Main findings

The synthesis argues that social work education should place questions of power, context, harm, professional judgment, and community accountability inside digital literacy. Pedagogical uses of AI should retain relational learning and critical reflection. A decolonial approach should examine whose knowledge enters training data and system outputs, the colonial histories of classification and surveillance, and the exclusion or distortion of marginalized knowledge.

The illustrative case presents five image prompts executed with Open Art Ai, Deep AI Image Generator, and ChatGPT 4. Direct inspection of the publisher PDF confirms the prompt-to-image order in Tables 2 and 3. The two boss-related prompts yielded a white-presenting woman and an older white-presenting man. The overworked prompt yielded a stressed white-presenting woman at a desk. The two prompts explicitly referring to Black women or community work yielded Black women in classroom-like and community settings. The article uses this small set to formulate teaching questions about recurring visual cues, whiteness, racialized placement, omission, and stereotypes. These observations describe the displayed examples and do not establish prevalence or comparative model performance.

## Limitations

The contribution is a conceptual integrative review rather than an evaluation of learning outcomes or professional practice. Its English-language search uses two databases and a restricted publication period. The image case contains five one-time outputs from three systems and supplies no repeated generations, sampling protocol, coding procedure, or quantitative comparison. It supports classroom interrogation of representations, not general claims about the frequency of bias in the named systems.
